## In brief

The Court declined to hear a homeowner's bid to revive her lawsuit seeking compensation after police heavily damaged her house while searching for a fugitive who was never there.

## What happened

In 2022, police searching for a fugitive mistakenly believed he was inside Amy Hadley's South Bend, Indiana home. Officers fired tear gas canisters into the house, deployed flash-bang grenades, and searched extensively, causing about $16,000 in damage. The fugitive was never there and was arrested elsewhere days later. Hadley sued the city and county, arguing the Constitution's requirement of payment when government takes property for public use entitled her to compensation. The district court dismissed her claim, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed, holding that circuit precedent treats property damage from a lawful search as an exercise of police power rather than a compensable taking.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the rule requiring government payment when it takes property applies when police damage a home while executing a valid search warrant, or whether such damage falls outside that payment requirement because it results from ordinary law-enforcement activity rather than the government formally acquiring property. Hadley also asked whether the government can avoid paying when officers intentionally destroy an innocent person's property while trying to catch a suspect.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference more than once, most recently for the October 16, 2026 conference, and the Court denied the petition on October 19, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Hadley, the homeowner, asked the Court to take up her case after losing in the lower courts. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Seventh Circuit's ruling in place: Hadley's lawsuit against the City of South Bend and St. Joseph County remains dismissed, and she recovers nothing for the property damage under this claim. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision as the final word in her case while the same legal question remains open in other courts.
