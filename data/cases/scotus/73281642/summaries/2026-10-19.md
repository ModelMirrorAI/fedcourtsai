## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a homeowner's bid to force compensation for severe property damage police caused while searching her house for a fugitive who was never there.

## What happened

Police in South Bend and St. Joseph County, Indiana, searched Amy Hadley's home for a fugitive based on a mistaken IP-address lead. No one connected to the fugitive was there. Officers fired dozens of tear-gas canisters, broke windows, and tore apart the interior, causing about $16,000 in damage. Hadley sued under the Takings Clause, which requires compensation when government takes property for public use. The district court dismissed her claim, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed, holding that circuit precedent treats property damage from a lawful police search as an exercise of "police power" rather than eminent domain, so no compensation is owed. The Seventh Circuit denied rehearing en banc.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether the rule that compensation is never owed for property damage caused by police exercising law-enforcement power, rather than the power to formally condemn property, is a valid reading of the Constitution's compensation requirement, and separately whether the government can avoid paying for intentionally destroying an innocent person's property while pursuing a fugitive.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference multiple times between September and October 2026. On October 19, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Hadley, who lost in the lower courts, asked the Court to take up her case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Seventh Circuit's ruling in place: Hadley and others in that circuit cannot recover compensation under the Takings Clause for property damage reasonably caused by police executing a valid search warrant. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision standing without deciding the underlying question itself.
