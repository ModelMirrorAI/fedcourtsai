## In brief

A homeowner whose house was badly damaged during a mistaken police search for a fugitive asks the Court to hold that the government owes her compensation under the Constitution.

## What happened

In 2022, South Bend and St. Joseph County police surrounded Amy Hadley's Indiana home, wrongly believing a murder suspect was inside based on faulty IP-address tracking. Officers fired tear gas canisters, deployed flash-bang grenades, and searched the house, causing roughly $16,000 in damage. The suspect was never there. Hadley did not challenge the search warrant itself, but sued the city and county, arguing the Constitution's Takings Clause required compensation for the property destruction. The district court dismissed her claim, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed, holding that circuit precedent bars compensation when damage results from police using their general law-enforcement powers rather than formally condemning property.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the rule requiring the government to pay for private property it takes for public use applies when police damage an innocent person's home while executing a valid search warrant, or whether that rule has no role once the damage results from police exercising general law-enforcement authority rather than formally condemning the property. Hadley also asks whether, even if there is no blanket carve-out for law-enforcement actions, the government can still avoid paying when officers intentionally destroy an innocent bystander's property while pursuing a fugitive.

## Where it stands

The petition and the brief in opposition have been filed, along with Hadley's reply and several amicus briefs supporting her position. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and again for its conference on October 9, 2026; the docket does not yet show any action taken.

## What each outcome would mean

Hadley, the homeowner, is asking the Court to take up her case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide whether compensation is owed in these circumstances, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Seventh Circuit's decision stands, leaving Hadley uncompensated and leaving in place that circuit's rule that such police-caused property damage does not trigger a right to compensation.
