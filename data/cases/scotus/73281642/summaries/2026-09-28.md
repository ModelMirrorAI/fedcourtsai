## In brief

A homeowner asks the Supreme Court to revive her lawsuit seeking payment for damage police caused searching her house for a fugitive who was never there.

## What happened

Police in South Bend, Indiana, searching for a fugitive mistakenly believed was using Amy Hadley's home to access the internet, surrounded her house, fired tear gas into it, and searched it extensively, causing about $16,000 in damage. The fugitive was not there and had no connection to Hadley. Neither the city nor the county compensated her. Hadley sued under the Constitution's requirement that government pay for private property taken for public use, but the district court dismissed her claims, and the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, applying its rule that this payment requirement does not apply when property is damaged through law enforcement action rather than through the government's power to formally condemn property.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the payment requirement for government-taken property has an exception for damage caused by police carrying out their law-enforcement duties, as opposed to formal condemnation proceedings, and, relatedly, whether government must pay when officers intentionally damage an innocent person's property while trying to catch a fugitive who turns out not to be there.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, with amicus briefs filed on both sides, and was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026. The docket does not yet show any action taken at or after that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Hadley, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up her case. If the Court grants review, it will hear the case in full and decide whether the payment requirement applies to police-caused property damage; a grant does not itself decide who is right. If the Court denies review, the Seventh Circuit's ruling against Hadley stands, and her claim for compensation remains barred in that circuit. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with the lower court's reasoning, only that the case will not be heard.
