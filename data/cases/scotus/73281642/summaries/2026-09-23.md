## What happened

Police in South Bend, Indiana, searching for a fugitive believed (mistakenly) to be inside, surrounded Amy Hadley's house, fired tear gas into it, and searched and damaged the home extensively. The fugitive was not there and had no connection to Hadley. The city and county refused to pay for the damage. Hadley sued under the Fifth Amendment's takings clause, which requires compensation when the government takes private property for public use. The district court dismissed her claims, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed, following its own prior ruling that damage caused by police exercising law-enforcement power, rather than the power of eminent domain, does not require compensation. Hadley argues this creates a split among appeals courts; the city and county respond that courts agree, by various routes, that such damage is not compensable.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether property owners must be paid when police damage their home while carrying out ordinary law-enforcement duties, like executing a search warrant, rather than when the government is deliberately acquiring property for public use. Hadley argues no such carve-out exists and that reasonableness under separate search-and-seizure rules should not decide the compensation question. The city and county answer that longstanding legal tradition has never required payment for reasonable damage done during a lawful search, regardless of which legal label courts use to explain that result.

## Where it stands

The petition, opposition brief, reply, and several friend-of-the-court briefs have all been filed. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, meaning the Justices will consider at that conference whether to take up the case.
