## In brief

A homeowner whose house was badly damaged during a mistaken police raid asks the Court to decide whether she can seek compensation under the Constitution's property-taking protections.

## What happened

Police in South Bend, Indiana, searching for a fugitive they wrongly believed was hiding in Amy Hadley's home, surrounded the house, fired dozens of tear gas canisters inside, and extensively damaged the property while executing a valid search warrant. The fugitive was never there and was arrested elsewhere days later. Hadley says the damage exceeded $16,000, only partly covered by insurance. When the city and county refused to pay, she sued under the Fifth Amendment's Takings Clause, which requires compensation when government takes private property for public use. The district court dismissed her claim based on circuit precedent, and the Seventh Circuit affirmed, noting several other circuits have rejected that precedent's reasoning.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the requirement that government pay for property it takes applies when police damage an innocent person's home while reasonably executing a valid search warrant, or whether such damage falls outside that requirement because it results from the government's general law-enforcement authority rather than its power to formally condemn property. Relatedly, the Court is asked whether the government can avoid paying for property it intentionally destroys while trying to catch a fugitive who, it turns out, had no connection to the property owner.

## Where it stands

The petition has been filed, briefed, and distributed for conference three times, most recently for the October 16, 2026 conference; the docket does not yet show any action taken on it.

## What each outcome would mean

Hadley, the homeowner, asked the Court to take up her case after losing in the lower courts. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear the case and decide whether damage from a lawful police search can require compensation, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies the petition, the Seventh Circuit's ruling against Hadley stands, leaving in place its rule that such police-power damage does not require compensation in the states that circuit covers, without deciding the broader question for other courts.
