## Why 30%, substantially above the anchor

**A serious categorical-rule disagreement.** The petition, printed pp. 8–18, places the Seventh Circuit's police-power exception against circuits that reject that categorical formulation. The opposition itself acknowledges that Baker and Slaybaugh rejected the police-power/eminent-domain distinction, although it contests the practical significance. Thus the disagreement is not supported solely by the petition's characterization. Resolving the scope of a constitutional compensation rule can matter nationally even where damages in this case are modest.

**A potentially useful pleading-stage vehicle.** As described in both briefs, police executed a warrant at the wrong home and deliberately damaged it; the fugitive was not there. The petition, pp. 17–18 and 28–30, argues that the lower courts relied on the blanket exception and did not fully consider alternatives. It distinguishes a search for a person thought to be using social media from an active hostage emergency. That supplies a plausible reason this case could receive a different analysis under a necessity rule and a narrow route to vacatur without recognizing an unlimited compensation entitlement. I do not assume that a mistaken address by itself defeats an objectively reasonable necessity defense.

**Interest extending beyond the litigants.** Five amicus filing entries support treating the subject as recurrent and significant. The petition also identifies Justice Sotomayor's statement, joined by Justice Gorsuch, respecting denial in Baker, 145 S. Ct. 11 (2024), at printed pp. 8 and 18. I rely on that reference only as reported in the provisioned petition: independent verification failed. Interest from Justices with different jurisprudential perspectives increases the plausibility of review, but a prior statement respecting denial is neither a dissent nor proof of four votes here.

These considerations justify a large, explicitly judgmental upward adjustment from 5.12% to **30%**. The adjustment is not a fitted statistical estimate; the amicus support, circuit discussion, and earlier judicial attention are correlated signals, not independent likelihood ratios.

## Why denial remains more likely

The opposition's strongest point is **convergence in outcomes despite divergent explanations**. Its printed pp. 12–15 argue that necessity and historical search-and-arrest privileges still foreclose compensation for reasonable warrant-related damage. Its factual account and vehicle discussion, pp. 1–3 and 34–37, emphasize that Hadley does not challenge the warrant's validity or frame the case as an unreasonable search. The petition's answer—that there was no actual fugitive or immediate danger—is substantial but does not establish how an objective privilege applies to a reasonable mistake.

The opposition further identifies tension between a per se takings rule and the petitioner's qualifications for innocence, lack of connection to the suspect, severity of damage, and incidental losses. That creates scope and administrability questions. Those are merits arguments rather than an independently established jurisdictional bar, but they may make the Court prefer a clearer outcome conflict or a narrower question. The record also lacks a relist or CVSG indicating attention from this Court to this petition. Five amici and the earlier Baker statement do not overcome that uncertainty enough to make grant the modal result.

The pivotal uncertainty is therefore whether the Court sees **a clean categorical-rule conflict needing immediate correction**, or **different routes to the same result on a fact-sensitive warrant search**. The present record supports both readings. I forecast denial, not a determination that the Seventh Circuit is correct.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The **44% further-distribution** forecast reflects a serious issue that may receive additional consideration, while preserving zero further distributions as most likely. **3% CVSG** recognizes a possible federal enforcement interest but little case-specific need for a federal recommendation. **12% summary route conditional on grant** reflects the available narrow categorical-rule correction, discounted because the historical exceptions and factual fit warrant plenary briefing. **24% separate writing conditional on denial** reflects the earlier reported Baker attention without assuming it repeats. The latter two figures are conditional probabilities, not unconditional shares of all outcomes. None of these procedural numbers is represented as an empirically estimated forward hazard.

The **0.65 big-case score** concerns significance if decided: compensation for police-inflicted damage implicates nationwide homeowner protection and public budgets. It is not a second grant probability. The individual damages are limited, and a narrow remand could leave much unresolved, so I place the stakes below a sweeping institutional or nationwide-program case.
