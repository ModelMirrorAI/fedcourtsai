## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review a ruling that a private corporation's power to enforce horseracing safety rules is unconstitutional.

## What happened

Congress created the Horseracing Integrity and Safety Authority, a private corporation, to write and enforce nationwide safety and anti-doping rules for thoroughbred racing, under Federal Trade Commission oversight. Horsemen's associations, racetracks, and Texas sued, arguing the arrangement unconstitutionally hands government power to a private entity. The Fifth Circuit upheld the Authority's rulemaking power but ruled that its enforcement powers—investigating, subpoenaing, searching, sanctioning, and suing covered persons—are unconstitutional because the FTC does not adequately supervise them. The Sixth Circuit, considering a similar challenge, had upheld the same enforcement provisions.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether letting a private corporation investigate, charge, and penalize people in the horseracing industry, with only limited and after-the-fact review by a federal agency, crosses a constitutional line against handing government enforcement power to private parties.

## Where it stands

The federal government and the Authority each petitioned for review; several respondents filed responses, some agreeing that the Court should hear the case while disputing how the question should be framed, and the government filed a reply. The docket shows no order yet granting or denying the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The Federal Trade Commission, joined by the Authority, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Fifth Circuit. If the Court grants review and later reverses, the Authority's enforcement powers would be upheld, letting the current enforcement structure continue. If the Court grants review and affirms, the Fifth Circuit's finding that the enforcement provisions are unconstitutional would stand, requiring Congress or the agency to change how enforcement works. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision striking down the enforcement provisions remains in effect, at least in that circuit, while the conflicting Sixth Circuit ruling stands elsewhere.
