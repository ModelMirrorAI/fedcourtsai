## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear Lane Mitchell's bid to block a retrial he says would violate his constitutional protection against being tried twice for the same crime.

## What happened

Lane Mitchell was tried in Mississippi for attempted murder after stabbing a man during a confrontation at a restaurant. After both sides presented their evidence but before the jury decided, the trial judge found that Mitchell's rights had been violated because the alleged victim never testified, and entered a judgment of acquittal. Mississippi appealed, and the Mississippi Court of Appeals, divided, reversed and ordered a new trial, treating the ruling as a procedural dismissal rather than a true acquittal. The Mississippi Supreme Court initially agreed to review the case but later dismissed its own review without deciding anything. Mitchell then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether holding a second trial would violate the constitutional rule against being tried twice for the same offense, where a trial judge, after all the evidence was in, stopped the case and entered an acquittal based on constitutional problems with how the trial had gone, without specifically declaring the prosecution's evidence legally insufficient.

## Where it stands

The State of Mississippi waived its right to respond to the petition. The petition was distributed for the Court's November 13, 2026 conference, and on November 16, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Mitchell, who lost before the Mississippi Court of Appeals, asked the Court to take up his case. With the petition now denied, the Mississippi Court of Appeals' decision stands, meaning the order allowing a second trial against Mitchell on the attempted murder charge remains in place and the case can proceed in Mississippi courts. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision undisturbed without the Supreme Court deciding the double jeopardy question itself.
