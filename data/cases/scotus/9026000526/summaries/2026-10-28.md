## In brief

Lane Mitchell asks the Court to decide whether a second trial would violate double jeopardy after his first trial ended in a judgment of acquittal.

## What happened

Mitchell was tried in Mississippi for attempted murder after stabbing a man who charged at his father in a restaurant. After both sides rested, the trial court found that Mitchell's constitutional rights to compulsory process had been violated because the alleged victim never testified, and it entered a judgment of acquittal rather than sending the case to the jury. The State appealed, and a divided Mississippi Court of Appeals reversed, treating the ruling as a procedural dismissal rather than a true acquittal, and ordered a new trial. The Mississippi Supreme Court initially agreed to review the case but later dismissed its own review without deciding the issue, leaving the Court of Appeals' decision in place.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the rule against being tried twice for the same crime blocks a second trial in this situation: the trial judge let both sides finish presenting evidence, then found a constitutional violation based on the trial record and entered an acquittal, but without separately declaring that the prosecution's evidence was too weak to convict. Mitchell argues that what the judge actually did, not the label used, should control, while the lower court treated the ruling as something other than a true acquittal.

## Where it stands

Mitchell's petition for the Court to hear the case was filed October 13, 2026. Mississippi waived its right to file a response on October 21, 2026, and the case is awaiting the Court's decision on whether to grant review.

## What each outcome would mean

Mitchell is asking the Court to hear the case and reverse the decision allowing a retrial. If the Court denies review, the Mississippi Court of Appeals' ruling stands, and Mitchell would face a second trial on the attempted murder charge. If the Court grants review, it would decide the double jeopardy question itself after full briefing and argument; affirming would let the retrial proceed, while reversing would bar a second trial. The Court could also vacate the decision and send the case back for further proceedings without resolving the question either way.
