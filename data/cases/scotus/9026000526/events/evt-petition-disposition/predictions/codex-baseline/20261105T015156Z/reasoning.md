## Vehicle and doctrine

The question presented asks whether a second trial is permissible after both sides rested and the judge entered an acquittal based on constitutional violations without separately finding insufficient evidence. The best grant considerations are the irreparable burden of a second prosecution, an express acquittal judgment, and the Mississippi Court of Appeals' five-to-four division among participating judges. The Mississippi Supreme Court initially granted review and then dismissed its writ. Those circumstances make the petition substantial enough to examine, but do not establish a national conflict on the same facts.

The appendix weakens the suggested conflict with acquittal-finality precedents. Appendix A, paragraphs 33–34, records denials of earlier directed-verdict motions and no ruling on the final renewed sufficiency motion. Appendix E, pages 63a–64a, dismisses for interference with compulsory process and then directs entry of an acquittal. The majority distinguished a procedural constitutional remedy from an adjudication of criminal liability; it did not simply demand particular words from an otherwise unmistakable insufficiency ruling. Much of the dissent also concerns the State's statutory appellate authority and compulsory-process treatment, not a new federal rule about genuine acquittals.

Most importantly, **United States v. Scott, 437 U.S. 82 (1978), itself involved dismissal at the close of all evidence**, on the defendant's motion alleging unconstitutional preindictment delay. I checked the majority opinion through CourtListener, opinion 9842021, its opening paragraph and discussion at 98–99. Thus the petition's repeated distinction between a completed evidentiary presentation and an earlier procedural dismissal is not enough by itself. Applying facts to a constitutional claim does not necessarily decide the sufficiency of proof of the charged offense. That leaves a record-specific application of the acquittal/procedural-dismissal boundary, rather than an obvious departure requiring Supreme Court intervention. Evans and McElrath, as discussed in the petition, protect actual substantive acquittals; whether this ruling was one remains the premise in dispute.

The petition's state-court comparisons span limitations defenses, element-related rulings, and other different terminations. I do not credit them as an independently verified square split. The appendix's substantive account controls over its inconsistent date headings. The opinion's state-law discussion also complicates a clean federal presentation, although I do not assert an established jurisdictional bar: the order authorizes a retrial, which itself is the federal injury claimed.
