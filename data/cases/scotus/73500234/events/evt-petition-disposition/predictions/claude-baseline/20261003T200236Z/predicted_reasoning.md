# Forecast: Wilkins v. United States, No. 25-1332 (evt-petition-disposition)

**Disposition.** The Court will deny the petition. P(any grant) = 0.08.

**Relists.** The docket shows **one distribution**, for the Conference of
October 9, 2026, and no relist. I expect the petition most likely to be decided
at that first conference, but there is a real chance (about 30%) of at least
one further distribution: this is a return trip after *Wilkins v. United
States*, 598 U.S. 152 (2023), the petition asks for summary reversal on a
"lower court ignored our mandate" theory, and that is the shape of case a
Justice sometimes holds over for a possible summary disposition or a statement
respecting denial. If relisted, expect one or two further distributions, not a
long string.

**CVSG.** None. The United States is the respondent and the Solicitor General
has already filed the brief in opposition, so there is no one to invite.

**Which question, if taken.** If the Court acts at all it would act on
Question 1 (whether law of the case can apply after this Court reverses and
remands), not Question 2. Question 2 (whether a Quiet Title Act claim can
accrue from private citizens' unauthorized use rather than a government act) is
factbound on this record and the asserted conflict with *Werner* (11th Cir.) is
distinguishable on the easement-over-federal-land versus easement-over-private-
land axis the Ninth Circuit drew.

**Summary disposition versus plenary review.** Conditional on a grant, a
summary route (per curiam reversal or a vacatur and remand directing the lower
courts to decide timeliness afresh without reference to the pre-*Wilkins I*
rulings) is the likelier path, about 55%, because the only question with any
pull is a mandate-compliance question of the kind the Court handles by per
curiam when it handles it at all. Plenary review of either question is
unlikely; there is no intervening decision that would support a GVR.

**Dissent or statement if denied.** Most likely a bare denial. About a 12%
chance that a Justice writes separately: the natural candidates are Justice
Gorsuch (the Court's most frequent separate writer on property-owner access to
court and on lower-court fidelity to this Court's remands) or Justice Thomas.
Any such writing would stress that the Ninth Circuit should not have treated
findings made under a jurisdictional-dismissal standard as settled after the
Court held the limitations period nonjurisdictional.

**If granted (conditional only).** The likely ground would be that a judgment
reversed by this Court carries no law-of-the-case force on remand, so the
district court had to decide timeliness under the summary-judgment standard
with the burden on the government; the Court would leave Question 2's accrual
rule for the lower courts.
