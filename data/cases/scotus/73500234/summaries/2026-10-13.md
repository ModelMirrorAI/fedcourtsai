## In brief

The Court declined to hear a Montana landowner's bid to revive his long-running suit disputing the scope of a federal road easement on his property.

## What happened

Larry Wilkins owns land crossed by Robbins Gulch Road, which is subject to a 1962 easement granted to the government. Wilkins sued under the Quiet Title Act, arguing the easement was meant only for limited government use, not public access. After earlier litigation reached the Supreme Court on a different issue, the case returned to the district court, which ruled his claims were filed too late and granted summary judgment to the government. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, finding that the public's long history of using the road, along with historic Forest Service maps, should have alerted a reasonable landowner that the government believed the easement allowed public access more than twelve years before Wilkins sued.

## What the Court is being asked

Wilkins asked the Court to decide two things: first, whether a lower court can treat its earlier, now-overturned ruling as binding on remand after the Supreme Court reversed the judgment it was part of; and second, whether a property owner's deadline to sue starts running because of unauthorized or illegal use of land by private citizens, rather than because of something the government itself did.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, and the Court denied it on October 13, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Wilkins, the property owner, asked the Court to take up his case. With the petition denied, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, meaning Wilkins's quiet-title claims remain time-barred and the government's win in the lower courts is final. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning on either the timing rule or the lower court's use of its earlier ruling; it simply leaves that decision in place without the Supreme Court deciding the questions itself.
