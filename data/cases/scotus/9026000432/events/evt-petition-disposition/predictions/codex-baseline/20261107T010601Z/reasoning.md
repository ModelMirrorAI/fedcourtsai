## What the filings establish

The question presented concerns terminating the final-judgment portion of an appeal as frivolous, without advance notice or merits briefing, while deciding a motion addressed only to a different, interlocutory portion. Appendix A, pages 1a–2a, confirms the two-part disposition: the panel dismissed the earlier-order challenges on nonmerger grounds, then separately dismissed the balance as lacking an arguable basis in law or fact. Appendix B, page 3a, records denial of reconsideration without a noted dissent. The petition, pages 10–21 and 28–32, accepts nonmerger and the appellate court's inherent screening authority, focusing on how that authority was exercised. Its footnote 2, page 29, expressly says the cited published decisions do not conflict on the process question. Variation in local summary-disposition procedures is a genuine argument for uniformity, but not the same evidence as an acknowledged split in holdings on this precise issue.

The strongest contrary evidence is Appendix C, especially pages 7a–11a: the district court applied a multi-factor dismissal standard, recounted repeated warnings, described a continuing refusal to commence arbitration despite an extension, and explained both why lesser sanctions would fail and how review could follow arbitration. These findings do not answer whether the appellate panel should have provided notice. They do make this an unattractive vehicle for establishing that an arguably meritorious appeal was cut short and give the Court a reason to leave a fact-bound screening determination alone.

The petition, pages 11–21, invokes party-presentation and notice precedents and describes Clark and Margolin as recent summary corrections. I treat those characterizations as advocacy, not independently verified holdings: attempted web retrieval returned no usable results. The analogy does not itself resolve whether screening a frivolous appeal requires the same procedure as introducing a new defense or deciding a case on an unbriefed theory. I independently read *Pillay v. INS*, 45 F.3d 14, 16–17 (2d Cir. 1995), through CourtListener (opinion 686765). It recognizes inherent authority to dismiss even a paid appeal without an arguably meritorious issue, while cautioning that the power must protect fair consideration of nonfrivolous claims. That supports both the panel's asserted authority and the petition's narrower cautionary argument; it does not establish an unconditional entitlement to full merits briefing.
