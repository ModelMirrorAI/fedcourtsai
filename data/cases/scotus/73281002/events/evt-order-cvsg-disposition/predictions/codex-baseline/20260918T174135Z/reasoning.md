## Why 56%, rather than the roughly 35% anchor

The substantive upward adjustment rests on the combination of a developed final-judgment vehicle, a close en banc division, and questions that extend well beyond this ballot-envelope requirement. The petition describes three disagreements: the reach of McDonald where in-person voting remains available; whether ordinary or minimally burdensome rules receive rational-basis treatment; and whether burden is measured by compliance effort or the lost vote following noncompliance. Its account of the 7-6 rehearing denial and the competing judicial treatments makes review more plausible than an isolated claim of error. These points come from the provisioned petition, especially its introduction and printed pages 17-25 and 35-37; I have not independently examined the full lower-court appendix.

The snapshot also records state and other amici, the Court's request for responses after initial waivers, and the eventual invitation for federal views. The CVSG and repeated distribution are already substantially represented in the high-band anchor, so I do not stack generic multipliers for them. Their case-specific significance is that the Court sought further examination of a recurring constitutional election-law issue rather than simply rejecting a fact-bound petition. A CVSG is not a commitment to grant or evidence of what recommendation the Solicitor General will make.

There are substantial downward considerations. The principal respondents' brief, printed pages 1-5 and 20-28, disputes that the asserted splits are current, square, or outcome-determinative. It distinguishes access to absentee ballots from rejection of ballots already lawfully cast. It also argues that meaningful balancing can remain deferential without becoming pure rational-basis review. Those are serious reasons not to accept the petition's characterization of three clean conflicts.

I checked one particularly concrete rebuttal through CourtListener: **Tully v. Okeson, 78 F.4th 377 (7th Cir. 2023)**, opinion 9556023. The opinion's opening discussion says the earlier preliminary-injunction decision did not establish law of the case and that the panel was not bound by its reasoning; it nevertheless affirmed on a fresh Twenty-Sixth Amendment analysis. This supports respondents' narrower point that reliance on the earlier decision needs qualification. It does not by itself establish that every asserted mail-voting conflict disappeared, particularly because the opinion concerns a different constitutional claim. I therefore discount, rather than erase, the split argument.

The factual justification is contested too. Petitioners emphasize trivial compliance costs and an instance of fraud detection. Respondents contend that the fraudulent ballot would have been rejected independently, that receipt tracking already supplies timeliness information, and that the injunction does not prevent officials from asking voters to date envelopes. Respondents also dispute that Coalfield Justice guarantees notice and a cure. These are competing accounts in the briefs, not facts I have independently resolved. The possibility that the result would survive even the petitioner's preferred standard reduces the vehicle's attraction.

Finally, both sides identify the pending state constitutional challenge, Baxter. Respondents say an independent state-law injunction could moot the federal dispute; petitioners expressly request Munsingwear-type vacatur if that happens and otherwise suggest a hold. I did not retrieve Baxter's subsequent history. The possibility of an unnecessary federal ruling reduces plenary-review probability, while the requested vacatur means that a mootness development need not produce an ungranted outcome under this cell's definition. That distinction is material to my 56% probability of **any** grant.

The move from approximately 35% to 56% is a judgmental case-specific adjustment, not a fitted coefficient or a claimed empirical rate for election-law CVSGs. Uncertainty about the government's recommendation, the genuine scope of the splits, and the state-law vehicle is large. I would not place this petition near certainty merely because its stakes are high.

## Other elicited judgments

- **Further distribution, 97%:** obtaining an invited brief normally leaves another conference consideration ahead. The forecast is an increment beyond the two frozen entries, not the probability that the petition has already been relisted.
- **CVSG increment, 0%:** the invitation is already on the record; the claim is vacuous for this CVSG cell.
- **Summary route given grant, 22%:** the principal summary possibility is procedural vacatur if the state-law proceeding removes the controversy; disagreement about the governing constitutional standard otherwise favors argument. This is conditional, not 22% of all outcomes.
- **Separate writing given denial, 24%:** the developed disagreement and election-law stakes make a statement plausible, but denial without comment remains the clear modal form. This is aggregate existence only.
- **Stakes, 0.85:** a nationwide standard governing ordinary election rules could affect much more than Pennsylvania's envelope dates. The score reflects that prospective reach and the competing voting-rights and state-authority interests, not grant likelihood or post-decision publicity.
