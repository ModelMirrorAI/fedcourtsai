Hunt has a strong vehicle for the second question. His crime occurred in 2019, when one juror could prevent a death sentence. All twelve jurors found five aggravating factors, but only ten voted for death. On that vote the former statute required life. Hunt objected before sentencing, and Florida’s highest court decided the federal issue on direct appeal. His reply makes a substantial argument under *Peugh* that lowering the threshold creates a significant risk of increased punishment. Florida relies on *Dobbert*, which upheld changes to Florida’s capital sentencing procedure, and *Collins*, which discussed a change in jury size. Hunt answers that a smaller unanimous jury can still have a holdout, while the new rule lets the judge impose death over two jurors’ objections. The principal weakness for review is the claimed conflict. The modern Massachusetts and South Carolina rulings concern parole-board votes. Oregon adopted a prospective rule by legislation, and the Louisiana decision used a since-abandoned test. The immediate retroactivity issue is confined to Florida offenses committed during its unanimity period. (Petition pp. 5–12, 19–23, appendix pp. 8a–12a, 21a–27a, opposition pp. 8–17, reply pp. 2–10.)

The first question has wider stakes, but it gives the Court less reason to take *Hunt* individually. Florida relies on *McKinney* for the distinction between finding a fact necessary for death eligibility and selecting a sentence once eligibility is established. Hunt stresses that a jury recommendation of life binds the judge. Florida also points out that the two states whose high courts took a contrary view, Connecticut and Delaware, have ceased using the death penalty. *Michael James Jackson v. Florida*, No. 26-76, is a genuine alternative vehicle. Hunt incorporates Jackson’s argument and offers to brief retroactivity if both cases are heard.
