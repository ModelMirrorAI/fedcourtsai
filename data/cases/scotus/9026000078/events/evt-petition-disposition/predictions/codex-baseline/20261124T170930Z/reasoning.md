I put the chance of any grant in *Hunt v. Florida* at **20%**. Hunt has an unusually clear record on retroactivity. He objected at trial, the Florida Supreme Court decided the federal question on direct appeal, and the jury’s 10–2 vote shows exactly what changed. Under the law in effect at the time of his 2019 crime, that tally bound the judge to impose life. Under the 2023 law, it authorized the death sentence the judge imposed. The reply makes a substantial argument under *Peugh* that a significant risk of greater punishment suffices even when the statutory maximum stays the same. (Pet. 4–7, 21–23, Pet. App. 8a–12a, 21a–27a, Reply 4–7.)

The obstacle to review is finding a disagreement that this case would settle. The Massachusetts and South Carolina cases involved parole boards. The older Louisiana case involved conviction under a since-displaced legal test, and Oregon limited retroactivity by legislation. Florida can also point to *Dobbert*, which upheld a change in capital sentencing procedure. Hunt has a concrete answer about what two votes for life used to accomplish, but the new threshold’s retroactive application concerns one state and a finite set of crimes. On the broader unanimity question, the jury unanimously found five aggravating circumstances. *Hurst* and *McKinney* give Florida a substantial argument that jurors must find the facts establishing death eligibility and can then divide over the choice of sentence. The two state courts Hunt cites on the other side sit in states that have since stopped using the death penalty. (Pet. 8–12, 18–22, BIO 1, 6–17, Reply 2–4, 9–10, Pet. App. 19a–21a.)
