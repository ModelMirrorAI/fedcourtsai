## Why this record moves well above the anchor

The Fifth Circuit affirmed an interlocutory refusal to dismiss a successive habeas application; it did **not** decide that Johnson is intellectually disabled or grant substantive habeas relief. Its appendix opinion, printed pages 5a–6a, explicitly reaffirmed Cathey's test requiring a previous possibility of merit before treating the rule as available. Thus a statutory reversal can be cleanly separated from a decision on Johnson's medical condition or the constitutional validity of his execution.

Texas's strongest argument is the distinction between **the availability of a rule and the factual capacity to win a claim under it**. Atkins preceded Johnson's crimes, final conviction, and first federal application. The respondent now agrees that “previously unavailable” modifies the rule. Under the State's reading, the diagnostic revision may supply a new factual predicate, but it does not turn Atkins itself into newly available constitutional law. The separate new-facts gateway in section 2244(b)(2)(B) makes the respondent's proposed overlap harder to defend. His alternative innocence-of-the-sentence argument was not the basis sustaining this application below.

The **United States supports Texas and reversal**, not Johnson. Its brief reinforces the structural distinction and adds that Atkins was not new relative to Johnson's final conviction. I substantially follow that recommendation because its independent textual route matches the difficulties exposed at argument; the government's participation alone does not supply the 0.94 figure. I expect the Court principally to reject the possibility-of-merit availability test, but the alternative new-rule ground makes reversal more robust than confidence in any single rationale. Moore's status as an independently retroactive rule need not be resolved: the parties presented Atkins as the qualifying rule, and the State says Moore has not been made retroactive by this Court.

## What argument adds, and its limits

The strongest pro-reversal signals are qualitative. Thomas required counsel to distinguish a rule from a claim and challenged the suggestion that a rule remains new indefinitely. Barrett pressed that same newness problem, the mismatch between legal and factual theories, and how to write a holding without deciding the pending-first-petition timing issue. Gorsuch pinned down the shared rule/claim grammar and queried the attempt to use both statutory gateways. Kavanaugh raised both the alternative new-rule route and the repeated-claims/finality objection. Alito supplied an account of Atkins under which medical criteria are evidence bearing on a constitutional category, not a self-updating constitutional rule. These exchanges suggest more than one path to five votes against the Fifth Circuit's approach.

Sotomayor and Jackson, by contrast, repeatedly explored whether the State's position denies any meaningful opportunity to present a newly viable claim and whether Panetti's ripeness reasoning should matter. Kagan developed the strongest legal answer for Johnson: the evolving clinical criteria might be incorporated into Atkins itself, so the change is not merely evidentiary. I take that seriously, rather than treating this as an uncontested statutory case. But Kagan also accepted the force of defendant-relative newness, making her the most plausible seventh vote for a narrow reversal. My modal call remains that she joins the dissent rather than accepting that rationale.

## Residual uncertainty, authorship, and stakes

The principal remaining uncertainty is **reversal versus vacatur and breadth**, not whether Johnson has the stronger statutory reading. The Court may reject only the relative-newness premise and remand without resolving all availability questions. A judgment leaving the Fifth Circuit intact is possible if the evolving-Atkins theory wins over a conservative Justice, but the transcript supplies no affirmative evidence of that fifth vote. The respondent's DIG request invokes the changing certified question and interlocutory posture. I allocate it 2% because the panel itself recast and answered the availability question, and the Justices engaged that question rather than dwelling on a vehicle defect. No nonparticipation is shown; an equal division is not a live independent scenario.

Thomas is my modal author, but only at **25%**, with Barrett close at **22%**: the rule/claim distinction fits Thomas's opening questions and Barrett's interest in framing a precise statutory holding. I have no verified sitting-assignment inventory and infer none. Authorship is substantially less predictable than the result.
