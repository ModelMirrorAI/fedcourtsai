## In brief

The Supreme Court will decide whether a death-row prisoner's long-available legal claim counted as "previously unavailable," letting him pursue it in a second federal habeas petition.

## What happened

Dexter Johnson was convicted of capital murder in Texas and sentenced to death. Years after his conviction, he sought to raise a claim that he is intellectually disabled and therefore cannot be executed, based on a rule the Supreme Court announced years before his crime. Federal law generally blocks prisoners from raising new claims in a second habeas petition unless they rely on a legal rule that was "previously unavailable." The Fifth Circuit allowed Johnson's claim to proceed, reasoning the claim had no realistic chance of success until diagnostic standards for intellectual disability changed. Texas, represented by prison official Eric Guerrero, appealed that interlocutory ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a legal rule counts as having been unavailable to a prisoner earlier only because a claim relying on it would likely have failed at that time, or whether unavailability instead depends solely on whether the rule itself existed and could have been raised, regardless of whether it would have succeeded.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on November 4, 2026; it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Texas, through Guerrero, is asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling that let Johnson's claim proceed. If the Court affirms, Johnson's intellectual-disability claim continues in the lower courts under the Fifth Circuit's approach. If the Court reverses, Johnson's claim would be barred as an improper successive petition, cutting off that avenue for relief. If the Court vacates, the case would return to the lower courts without a decision favoring either side.
