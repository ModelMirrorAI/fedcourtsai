## In brief

Texas asks the Supreme Court whether a death-row prisoner's long-available constitutional claim can still count as "previously unavailable" once new medical standards gave it a realistic chance of success.

## What happened

Dexter Johnson was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas. Years later, after diagnostic standards for intellectual disability changed, he sought to raise a claim under a Supreme Court case barring execution of people with intellectual disabilities, arguing that although that rule existed earlier, his claim had no real chance of success until the new standards appeared. A federal appeals court authorized him to file this new claim in a second habeas petition, and lower courts allowed it to proceed over the state's objection that federal law strictly limits repeat habeas filings. The state asked the appeals court to reconsider; it declined, prompting this appeal.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide when a legal rule that existed at the time of a prisoner's first federal case can still be treated as "unavailable" to him then, allowing him to raise it again later. Texas argues that availability should depend only on when the Supreme Court announced the rule, not on whether evidence or diagnostic standards made a claim likely to succeed at the time. Johnson argues that a rule is not truly available if a claim relying on it had no realistic chance of succeeding, regardless of when the rule was announced.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed, including merits briefs from both sides and amicus briefs from the federal government and others. Oral argument was scheduled, and the case was argued; the docket shows briefing and argument preparation continuing through late September 2026, with no decision yet recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Texas, as petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling that allowed Johnson's claim to proceed. If the Court rules for Texas, it would hold that Johnson's claim is barred because the underlying rule was available to him long ago, likely ending this habeas claim and reinforcing strict limits on repeat petitions nationwide. If the Court rules for Johnson, it would allow his claim to continue toward a decision on the merits in the lower courts. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the question either way.
