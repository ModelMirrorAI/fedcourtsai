## In brief

Texas asks the Supreme Court to bar a death row inmate from pursuing a renewed habeas claim that he is intellectually disabled and so ineligible for execution.

## What happened

Dexter Johnson was sentenced to death in 2007. At trial, an expert found his IQ too high to qualify as intellectually disabled under the diagnostic standards then used, so he did not raise a claim under Atkins v. Virginia, the Supreme Court decision barring execution of intellectually disabled people. After a 2013 revision to psychiatric diagnostic standards deemphasized IQ cutoffs, Johnson sought to raise an Atkins claim for the first time in a second federal habeas petition. The Fifth Circuit authorized him to proceed, reasoning that his claim had been "previously unavailable" under the law governing repeat habeas petitions. The federal district court agreed the claim could proceed, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed that ruling in an interlocutory appeal, prompting Texas's petition.

## What the Court is being asked

The case turns on a federal law limiting repeat habeas petitions, which allows a new claim only if it relies on a new constitutional rule that was previously unavailable to the prisoner. The Court must decide whether a rule counts as "previously unavailable" merely because a claim relying on it would have failed earlier under then-prevailing diagnostic standards, or whether the rule is only "previously unavailable" if it did not yet exist, or could not practically have been invoked, at the time of the prisoner's earlier case.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on November 4, 2026. It now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Texas, which lost below, is asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit and block Johnson's renewed claim. If the Court reverses, Johnson's Atkins claim would be barred from proceeding, leaving his death sentence in place unless he has other avenues for relief. If the Court affirms, Johnson's claim would continue toward a decision on the merits in the lower courts, without the Court having decided whether he is in fact intellectually disabled. The Court could also vacate and send the case back for further proceedings without resolving the question for either side.
