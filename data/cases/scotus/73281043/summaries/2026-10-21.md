## In brief

Texas asks the Supreme Court to decide whether a death row inmate's intellectual-disability claim came too late under federal habeas law, after lower courts let it proceed.

## What happened

Dexter Johnson was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas. A federal law called AEDPA generally bars prisoners from filing a second round of habeas corpus challenges to their conviction, unless the claim relies on a new constitutional rule that was "previously unavailable." Years after his first federal habeas petition was denied, Johnson sought to raise a claim under Atkins v. Virginia, which bars executing people with intellectual disability, after a revised diagnostic manual changed how such disability is assessed. The Fifth Circuit allowed him to pursue this claim in a second petition. The state prison director challenged that decision, and a federal district court and the Fifth Circuit again sided with Johnson. The Fifth Circuit declined to reconsider the case as a full court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide what "previously unavailable" means for a rule a prisoner wants to use in a second habeas case: does it depend only on whether the Supreme Court had already announced that legal rule before the prisoner's earlier case, or can a rule count as previously unavailable if a claim relying on it would have had no realistic chance of succeeding at that earlier time, even though the rule already existed.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; it was argued on November 4, 2026, and now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Texas, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up this case after losing in the lower courts. If the Court rules for Texas, it would mean Johnson's intellectual-disability claim does not meet the legal standard for a second habeas petition, and his case could be dismissed on that basis. If the Court rules for Johnson, the lower courts' decisions allowing his claim to proceed would stand, and his case would continue toward a ruling on whether he is in fact intellectually disabled. The Court could also send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
