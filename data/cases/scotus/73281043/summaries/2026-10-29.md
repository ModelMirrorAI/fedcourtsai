## In brief

The Court heard Texas's challenge to a Fifth Circuit ruling that let a death-row inmate pursue a second habeas claim based on updated medical standards for intellectual disability.

## What happened

Dexter Johnson was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas. He did not raise a claim that he was intellectually disabled, which would make him ineligible for execution, in his first round of state and federal habeas proceedings. Years later, after a medical association revised its diagnostic standards for intellectual disability, Johnson sought permission to raise that claim in a second federal habeas petition. The Fifth Circuit authorized the new petition and then, in an interlocutory appeal, held that the claim could proceed because federal law barring repeat habeas claims did not apply. Texas asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling, arguing it conflicts with other circuits' interpretation of the federal habeas statute.

## What the Court is being asked

Federal law generally blocks prisoners from filing a second federal habeas petition unless, among other exceptions, the new claim relies on a new constitutional rule that the Supreme Court has made retroactive and that was not available before. The Court is asked whether a rule counts as previously unavailable when the Supreme Court had already announced it before the prisoner's earlier habeas case, but a claim relying on that rule would not have succeeded at that time because the medical or legal standards for applying it later changed.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on November 4, 2026. Briefing is complete, including merits briefs from both sides and amicus briefs from the United States, the states of Louisiana and others, and legal defense organizations. The case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Texas, through prison official Eric Guerrero, is asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit and block Johnson's second habeas petition. If the Court affirms, Johnson's claim that he is intellectually disabled and ineligible for execution could proceed toward a decision on the merits in the lower courts. If the Court reverses, Johnson's second petition would be barred, ending that avenue for challenging his death sentence. If the Court vacates, the Fifth Circuit's decision would be set aside and the case sent back without resolving which side is right.
