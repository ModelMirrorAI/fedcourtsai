## In brief

A death row inmate asks the Court to review whether his trial lawyer's repeated sleeping during witness testimony violated his right to counsel.

## What happened

Juan Carlos Alvarez was convicted of capital murder in Texas and sentenced to death. He later argued in state and federal habeas proceedings that his lead trial attorney, Frumencio Reyes, repeatedly fell asleep during the trial, including while prosecution witnesses testified, and that his second attorney was barred by a local court rule from stepping in on those witnesses. State courts and a federal district court denied relief. A divided Fifth Circuit panel affirmed, holding that no clearly established Supreme Court law found a Sixth Amendment violation where a second, engaged attorney participated alongside the one accused of sleeping. A dissenting judge would have granted relief.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a defendant is denied his right to a lawyer's help when his trial attorney sleeps through parts of the trial, even though he had a second attorney present, and whether the appeals court applied the wrong, overly strict standard for deciding that question under the federal habeas law that limits review of state convictions.

## Where it stands

The petition, opposition brief, and reply have all been filed, and two amicus briefs support Alvarez. The case was distributed for the Court's conference of December 4, 2026, and the docket shows no action since then.

## What each outcome would mean

Alvarez, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether his sleeping-lawyer claim should succeed; a grant alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision rejecting Alvarez's claim stands, leaving his conviction and death sentence in place. Either outcome is a decision only about whether to hear the case, not a ruling on the underlying claim.
