I put the chance of any grant at **12%**. The two jurors’ accounts identify what made lead counsel’s alleged sleeping matter. They described Frumencio Reyes repeatedly dozing while the prosecution questioned witnesses. Reyes handled 25 of the State’s 40 witnesses across the guilt and penalty phases, and the trial judge enforced a one-lawyer-per-witness rule for objections. A second lawyer in the courtroom therefore offered limited protection when Reyes missed testimony from his own witnesses. Judge Richman’s panel dissent found actual prejudice under *Strickland*, including the impression counsel’s sleeping made on jurors who would decide whether Alvarez lived. She could reach that conclusion without presuming prejudice under *Cronic*. The published opinion in No. 18-70001 matches the petition appendix. Rehearing was denied without a request for an en banc poll. (Pet. 6–12, 19–23, App. 33a–36a, 57a–59a, 224a, 272a, and the staged decision and lower-court docket.)

The obstacle is proving that the state court unreasonably denied relief on the record before it. Texas points to co-counsel’s active participation, the lack of an identified question or objection that Reyes missed, and the evidence supporting the conviction and sentence. Alvarez answers that the Texas Court of Criminal Appeals expressly declined to adopt the trial court’s findings on counsel’s performance. His juror affidavits were before the state courts. Reyes’s own much later affidavit was excluded. Even crediting the jurors, tying particular lapses to particular testimony and deciding what co-counsel could do from counsel table remain difficult. Those questions matter to whether Alvarez lacked any assistance at a critical stage and, alternatively, whether the lapse affected the result. (BIO 7–20, Reply 3–9, App. 13a–25a.)

Alvarez’s strongest ground for intervention is the panel’s apparent demand for a Supreme Court precedent involving a second, engaged lawyer. *Andrew v. White*, 604 U.S. 86, 94–95 (2025), allows an established holding to govern new facts. But the five circuits Alvarez cites for their reading of *Andrew* did not decide this two-lawyer question. The petition itself explains why the local courtroom rule makes a closer conflict unlikely. *Woods v. Donald*, 575 U.S. 312 (2015), also illustrates how closely the Court examines what testimony counsel missed before allowing a presumption of prejudice on federal habeas review. Donald’s lawyer missed testimony about codefendants. Alvarez claims his lawyer missed testimony against him. (Pet. 22–23, Reply 2–4.)
