## Adjustment from the anchor

**The forfeiture obstacle dominates.** The petition's account of the Third Circuit decision says the argument that Ignelzi acted as a law-enforcement officer was forfeited because it was not raised in the district court (petition, printed pp. 9–10). It also reproduces the district court's observation that the plaintiffs did not dispute judicial capacity and instead challenged jurisdiction. The petition argues that its factual allegations preserved the functional theory and invokes discretionary consideration of an unpreserved question (printed pp. 19–21). That is an argument for overcoming a threshold obstacle, not proof that the obstacle has disappeared. Even accepting its account of an alternative merits ruling, obtaining substantive review would require the Court to confront preservation first or explain why it does not prevent relief. This makes a poor vehicle for resolving a generally framed immunity question.

**The claimed four-to-one split is not established by different outcomes alone.** On the petition's descriptions, Gibson involves a judge personally participating in a home search; Rockett involves personally placing children in cells; King involves misidentifying a person to police; and Harper involves action unrelated to a pending matter (printed pp. 12–15). Here the quoted allegations emphasize directions to deputies and coercive proceedings connected with an existing landlord-tenant dispute. I cannot infer from the petition's repeated characterization of a judge-led raid that the complaint establishes the same physical participation or absence of a pending judicial matter as those comparators. The lower decision is also described as nonprecedential. Together these features weaken the claim that a clean, binding conflict requires intervention, while leaving a genuine functional-boundary issue worth some positive weight.

**Controlling precedent complicates summary correction.** I independently retrieved *Mireles v. Waco*, 502 U.S. 9, 12–13 (1991), through CourtListener. It protects an order directing officers to bring counsel before a judge despite allegations of excessive force, treating the function in relation to a pending proceeding rather than isolating the wrongful means. The Court also distinguishes directing officers from personally performing their function. That does not decide Bernard's home-arrest question: the location and alleged operational involvement could matter. It does make the petition's claim of obvious conflict with settled law substantially less certain. This is my analogy, not a holding about Bernard. The petition's distinction of Mireles and its express request for summary reversal receive weight, but not the weight of an uncontested legal error (petition, printed pp. 19–22).

The alleged warrantless home arrest and the protection of civil-rights remedies support a nonzero chance of interest. Against that are forfeiture, fact-sensitive comparator distinctions, a nonprecedential decision, and a docket showing waiver without further attention. I therefore reduce the roughly 5.12% anchor to **1.5%**, rather than increasing it simply because the petition asserts a mature split. The adjustment is judgmental, not a fitted numerical model. The missing opposition and appendix limit confidence in its size.
