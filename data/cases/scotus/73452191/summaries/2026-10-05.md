## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a Pennsylvania man's bid to revive his lawsuit accusing a state judge of directing his warrantless home arrest.

## What happened

Walter Bernard sued Judge Philip Ignelzi under a federal civil-rights law, claiming Ignelzi personally directed sheriff's deputies to arrest him at home without a warrant during a landlord-tenant dispute, and later had him jailed again. A federal district court dismissed the claims against Ignelzi, ruling he was protected by absolute judicial immunity, a legal rule that shields judges from being sued over their judicial acts. The Third Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, and also found Bernard had forfeited, or failed to properly raise, part of his argument. The Third Circuit denied rehearing, and Bernard then asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether judicial immunity protects a judge from a lawsuit when the judge personally directs and oversees police officers carrying out a warrantless arrest of someone in their own home, rather than simply issuing orders from the bench. Bernard's petition argues that other appeals courts have ruled such hands-on direction of an arrest is not a judicial act and gets no immunity, while the Third Circuit ruled the opposite.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, where the justices considered it. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Bernard, the petitioner, asked the Court to hear his case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Third Circuit's ruling in place, meaning Ignelzi keeps the protection of judicial immunity and Bernard's lawsuit against him remains dismissed. Had the Court instead granted the petition, it would have agreed to hear the case and decide the immunity question itself, without yet deciding which side was right.
