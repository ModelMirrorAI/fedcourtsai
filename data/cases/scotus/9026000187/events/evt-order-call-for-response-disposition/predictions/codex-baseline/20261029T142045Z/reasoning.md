None of these terminal cuts estimates the forward hazard of this particular response request, and none supplies a response-request-conditioned rate.

I move above 5.11% because the petition identifies a developed multi-circuit disagreement about extending Kingsley's objective excessive-force standard to medical care (QP; petition pp. 6–13), and the Court affirmatively requested a response. The Fifth Circuit's published opinion applies the subjective-awareness requirement directly, making the legal question more than an abstract request for error correction. The response request is still a low-threshold signal, not evidence that four Justices favor review.

The strongest downward adjustment is the vehicle. In the appendix, Judge Wiener's concurrence disagrees with the majority's treatment of subjective knowledge but would affirm on qualified immunity because the pertinent right was not clearly established (pp. 7a–9a). The district court also supplies a separate clearly-established-law ground (pp. 24a–26a). Thus changing the constitutional liability standard might not change the judgment. This is an obstacle, not an assertion that the panel majority itself adopted the concurrence's alternative reasoning. The petition's final argument explains why the plaintiff could survive under an objective test (pp. 15–17) but does not comparably resolve this immunity problem. The disagreement over the doctor's knowledge, delegated care, and an unopened email attachment adds fact-bound complications. These considerations keep my forecast at 14%, well short of a likely grant despite the split and tragic facts.
