## In brief

A mother whose newborn died after an unassisted jail birth asks the Court to apply a more lenient legal standard to her medical-care claim against the jail doctor.

## What happened

Chasity Congious was a pregnant pretrial detainee at a Texas jail. She gave birth alone in her cell, and the baby, found with the umbilical cord around her neck, died ten days later. Congious sued Dr. Aaron Shaw, the jail's medical director, claiming he denied her adequate medical care in violation of her constitutional rights. The district court granted summary judgment to Shaw, finding he lacked personal knowledge of her danger and had qualified immunity, a legal protection shielding officials from damages unless they violated clearly established rights. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, with one judge concurring only on the immunity ground.

## What the Court is being asked

Federal appeals courts disagree on what a detainee must prove when a jail official is accused of failing to provide needed medical care: some require showing the official actually realized the danger and ignored it, while others ask only whether the official's conduct was unreasonable given what a person in that position should have recognized. The Court is asked to decide which approach applies.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, after which the Court requested a response from Shaw rather than acting on the petition. Shaw has since filed his opposition and Congious has replied; the petition remains pending with no further action recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Congious is asking the Court to take up her case and adopt the more lenient standard. If the Court denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's ruling for Shaw stands, leaving the stricter standard in place in that circuit and ending Congious's case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments on which standard applies; a decision adopting Congious's preferred standard could send her case back for further proceedings, while a decision upholding the stricter standard would likely leave Shaw's win intact. Shaw's brief also argues that qualified immunity would protect him regardless of which standard applies.
