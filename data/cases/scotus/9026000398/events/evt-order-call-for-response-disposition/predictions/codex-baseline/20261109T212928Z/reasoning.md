## Anchor and adjustments

The authoritative frozen `cert_anchor.rate` is **0.051587928582714486**, approximately 5.16%, for the sal-v8 baseline risk set pooled over prior Terms under the ten-Term lookback. I use this reached-band rate, not the roughly 1% terminal-baseline rate. The committed `metrics/statpack.md` is consistent with this scale: prior-Term baseline reached rates range from 4.0% to 6.0%. Its modern discretionary-cert disposition counts also show why the grant family, rather than grants alone or historical merits labels, is the relevant target.

The required signal cross-checks are population shapes, not conditional response-request rates. In the paid scored segment, the relist buckets report grant-family shares of approximately 1.8% at zero relists (10,204 resolved), 13.5% at one (2,532), 41.2% at two (495), and 37.3% at three or more (483). These are terminal buckets and can include reschedules. They do not justify treating this petition's next administrative distribution as the same evidence as a considered petition repeatedly relisted. The CVSG cut reports approximately 6.4% grant-family without a CVSG (13,550 resolved) and 34.8% with one (164); that association is not a forecast that a CVSG will occur here. There is no committed response-request-conditioned table.

I move substantially above the 5.16% anchor because the Court affirmatively requested an opposition and the petition presents a concrete statutory disagreement, rather than only error correction. The question concerns whether knowingly sending video-viewing information that the actual recipient can link to an individual falls within the VPPA even when an ordinary person could not decode it. The petition identifies the First Circuit's recipient-sensitive approach against the Second, Third and Ninth Circuits' ordinary-person approach (petition pp. 12–17). The alleged disclosure is a Facebook identifier paired with viewed-video information, with actual knowledge alleged. Final judgment and a pleading-stage legal issue are favorable vehicle features.

The original First Circuit opinion, *Yershov v. Gannett*, 820 F.3d 482, 485–490 (2016), independently retrieved through CourtListener, supports the recipient-capability distinction. But it also involved GPS coordinates and an Android identifier and emphasized the particular alleged transaction. The factual distinction leaves room for an opposition to contest how cleanly its holding conflicts with this Facebook-Pixel case. I do not assume that every formulation in the petition's claimed 3–1 split is equivalent.

Two counterweights keep the forecast well below even odds. First, the bound Second Circuit summary order expressly records that certiorari in *Solomon*, its controlling, factually similar precedent, was denied on **December 8, 2025** (appendix pp. 3a–7a). This is an earlier case's denial stated in the provisioned record, not Golden's outcome. It is meaningful adverse evidence against immediate plenary review of essentially the same issue. Golden's nonprecedential order applies that existing rule rather than creating a new circuit conflict. Second, the panel rejected the contention that general recent textualist decisions displaced *Solomon*. The petition's merits argument may be persuasive without showing a new reason for the Court to revisit its cert selection.

## Salazar and the route uncertainty

The petition expressly requests a hold for *Salazar v. Paramount Global*, No. 25-459, and a later GVR if appropriate (petition pp. 12, 32). The official Supreme Court docket independently confirms a January 16, 2026 grant and October 6, 2026 argument. The official question-presented sheet asks about the VPPA's **consumer/subscriber** definition, not the personally-identifiable-information definition here. These are pre-snapshot developments in another case. They make a hold plausible and a GVR a meaningful part of the grant probability, but do not establish that Salazar will resolve Golden's different question. A decision excluding newsletter-only subscribers could instead supply an additional obstacle to Golden's claim; a broad subscriber definition need not disturb the separate PII ruling.

I therefore allocate **40% of the grant probability to a summary route**, predominantly a GVR, and 60% to plenary review. In unconditional terms those are 8% and 12%, respectively; denial remains overwhelmingly the largest disposition category. This decomposition is judgment, not a measured historical frequency. I did not seek argument commentary or predict Salazar's holding as a fact. The companion docket page also displayed an October 29 corporate letter entry, which I disregarded; it concerns another case, not Golden's disposition, and contributes nothing to the forecast.
