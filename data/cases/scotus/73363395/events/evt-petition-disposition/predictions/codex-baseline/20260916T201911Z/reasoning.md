## Why 0.5%, below that anchor

The QPs ask whether the estate should obtain a jury trial against the Uber entities so that jurors can weigh the evidence and draw adverse inferences, invoking first the Seventh Amendment and then Fourteenth Amendment due process. The petition's factual presentation concerns the driver's phones, app status, driving history, corporate knowledge, expert testimony, and Uber's degree of control. Those allegations explain why the litigant contests summary judgment; they do not themselves establish a national federal-law conflict. I treat the factual account as advocacy, not adjudicated fact. See petition pp. 1–3, 4–9, 17–20, 22–30, and 40–47.

The largest downward adjustment is the federal vehicle. In **Walker v. Sauvinet, 92 U.S. 90, 92–93 (1876)**, independently retrieved through CourtListener, the Court held that the Seventh Amendment jury guarantee concerns federal courts and that the Fourteenth Amendment does not require a civil jury in every state proceeding affecting property. The petition itself reproduces Florida authority acknowledging the federal-court limitation and using federal precedent as persuasive guidance for Florida's own jury guarantee (pp. 42, 44). Reliance on that state-law analogue does not by itself establish a federal constitutional violation. My inference is that the petition's general insistence on a demanded jury leaves a major gap between a potentially contestable state summary judgment and a cert-worthy federal question.

The reasons section emphasizes Florida constitutional authorities and general jury-trial language from federal cases, rather than identifying opposed holdings on a sharply framed federal question. The petition does not persuasively separate an independent due-process violation from disagreement about evidentiary sufficiency. I cannot rule out a preserved constitutional issue or trial-court error without the appendix; I therefore retain a small nonzero grant probability rather than treating the legal obstacle as jurisdictional certainty.

The response waiver, absence of a recorded call for response, and lack of CVSG or a petition relist add a modest negative procedural adjustment. A waiver is not dispositive: the Court could request a response. I do not mistake the absence of a BIO for a concession, or the sealing motion's grant for favorable cert attention. Together the weak federal framing and ordinary processing justify a roughly tenfold reduction from the private-petitioner anchor to **0.005**. This adjustment is judgmental, not a fitted likelihood ratio.

## Other probabilities and limitations

The **3.5%** additional-distribution probability allows for further examination, a response request, or rescheduling; zero is the modal increment. **0.1%** for a CVSG reflects the absence of a developed federal governmental or regulatory question despite the corporate respondent. The petition mentions an FTC order as background evidence, but that does not turn its jury-access questions into a request to interpret a federal regulatory program. **15% conditional on grant** for the summary route leaves room for error correction while favoring plenary consideration of any constitutional issue important enough to attract review. **1% conditional on denial** for a dissent or statement reflects the absence of an identified recurring doctrinal controversy in the submitted argument. These latter estimates are subjective; I do not present the terminal rate cuts as their baselines.

The stakes score **0.28** recognizes fatal injury, rideshare liability, and the potential reach of a civil-jury ruling while distinguishing those stakes from this petition's weak grant prospects. No Justice-level cert votes are predicted.
