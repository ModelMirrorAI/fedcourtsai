## In brief

The Court declined to hear Radiall's bid to revive its challenge to a jury verdict favoring a former employee's family leave retaliation claim.

## What happened

Gary Ramadei sued his former employer, Radiall USA, for retaliating against him for taking medical leave, among other claims. A jury found for Ramadei on his family-leave retaliation claim, awarding him about $187,000 in damages, later increased to over $770,000 with fees and interest. The trial court had instructed the jury using a standard allowing Ramadei to win by showing his leave was only a motivating factor in his firing, rather than requiring him to show it was the determining cause. After the trial, the Supreme Court issued a decision ending a long-standing practice of courts deferring to certain agency interpretations of ambiguous statutes. On appeal, Radiall argued this change undermined the lower causation standard, but the Second Circuit held Radiall had not properly preserved that argument and affirmed the judgment.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether family-leave retaliation claims require proof that taking leave was the determining cause of a firing, rather than merely one factor among others, and whether an appeals court may decline to decide such a purely legal question on the grounds that a party failed to raise it early enough, when the earlier courts were bound by existing precedent and the argument only became available after a later Supreme Court ruling changed the legal landscape.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference, after the respondent waived his right to respond and two amicus briefs were filed. The Court denied the petition on November 9, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Radiall, which lost below, asked the Court to take up the case and reverse. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Second Circuit's decision in place, so the judgment for Ramadei, including the damages award, stands. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Second Circuit's reasoning on causation or on preservation of legal arguments; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed without deciding the underlying questions.
