## Why 0.6% rather than 5.12%

**Preservation is the strongest negative.** Petition pp. 11 and 14–15 acknowledge that counsel did not raise the constitutional issues at the merits hearing and that the appellate court rejected them on that basis. The petition's ineffective-assistance and plain-error framing does not itself establish a route around that obstacle. Without the appellate opinion I cannot determine whether there is an adequate and independent state-law ground, so I use this as a substantial vehicle risk rather than pronounce a jurisdictional holding.

**The requested review is unusually fact-bound and diffuse.** QPs 1 and 4 and petition pp. 8–14 seek reassessment of harassment, intent, witness treatment, transcript changes, exhibits, and briefing deadlines. QP 3 adds a broad attack on the state statute without identifying a developed conflict among courts. The table of authorities lists Kennedy v. Bremerton School District as its sole case authority; the petition does not develop a competing appellate rule. These features sharply reduce the force of a systemic-review rationale. Rule 10's discretionary, compelling-reason standard and its caution about factual-error correction reinforce that inference; they do not establish how this particular petition must resolve. (Provisioned petition, pp. i, ix, 8–15; Supreme Court Rules, official 2023 edition, Rule 10, pp. 5–6.)

**The constitutional interests are real but do not cure the vehicle.** The prayer-walk allegation provides a potentially intelligible First Amendment issue, and the claimed firearm surrender makes the consequences more than a disagreement over family contact. Nevertheless, Rahimi holds that temporary disarmament following a judicial finding of a credible physical-safety threat can be consistent with the Second Amendment. It does not decide every protective-order application or eliminate notice, hearing, and individualized-finding questions. This petition disputes the findings and covers state-law restrictions and family relationships distinct from Rahimi's federal prosecution; I do not equate its orders with the federal statute there. Rahimi therefore weakens the broad proposition that an absence of criminal conviction alone compels intervention, without settling petitioners' actual constitutional claims. (United States v. Rahimi, 602 U.S. 680 (2024), majority opinion, slip pp. 8, 13–17, accessed through CourtListener opinion 10145945; provisioned petition, pp. 7, 11, 14–15.)

**No observed institutional-attention signal offsets those concerns.** The supplied record has a first distribution and no response request, CVSG, or amicus entry. This is a limited negative signal, not a conclusion from docket silence that the merits are weak. I put substantially more weight on the petition's conceded preservation problem and lack of a developed split than on pro se status or the lack of an opposition text.

The resulting 0.006 is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted likelihood calculation: roughly an eight-and-a-half-fold reduction from the reached-band anchor. I retain a small nonzero chance that a serious constitutional defect survives the procedural obstacles or that additional record material changes the vehicle assessment. My modal disposition is denial, not a confident prediction of which jurisdictional or discretionary concern any Justice would privately find decisive.
