## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a couple's challenge to Illinois protective orders they say violated their constitutional rights.

## What happened

Katie Dittmer obtained orders of protection against her parents-in-law, Ronald and Irene Dittmer, under the Illinois Domestic Violence Act, after incidents including a prayer walk Irene took near Katie's home and emails Ronald sent to Katie and her husband. An Illinois circuit court found by a preponderance of the evidence that Ronald and Irene posed a risk of future harm and entered two-year protective orders, including surrender of firearms. The Appellate Court of Illinois affirmed, finding Ronald and Irene had not properly raised their constitutional arguments below. The Illinois Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

Ronald and Irene ask the Court to decide whether the protective orders against them improperly restricted their religious practice, speech, gun rights, due process, and equal protection. They also ask whether the Illinois Domestic Violence Act itself is unconstitutionally vague or broad, whether their trial lawyer's failure to raise these constitutional arguments should excuse not raising them earlier, and whether the state courts ignored evidence of procedural errors in handling their case.

## Where it stands

## What each outcome would mean

Ronald and Irene, who lost in the Illinois courts, asked the Supreme Court to take their case. The denial means the Court will not review the matter, so the Illinois Appellate Court's decision upholding the protective orders against Ronald and Irene stands. Katie's position, which prevailed in the Illinois courts, remains in effect. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning, only that it left that decision in place.
