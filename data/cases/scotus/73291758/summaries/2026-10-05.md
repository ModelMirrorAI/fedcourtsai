## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a couple's challenge to Illinois protective orders they said violated their constitutional rights.

## What happened

Katie Dittmer obtained emergency and then two-year orders of protection against her parents-in-law, Ronald and Irene Dittmer, under the Illinois Domestic Violence Act, after incidents including a prayer walk near her home and family communications she said amounted to harassment. An Illinois circuit court found by a preponderance of the evidence that the Dittmers posed a risk of future harm and entered the orders, requiring them to surrender firearms. The Illinois Appellate Court affirmed, and the Illinois Supreme Court denied further review. The Dittmers, representing themselves, then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition raises several questions: whether the protective orders improperly burdened the Dittmers' religious, speech, gun-rights, due process, and equal protection rights; whether the Illinois Appellate Court wrongly refused to consider constitutional arguments their trial lawyer failed to raise; whether the Illinois domestic violence law itself is written broadly enough to risk violating such rights; and whether the appellate courts ignored evidence of procedural errors in handling the case.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and on October 5, 2026, the Court denied it.

## What each outcome would mean

Ronald and Irene Dittmer, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up their case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Illinois Appellate Court's decision in place, meaning the protective orders against the Dittmers, including the loss of their firearms, stand. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the lower courts' reasoning; it simply means the case will not be reviewed further, and Katie Dittmer's position as the party who obtained the orders prevails as a result.
