## Why above the anchor, but below even odds

**Reasons to increase grant probability:** The petition presents a recognizable, potentially consequential legal question rather than merely disputing damages or factual findings. It argues that the Fifth Circuit has treated Hope's obvious-violation principle as unavailable outside Eighth Amendment litigation, in conflict with other circuits' First Amendment decisions (petition pp. 24–32). The petition describes a fractured panel, Judge Ho's express invitation to revisit the immunity issue, and disagreement during rehearing (pp. 9–13). Fifteen amicus filings indicate unusually broad organized interest, although their number does not establish the merits of the asserted split. The additional distribution after the September conference confirms continued consideration. That signal is already partly priced into the elevated anchor; I do not count it as fifteen independent endorsements or a wholly new second adjustment.

**Reasons to resist a much higher forecast:** The BIO directly attacks the existence of a categorical split rather than conceding one. It contends that the Fifth Circuit applies Hope outside the Eighth Amendment, that Judge Ho's interpretation is not the panel's holding, and that Judge Richman's reasoning turns on uncertain forum status and inadequate allegations of viewpoint discrimination (BIO pp. 18–26). These are advocacy positions, not independently adjudicated findings in this forecast. They nevertheless offer a substantial basis to treat the petition as an application dispute rather than a clean conflict.

The preservation objection is especially significant: the BIO says Hershey did not invoke Hope in his district-court or initial appellate briefing, sought rehearing on this issue only out of time, and never pleaded a free-exercise claim (pp. 26–28). The petition itself acknowledges the unsuccessful out-of-time rehearing effort (p. 13), although it portrays the resulting opinions as making this a suitable vehicle. I cannot resolve preservation conclusively from the excerpts alone. The BIO additionally disputes whether an internal sidewalk serving a privately operated, ticketed arena was obviously a traditional public forum and whether the officers knew the leaflets' viewpoint (pp. 28–32). Those alternative obstacles could defeat relief even if the Court agreed with the broad legal proposition. The City's separate Monell petition supplies procedural context, not evidence that Hershey's petition will be granted.

For general legal context I verified the relevant majority passage in **Hope v. Pelzer, 536 U.S. 730, 740–41 (2002)** through CourtListener opinion 9434318. It explains that sufficiently clear general principles can supply fair warning in novel circumstances. That supports the seriousness of Hershey's doctrinal argument, but does not verify his characterization of the Fifth Circuit or establish that these pleaded facts meet the standard. Taken together, the substantive interest warrants moving from approximately 17% to 26%; the contested premise and vehicle objections keep denial the clear modal result. This adjustment is judgmental, not an estimated regression effect.
