## What happened

Texas law requires every public-school classroom to permanently display a state-specified version of the Ten Commandments. Parents from various faiths, including Jewish, Hindu, Christian, Baha'i, and nonreligious families, sued two groups of school districts, arguing the law violates their and their children's religious freedom. Federal district courts blocked the law. In one case, the full Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals, sitting together, reversed by a 9-8 vote, ruling the displays are constitutional. The court reasoned that an older Supreme Court decision striking down a similar Kentucky law is no longer valid because of more recent Supreme Court rulings. In the second case, a Fifth Circuit panel followed that ruling to reverse the injunction there too. The families, who lost in the court of appeals, now jointly ask the Supreme Court to review both cases.

## What the Court is being asked

The families ask the Court to decide two things. First, whether it is unconstitutional for a state to require every public-school classroom to permanently display a government-chosen version of the Ten Commandments. Second, whether such mandatory, permanent displays interfere with parents' right to guide their children's religious upbringing and burden children's own religious freedom. The petition argues the appeals court was wrong to treat an old Supreme Court precedent striking down a similar law as no longer binding, and separately argues the displays conflict with the Court's more recent rulings on parents' rights to direct their children's religious education.

## Where it stands

The petition for certiorari, the formal request that the Supreme Court take up the case, was filed on August 17, 2026, after the deadline to file was extended by Justice Alito. The school districts' response is due September 21, 2026, and the Court has not yet acted on the petition.
