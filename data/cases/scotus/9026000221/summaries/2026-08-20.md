## In brief

Texas families ask the Supreme Court to strike down a law requiring Ten Commandments displays in every public school classroom.

## What happened

Texas enacted a law, S.B. 10, requiring every public school classroom to permanently display a state-specified version of the Ten Commandments. Two groups of families of various faiths sued separate school districts, arguing the mandate violates the First Amendment's protections against government establishment of religion and for free exercise of religion. District courts blocked the law in both cases. In one case, the full Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, voting 9-8 that the law is constitutional and that an older Supreme Court decision striking down a similar Kentucky law no longer controls. The Fifth Circuit then summarily reversed the injunction in the second case based on the first ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state may require every public school classroom to permanently display a government-chosen version of the Ten Commandments without violating the ban on government establishment of religion, and separately, whether such displays interfere with parents' right to direct their children's religious upbringing and burden children's religious freedom.

## Where it stands

The families filed a joint petition for certiorari on August 17, 2026, asking the Court either to summarily reverse the lower court or grant full review. The school districts' response is due September 21, 2026, and the Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The families, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling upholding the displays stands, and the school districts may continue enforcing the law. If the Court grants review, it will hear full briefing and argument before deciding the constitutional questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who prevails. The petition also asks the Court to summarily reverse without full briefing, which would immediately overturn the Fifth Circuit's ruling in the families' favor, or to vacate that ruling and send the case back for reconsideration without deciding the outcome.
