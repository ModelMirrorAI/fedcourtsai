## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a Kansas man's claim that police testimony relaying a child's accusation against him violated his confrontation rights.

## What happened

Theodore Purdy was convicted of rape and aggravated indecent liberties with a child after a three-year-old accuser did not testify at trial. Instead, a detective testified that the child had accused Purdy of inserting something into her vagina, offered as background explaining the police investigation rather than for its truth. The prosecution later cited this testimony in closing argument to corroborate Purdy's confession. Purdy argued in state postconviction proceedings that his trial lawyer should have objected on Confrontation Clause grounds. Kansas courts found the testimony was not hearsay and that any error did not affect the trial's outcome. The Kansas Court of Appeals affirmed, and the Kansas Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether prosecutors can get around a defendant's right to confront accusers by having a police officer repeat an absent witness's accusation under the label of explaining an investigation, when the accusation describes the very crime charged and is then used to help prove guilt.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, and on October 13, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Purdy, who lost in the Kansas courts, asked the Supreme Court to take up his case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Kansas Court of Appeals' decision in place, meaning Purdy's rape conviction stands and the testimony at issue was not found to violate his confrontation rights. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Kansas courts' reasoning; it simply means the case will not be reviewed, and no nationwide rule on this question was decided.
