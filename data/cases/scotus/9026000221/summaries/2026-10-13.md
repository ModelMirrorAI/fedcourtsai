## In brief

The Court declined to hear Theodore Purdy's challenge to his Kansas rape conviction, which argued that testimony relaying a child's accusation violated his confrontation rights.

## What happened

A Kansas jury convicted Theodore Purdy of rape based partly on his own confession to police. At trial, two law-enforcement witnesses testified about statements the three-year-old complainant made, though she never testified herself. The prosecution argued this testimony merely explained the investigation but then used it in closing argument to support Purdy's confession. On direct appeal, Kansas courts treated the testimony as corroborating evidence. In a later postconviction proceeding, Purdy argued his trial lawyer should have objected on constitutional grounds; Kansas courts rejected this, calling the testimony non-hearsay "background" and finding no prejudice. The Kansas Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks whether the Constitution's guarantee of confronting witnesses allows prosecutors to introduce an absent child accuser's statement through a police officer by labeling it investigative background, when the statement describes the charged crime and is later used to prove guilt. Purdy's petition says lower federal courts disagree about when such "background" testimony crosses into using an absent witness's accusation for its truth.

## Where it stands

The State of Kansas waived its right to respond to the petition. The Court distributed the case for its October 9, 2026 conference and denied the petition on October 13, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Purdy, who asked the Court to hear his case, sought to overturn the Kansas Court of Appeals' decision upholding his conviction. The denial leaves that decision in place, meaning Purdy's rape conviction and life sentence stand, and the courts' ruling that the officer's testimony was properly admitted remains the final word in his case. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Kansas courts' reasoning; it simply leaves the lower court's judgment undisturbed without deciding the constitutional question Purdy raised.
