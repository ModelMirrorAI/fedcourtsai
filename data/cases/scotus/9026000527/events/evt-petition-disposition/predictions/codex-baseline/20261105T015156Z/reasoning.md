## Why below the anchor

The petition presents a genuine legal tension: B&B Hardware, Inc. v. Hargis Industries, Inc., 575 U.S. 138, 154–57 (2015), equates the legal likelihood-of-confusion standard in registration and infringement proceedings, yet conditions preclusion on materially equivalent uses. Cesari argues that the Second Circuit improperly revived Jim Beam and Levy and made narrower uses categorically fatal to preclusion. The panel instead relies on B&B Hardware's exception for marketplace uses the Board did not adjudicate. These competing readings appear in the petition at 12–21 and the appellate order at appendix 6a–10a. The doctrinal distinction is between the same legal standard and the same factual issue; satisfying the former does not automatically establish the latter.

The strongest reason for intervention is QP2. The TTAB decision at appendix 33a–34a finds the marks nearly identical and addresses the dessert-wine contention but declines to limit the application by that contention. The district court at appendix 18a–20a treats actual wine sales as encompassed by the unrestricted application. An argument that merely narrower, ordinary uses cannot defeat preclusion therefore has substance. Cesari also alleges that the appellate court incorrectly treated dessert wine as the principal infringement defense, although the judgment covered many wines; that is advocacy in the petition at 22–25, not an independently established finding.

Against review, the decision is an unpublished, nonprecedential summary order remanding for further proceedings, rather than a developed precedential resolution of an acknowledged post-B&B inter-circuit conflict. The petition mainly alleges conflict with this Court's precedent; it does not demonstrate contemporary, competing circuit holdings on materially identical uses. The panel expressly engages B&B Hardware rather than openly refusing to follow it. Deciding whether the panel's distinction is valid requires close attention to the registration record, actual products, and litigation positions. The party-presentation criticism adds another record-specific dispute, not a separately stated question presented. Respondent's waiver is a modest negative signal, although it is not an admission on the merits and a response could still be requested. Together these features justify reducing the anchor to 2.5%, while retaining a real allowance for correction of the included-usages issue.
