## In brief

An Italian winemaker asks the Court to restore its trademark infringement win against a California winery, arguing the appeals court wrongly revived outdated legal standards.

## What happened

Cesari, an Italian winery, registered the trademark LIANO for wine. Peju, a California winery, later sought to register LIANA for wine; Cesari opposed, and a trademark office board found the marks likely to cause confusion and refused Peju's registration. Peju continued using LIANA anyway, and Cesari sued for infringement. The district court ruled Peju could not relitigate the confusion question and, after a trial, awarded Cesari profits and fees. The court of appeals vacated that judgment, holding the trademark office's earlier decision did not resolve the same issue as the infringement case, and sent the case back.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a federal appeals court may deny carry-over effect to a trademark office ruling by comparing how the agency and the courts weigh different factors, when the Supreme Court has said both apply the same basic standard for likely confusion. It also asks whether a product use that is narrower than what a trademark application broadly covers can defeat treating the issue as already decided, when the governing rule says uses that fall within the application's scope should count as the same issue.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in October 2026, and Peju waived its right to respond. The case was distributed for the Court's December 4, 2026 conference, where the justices will decide whether to take it.

## What each outcome would mean

Cesari, which won in the trial court, is asking the Court to take the case and reverse the appeals court's decision. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the trademark office ruling should have blocked Peju from relitigating the confusion issue; a grant alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the appeals court's decision vacating Cesari's judgment stays in place, and the case would continue in the lower courts without the earlier trademark office ruling automatically deciding the confusion question.
