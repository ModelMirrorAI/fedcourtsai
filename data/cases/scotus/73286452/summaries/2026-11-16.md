## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a doctor's challenge to a California law requiring medical education courses to cover implicit bias, leaving a lower-court ruling against her in place.

## What happened

Dr. Azadeh Khatibi, an ophthalmologist who teaches continuing medical education (CME) courses, and Do No Harm, a nonprofit of healthcare professionals, sued officials of the Medical Board of California. They argued that a state law requiring all CME courses to address "implicit bias" forces private instructors to convey a message they disagree with, violating the First Amendment. A federal district court dismissed the suit, and the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, ruling that accredited CME courses count as "government speech" because California extensively shapes and controls their content, so the First Amendment's free-speech protections do not apply. The full Ninth Circuit declined to rehear the case, over two dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether instruction that private teachers give in courses required for a professional license should be treated as the government's own speech, immune from free-speech review, or as private speech that the government cannot force instructors to deliver against their will.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for several conferences between September and November 2026. On November 16, 2026, the Court denied the petition, with one justice noting he would have granted it and two other justices dissenting from the denial.

## What each outcome would mean

Khatibi and Do No Harm, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up and reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which had sided with the California officials. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Ninth Circuit's decision in place: California's implicit-bias requirement for CME courses stands, and the lower court's holding that such courses are government speech, not subject to First Amendment scrutiny, continues to govern. Had the Court instead granted the petition, it would have heard full arguments and decided the underlying free-speech question itself, without resolving who would ultimately win. The denial itself decides nothing about the merits; it simply leaves the Ninth Circuit's judgment as the final word in this case.
