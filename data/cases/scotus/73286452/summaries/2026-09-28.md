## In brief

A doctor and a physician group ask the Court to decide whether California can require privately taught medical education courses to cover implicit bias without violating free speech protections.

## What happened

Azadeh Khatibi, an ophthalmologist who teaches continuing medical education (CME) courses, and Do No Harm, a physicians' organization, sued California officials over a state law requiring CME courses to address implicit bias in healthcare. They argued this forces private instructors to convey a government message they disagree with. A federal district court dismissed the suit, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, ruling that California's CME program counts as "government speech" not subject to free-speech challenges, given the state's extensive control over course content and licensing. The full Ninth Circuit denied rehearing, over dissents from three judges.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether instruction that private teachers create and deliver, in courses required for a professional license, counts as the government's own speech rather than the instructors' private speech. If it counts as government speech, free-speech protections do not apply to what the mandate requires instructors to say.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for September 28, 2026, after full briefing including the state's opposition and the petitioners' reply. No order granting or denying the petition has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Khatibi and Do No Harm are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the CME mandate must satisfy free-speech scrutiny, with no outcome decided yet. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, leaving California's implicit-bias requirement in place and treating CME content, as regulated by the state, as government speech exempt from First Amendment challenge.
