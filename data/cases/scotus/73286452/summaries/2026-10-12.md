## In brief

A California physician and a medical nonprofit ask the Court to revive their challenge to a state rule requiring continuing medical education courses to cover implicit bias.

## What happened

Azadeh Khatibi, an ophthalmologist who teaches continuing medical education (CME) courses, and Do No Harm, a nonprofit of healthcare professionals, sued officials of the Medical Board of California. They argued that a state law requiring all CME courses to address "implicit bias" forces private instructors to convey a message they disagree with, violating their free speech rights. A federal district court dismissed the suit, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that accredited CME courses count as "government speech" because California extensively shapes and controls their content, so the free speech protections the plaintiffs invoked do not apply. The full Ninth Circuit declined to rehear the case, over two dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether instruction that private teachers give in courses required for a professional license should be treated as the government's own speech, which would place it outside ordinary free-speech protections, or as private speech by the instructors themselves.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for the October 9, 2026 conference, after full briefing including a brief in opposition and a reply; the docket does not yet show a ruling on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Khatibi and Do No Harm, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the CME requirement counts as government speech; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's ruling that the CME content counts as government speech stands, leaving the Medical Board's implicit-bias requirement in place and the lawsuit over.
