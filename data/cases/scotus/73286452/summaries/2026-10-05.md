## In brief

A doctor and a physician group ask the Court to decide whether California can require private continuing-education courses to cover implicit bias without triggering First Amendment review.

## What happened

California requires physicians to complete continuing medical education (CME) and, since 2022, has required CME courses to address implicit bias in healthcare. Dr. Azadeh Khatibi and Do No Harm, Inc., who teach and support CME courses, sued California medical board officials, arguing the requirement unconstitutionally compels private speech. A federal district court dismissed the suit, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that California's CME program counts as the government's own speech, not private speech, because the state closely shapes and controls CME content. The full Ninth Circuit denied rehearing, over two dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether courses that private instructors create and teach, in order to satisfy a state licensing requirement, should be treated as the government's own speech, which would place them outside First Amendment protection, or as private speech that the government cannot force instructors to deliver.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference multiple times, most recently for October 9, 2026, after full briefing including an opposition brief and a reply; the docket does not yet show a decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Khatibi and Do No Harm, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the free-speech question itself, without presupposing which side wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling that California's CME program is government speech stands, leaving the state's implicit-bias requirement in place for CME instructors in that circuit; denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right, only that its decision remains undisturbed.
