## In brief

A California physician asks the Court to decide whether state-mandated continuing education content for doctors counts as government speech exempt from free speech protections.

## What happened

Azadeh Khatibi, an ophthalmologist who teaches continuing medical education (CME) courses, and Do No Harm, a physicians' organization, sued officials of the Medical Board of California. They challenged a state law requiring all CME courses to address "implicit bias," arguing this compels them to convey a message they disagree with, violating the First Amendment. The district court dismissed their claims, holding that CME courses approved for credit are government speech, not private speech subject to free speech protections. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and the full court denied rehearing en banc over two dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether instruction that private teachers give in courses required for a professional license should be treated as the government's own speech, which would place it outside ordinary free speech protections, or as private speech that the government may not freely compel or control.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for several conferences, most recently one scheduled after the snapshot date, following full briefing including a brief in opposition and a reply. The docket does not yet show any action granting or denying the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Khatibi and Do No Harm, as petitioners, are asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling in favor of the Medical Board. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments on whether CME instruction is government speech, deciding the question itself without presuming either side correct. If the Court denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the rule that California's CME content requirements count as government speech and are not subject to free speech challenge, a result favoring the Medical Board.
