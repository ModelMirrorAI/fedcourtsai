## In brief

A doctor and a physicians' group ask the Court to revive their First Amendment challenge to California's requirement that medical education courses discuss implicit bias.

## What happened

Dr. Azadeh Khatibi teaches continuing medical education (CME) courses in California, which physicians must complete to keep their licenses. A 2019 state law requires CME courses to address "implicit bias," content Khatibi and the group Do No Harm say is irrelevant and controversial and that they do not wish to teach. They sued, claiming the requirement unconstitutionally compels their speech. The district court dismissed the suit, holding that accredited CME content counts as government speech, not private speech, and so is not subject to free-speech scrutiny. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and rehearing en banc was denied over two dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether instruction that private teachers create and deliver, in courses the state requires for professional licensing, counts as the government's own speech or as private speech the government cannot control. How that question is answered decides whether the instructors' free-speech claim can even be considered, or whether the requirement is automatically immune from First Amendment review.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference multiple times, most recently for October 16, 2026, after full briefing including an opposition brief and a reply; the docket does not show that the Court has yet acted on it.

## What each outcome would mean

Khatibi and Do No Harm, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case and decide the government-speech question itself, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling that California's CME content counts as government speech stays in place, leaving the implicit-bias requirement in effect and the doctors' compelled-speech lawsuit dismissed. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with that ruling, only that it stands.
