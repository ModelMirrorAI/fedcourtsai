## In brief

United Airlines asks the Court to overturn a class certification allowing thousands of employees' religious-accommodation claims over its COVID-19 vaccine mandate to proceed together as one lawsuit.

## What happened

United required U.S. employees to get vaccinated against COVID-19 but let them seek religious or medical exemptions. Customer-facing employees who received religious accommodations, mostly pilots and flight attendants, were placed on indefinite unpaid leave rather than given another way to keep working and getting paid. Employees sued under Title VII, the federal law barring religious discrimination at work, saying this was not a reasonable accommodation. A federal district court certified a class of these employees to sue together, and the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that decision, though one judge wrote separately expressing reservations. United then asked the full Fifth Circuit to rehear the case, which was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a court may let employees sue as a group when their claims rest on evidence a jury might accept for the group as a whole, even though a jury could instead reject that evidence for some individual employees and accept it for others, producing different outcomes for different people rather than one answer for everyone.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply from United and amicus briefs supporting United, and was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026. The docket does not yet show any action taken at or after that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

United, which lost before the Fifth Circuit, is asking the Court to step in. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide whether the class was properly allowed to proceed, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If the Court denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, and the employees' case continues as a class action in the lower courts. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the class was properly certified, only that it leaves that decision in place.
