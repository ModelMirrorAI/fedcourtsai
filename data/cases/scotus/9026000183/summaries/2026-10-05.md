## In brief

United Airlines asked the Court to review a class certified on behalf of employees who were denied vaccine-mandate religious accommodations; the Court declined to hear the case.

## What happened

United Airlines placed customer-facing employees who received religious accommodations to its COVID-19 vaccine mandate on indefinite unpaid leave. A group of those employees sued, arguing this failed to reasonably accommodate their religious beliefs under Title VII. A federal district court certified a class of these employees, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed, finding that questions about the sincerity of employees' beliefs, the reasonableness of the accommodation, and whether alternatives would have burdened United could be resolved using evidence common to the class. United asked for rehearing before the full Fifth Circuit, which was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a group of employees can be treated as a single class when a jury might ultimately decide some of their claims differently than others, or whether class treatment requires that the evidence lead to the same answer for every member at once.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, and on October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition. Justice Kavanaugh noted he would have granted it.

## What each outcome would mean

United Airlines, which lost in the lower courts, asked the Court to take up the case. The denial leaves the Fifth Circuit's decision in place, meaning the class of employees Kincannon and the other plaintiffs represent remains certified, and the case returns to the district court to proceed on that basis. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fifth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed. Had the Court granted the petition, it would have heard full arguments and decided the class-certification question itself.
