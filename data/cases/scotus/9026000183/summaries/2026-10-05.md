## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review United Airlines' challenge to a class action by employees denied religious exemptions from its COVID-19 vaccine mandate.

## What happened

Employees sued United Airlines, claiming it failed to reasonably accommodate their religious objections to its vaccine mandate by placing them on indefinite unpaid leave. A federal district court certified a class of customer-facing employees who received religious accommodations but were put on unpaid leave. The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that certification, finding the district court had not abused its discretion, though one judge wrote separately expressing reservations about whether some issues were truly common to the whole class. United then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

United asked the Court to decide how strictly courts must apply the rule that a class action can only go forward if the key questions can be answered the same way for everyone in the class at once. United argued the lower court wrongly allowed the case forward even though a jury might answer those questions differently for different employees. The employees countered that the lower courts correctly found the evidence about the company's uniform policy common enough, and that this was a poor case for the Court to take up.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026. Justice Kavanaugh noted he would have granted it.

## What each outcome would mean

United Airlines, which lost below and asked the Court to step in, sought to overturn the class certification. By denying the petition, the Court left the Fifth Circuit's decision in place, meaning the class action approved by the lower courts can proceed. This denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling standing. The employees, known as the Kincannon group, can continue pursuing their claims as a certified class rather than through individual lawsuits.
