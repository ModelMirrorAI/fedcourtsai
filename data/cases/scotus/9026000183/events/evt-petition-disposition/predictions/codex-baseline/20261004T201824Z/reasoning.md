## Why 12%, rather than the 5% anchor

**Upward evidence.** The petition presents a developed federal procedural issue with consequences beyond these employees. Its strongest point is the distinction between genuinely common proof and merely similar types of individualized evidence: class members' separate attestations and conduct may support different sincerity findings. The petition invokes Wal-Mart and Amgen, cites other circuits' formulations, and emphasizes Judge Willett's reservations about the majority's sincerity analysis and trial management (petition, printed pp. 7–17). The proposed sequencing of sincerity, liability, and damages makes the issue concrete. Two industry amicus filings support an inference of broader interest, although their actual arguments remain unread. Together these justify more than a generic private-petitioner prior.

**Why denial remains much likelier.** The opposition gives a substantial answer to the asserted split: these employees challenge a uniform unpaid-leave practice after United applied a common accommodation process, whereas several cited comparators involved different practices or different alleged defects (opposition, printed pp. 1–3, 16–23). That characterization is advocacy, not an independently established factual finding, but it prevents treating the petition's split label as conclusive. Both briefs acknowledge that Judge Willett concurred in the judgment. His reservations are an attention signal, not a dissent calling for a different outcome.

The opposition also identifies other potentially common questions—reasonableness of the accommodation and undue hardship—that could sustain certification even if sincerity is individualized (printed pp. 36–37). The interlocutory posture matters: the trial-management problems emphasized by United may not materialize as alleged, and the district court can modify certification as proceedings develop (opposition, printed pp. 34–36). This is a vehicle discount, not a categorical assertion that Rule 23(f) cases cannot receive review.

As a limited independent precedent check, I consulted the majority's predominance discussion in **Tyson Foods, Inc. v. Bouaphakeo, 577 U.S. 442, 453 (2016)**, through CourtListener opinion 3187537. It recognizes that predominating common issues can coexist with some separately tried damages or defenses. That does not decide whether this class meets the standard, but it supports treating respondents' answer as doctrinally substantial rather than assuming that any individualized question establishes a clear conflict. The CourtListener citation lookup located Wal-Mart and Tyson Foods; its item-level result did not locate the supplied Halliburton citation, so I do not represent that citation as independently verified.

My **12%** is a judgmental update, not a fitted estimate. One coherent allocation is roughly 9% plenary grants and 3% summary grant-family dispositions, with the remaining 88% overwhelmingly denial and a small possibility of other nongrant exits. The split's strength, the unreviewed reply, and any future developments concerning Detwiler are the largest uncertainties. I did not retrieve Detwiler's current posture; both sides' discussion is the sole basis for retaining that contingent hold/GVR route.
