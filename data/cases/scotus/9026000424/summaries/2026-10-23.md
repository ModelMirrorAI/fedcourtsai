## In brief

Beckie Boddie asks the Court to review federal courts' handling of her property claims in a long-running FTC fraud case where she was never a named party.

## What happened

The FTC sued several defendants over alleged fraud involving real estate sold as "Sanctuary Belize" and "Kanantik." A receiver was appointed and a court-approved redress plan governed how affected property owners could recover. Boddie, as trustee for a family trust that owned a lot at these properties but was never named as a defendant in the FTC's suit, repeatedly tried to raise objections in the district court. The district court struck or returned her filings, ruling she was not a party and could not seek relief in the case. The district court later approved a sale of the properties to a third party. The Fourth Circuit affirmed the denial of her request to intervene and dismissed the rest of her appeal, holding she lacked standing to appeal as a nonparty who had not actively participated below.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it violates constitutional due process rights for a federal court to make decisions affecting a nonparty's real property without giving that person notice or a chance to be heard, and then bar that person from appealing because they were not a party. The Court is also asked whether the law letting the FTC seek injunctions in enforcement actions also allows a court to issue a redress order that changes a nonparty's private contracts and disposes of their property.

## Where it stands

## What each outcome would mean

Boddie is asking the Court to take up her case and reverse the Fourth Circuit's dismissal. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the lower courts' handling of her claims violated due process or exceeded the FTC's statutory authority. If the Court denies the petition, the Fourth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the dismissal of her appeal and the property sale and redress orders she challenged.
