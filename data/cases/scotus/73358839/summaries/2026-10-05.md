## In brief

The Court declined to hear a challenge by Texas Top Cop Shop and other businesses to a federal law requiring companies to report their owners' identities to the government.

## What happened

Congress passed the Corporate Transparency Act, requiring most corporations and similar entities to report information about their owners to a federal agency, with civil and criminal penalties for noncompliance. Texas Top Cop Shop and several other businesses and organizations sued, arguing the law was unconstitutional. A federal district court agreed and blocked the law, but the Supreme Court later stayed that injunction while the case continued. The federal appeals court put the case on hold pending further agency rulemaking. Meanwhile, a different appeals court, the Eleventh Circuit, upheld the same law in a separate case. Texas Top Cop Shop asked the Supreme Court to take its case directly, before the appeals court ruled, to resolve the disagreement between the courts.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether Congress had the constitutional power to require companies to report ownership information simply because they exist as legal entities, even without any business activity, and whether demanding this private information without individual suspicion or a warrant violates constitutional protections against unreasonable searches.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court denied it, leaving the matter undecided at the Supreme Court level.

## What each outcome would mean

Texas Top Cop Shop and the other businesses asked the Court to take up their case. Because the Court denied the petition, it will not review the constitutional questions now, and the lower courts' handling of the dispute continues without Supreme Court intervention. The denial does not mean the law is constitutional; it simply leaves the case as it stood, with the appeal still on hold at the Fifth Circuit and the law's requirements affected by ongoing federal rulemaking.
