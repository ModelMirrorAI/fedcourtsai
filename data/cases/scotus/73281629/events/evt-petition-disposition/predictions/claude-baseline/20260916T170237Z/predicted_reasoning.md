# Forecast: what the Court will do with No. 25-1142, Zavislak v. Netflix

**Disposition.** The petition will most likely be **denied**, with a probability of grant of about 14%. The Court will consider it at the September 28, 2026 long conference, where it sits after a call for a response and a full round of briefing (petition, BIO, reply/supplemental brief).

**Relists.** The docket shows **two distribution entries** (April 15 for the May 1 conference, superseded when the Court called for a response on April 24; and July 15 for the September 28 conference). The petition has therefore **never actually been considered at a conference** — the second distribution is a re-set after the BIO, not a relist in the substantive sense. From this state I expect roughly a 30% chance of at least one further distribution. The paths that produce one are: a grant (the Court now nearly always relists once before granting), a CVSG (the petition is redistributed once the Solicitor General files), or a relist followed by denial (a closer look at the Kelly development, or a Justice considering a separate writing). The modal path, about 70%, is a straight denial on the October 5 order list with no further distribution.

**CVSG.** No CVSG is on the docket. I put about 12% on one issuing. The question is the scope of a disclosure duty in Title I of ERISA that the Department of Labor administers, and the Court often wants the Solicitor General's view before taking an ERISA statutory question with a claimed split. Against that, the question is narrow, no federal program or agency rule is implicated, and the Court called for a response rather than for the government's views when it first flagged the petition. If a CVSG issues, it would come off the September 28 conference or after one relist, with the SG brief arriving in the spring of 2027 and disposition late in OT2026.

**Which question, if granted.** The Court would take the question presented essentially as written — whether a contract governing the administration of benefit claims is a "contract ... under which the plan is established or operated" under 29 U.S.C. § 1024(b)(4) — possibly tightening it to the "claims administration agreements" actually at issue. It would not take up the petition's broader "information test versus governing-documents test" framing as a separate question.

**Summary disposition.** Very unlikely, about 5% conditional on a grant. No intervening decision of this Court bears on the question, so there is nothing to GVR in light of; Kelly v. Altria (4th Cir. Aug. 10, 2026) is a circuit decision and cannot ground a GVR. A per curiam reversal of an en banc-rooted Ninth Circuit rule on a question this Court has never addressed is not the Court's practice. A grant would mean plenary review, argued in the spring of 2027 or in OT2027.

**Dissent from denial.** Unlikely, about 5% conditional on denial. The issue has no natural champion on the Court, the case is low-profile, and the Ninth Circuit's disposition is unpublished and framed as factbound, which gives a Justice inclined to write little to write against. A statement respecting denial noting the circuit disagreement and inviting a cleaner vehicle is the most plausible form, and I do not expect it.

**If granted (conditional).** The Court would most likely reverse or vacate. The textual reading now adopted by the Fourth, Seventh and Tenth Circuits — a contract that governs some part of the plan's operating process is disclosable whether or not it describes participant benefits — fits the statutory words better than the Ninth Circuit's Hughes gloss, and the Ninth Circuit panel itself described the agreements as governing the claims-related relationship between Netflix and its administrators, which is the very feature that makes them disclosable under the majority view. That is a forecast about the merits only if review is granted; a denial produces no opinion.
