## What happened

Youth 71Five Ministries, a Christian nonprofit serving at-risk youth, had received grants for years from an Oregon Department of Education youth program. Oregon later added a rule requiring grantees to certify they do not discriminate in employment based on religion, among other categories. Because 71Five hires only staff who share its Christian faith, Oregon withdrew its conditional grant award. 71Five sued Oregon officials, arguing this violated its First Amendment rights. A district court denied a preliminary injunction and dismissed the case. On appeal, a Ninth Circuit panel found the rule neutral and generally applicable, rejected 71Five's claim that it could assert "religious autonomy" as a standalone claim, but ruled for 71Five on a separate expressive-association theory as to non-grant-funded activities.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two issues. First, whether a religious organization may bring a lawsuit affirmatively claiming interference with its internal religious autonomy, or whether that principle can only be raised as a defense once already sued, as the appeals court held. Second, whether a state violates religious freedom protections by requiring a religious organization, as a condition of getting public grant money, to give up its practice of hiring only people who share its faith. Oregon responds that the first question was not properly developed below and the second is moot because the appeals court already ruled partly in the ministry's favor and the challenged rule has since been repealed.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for April 17, 2026, after full briefing including the respondents' opposition and the petitioner's reply. The Court has not yet acted on whether to grant review.
