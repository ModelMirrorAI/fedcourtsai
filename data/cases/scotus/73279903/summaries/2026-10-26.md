## In brief

A Christian youth ministry asks the Court to revive its claim that Oregon illegally stripped its grant funding over faith-based hiring practices.

## What happened

Youth 71Five Ministries, a Christian nonprofit serving at-risk youth, had received Oregon grant funding for years. When Oregon added a rule barring grantees from discriminating in employment based on religion, among other traits, it withdrew 71Five's grant after learning the ministry requires staff to affirm a Christian statement of faith. 71Five sued, claiming violations of its free exercise, religious autonomy, and expressive association rights. The district court denied preliminary relief and dismissed the case. The Ninth Circuit affirmed denial of relief on the free-exercise and religious-autonomy claims, but ruled for 71Five on its expressive-association claim, ordering a narrower injunction protecting hiring outside grant-funded activities.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions. First, whether a religious organization may sue state officials directly, under a federal civil-rights law, claiming the government violated its right to control its own religious affairs, or whether that right can only be raised as a defense once already sued. Second, whether a state violates religious freedom protections by requiring a religious organization to give up its right to hire only people who share its faith as a condition of receiving public grant money.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after briefing extensions and a round of amicus filings, most recently for the April 17, 2026 conference. The docket shows no record yet of the Court's action on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Youth 71Five Ministries, which lost on two of its three claims below, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the legal questions; granting does not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place its rulings against 71Five on the free-exercise and religious-autonomy claims, while 71Five's narrower injunction on expressive-association grounds remains intact and the case continues in the lower courts.
