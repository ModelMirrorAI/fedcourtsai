## In brief

A Christian youth ministry asks the Court to review a ruling that limited how it could challenge Oregon's denial of grant funding over its religious hiring practices.

## What happened

Youth 71Five Ministries, a Christian nonprofit serving at-risk youth, had long received Oregon grant funding. When Oregon added a rule barring grantees from religious discrimination in hiring, and 71Five requires staff to affirm a Christian faith statement, Oregon withdrew its grant award. 71Five sued Oregon officials, seeking to reinstate the funding and protect its hiring practices. The district court denied a preliminary injunction and dismissed the case. A Ninth Circuit panel affirmed denial of the injunction on 71Five's free-exercise and religious-autonomy claims, but ordered a narrower injunction protecting 71Five's hiring for non-grant-funded activities, based on a separate expressive-association claim.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions: first, whether a religious organization can sue government officials directly over interference with its internal governance, or whether that protection can only be raised defensively after being sued by someone else. Second, whether a state violates religious freedom protections by requiring a religious organization to give up its practice of hiring only people who share its faith as a condition of receiving public grant money.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for April 17, 2026, after Oregon filed its brief opposing review and 71Five filed a reply. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Youth 71Five, which lost on the claims at issue below, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands: 71Five's broader religious-autonomy and free-exercise claims fail, though it keeps the narrower injunction protecting its hiring for activities that receive no state grant money. Oregon's brief notes the challenged rule has since been repealed.
