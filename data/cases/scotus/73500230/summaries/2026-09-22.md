## What happened

Wealthy, Inc. and Dale Buczkowski sued Spencer Cornelia and others over YouTube videos that Wealthy says made false and defamatory claims, including about lying, money laundering, drug offenses, and involvement in a death. A federal district court in Nevada ruled for Cornelia, finding Wealthy and Buczkowski were "limited-purpose public figures" who could not show the statements were made with actual malice, and separately rejected Cornelia's request to dismiss the case early under Nevada's anti-SLAPP law (a statute letting defendants get speech-related claims tossed quickly). On appeal, the Ninth Circuit kept the public-figure ruling but reversed course and revived the anti-SLAPP motion, which could lead to a large fee award against Wealthy. Wealthy asked the full Ninth Circuit to reconsider; that request was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things. First, when a state law lets defendants get early dismissal of speech-related lawsuits and forces the losing plaintiff to pay fees, does that state law apply in federal court at all, and if so, in what form — as written, not at all, or in some modified version blending state and federal procedures? Federal appeals courts currently disagree on this. Second, did the appeals court wrongly label Wealthy and Buczkowski as limited-purpose public figures — a status that raises the bar for proving defamation — without properly finding that they had voluntarily entered a specific public debate connected to the disputed statements.

## Where it stands

The case has been distributed for the Court's conference twice, and the Court asked the other side to respond, which it did; Wealthy then filed a reply. The petition is awaiting the Court's decision on whether to hear the case.
