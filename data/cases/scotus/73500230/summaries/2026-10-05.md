## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a dispute over how state anti-SLAPP laws and public-figure status apply in a defamation and false-advertising case between Wealthy, Inc. and Spencer Cornelia.

## What happened

Wealthy, Inc. and Dale Buczkowski sued Spencer Cornelia and others over YouTube videos alleging Buczkowski lied about his education, laundered money, and was involved in other misconduct. A federal district court granted summary judgment against Wealthy, finding Buczkowski a "limited-purpose public figure" who had not shown actual malice, and separately denied Cornelia's motion to dismiss under Nevada's anti-SLAPP law. On appeal, the Ninth Circuit affirmed the summary judgment but reversed course on the anti-SLAPP motion, reviving it and triggering a mandatory fee award against Wealthy. Wealthy asked the full Ninth Circuit to reconsider; that request was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

Wealthy asked the Court to decide two things: first, whether state laws that let defendants seek early dismissal of speech-related lawsuits, with special burdens on the plaintiff and fee awards to the winning defendant, can be used in federal court at all, and if so, in what form, given that federal appeals courts have split into three different approaches. Second, whether the appeals court wrongly labeled Wealthy a public figure for defamation purposes without properly examining whether it had voluntarily entered a public controversy connected to the specific accusations made against it.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, the Court asked Cornelia's side to respond, and the case was distributed again after briefing was complete. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Wealthy, Inc. asked the Court to take up the case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Ninth Circuit's decision in place: the summary judgment against Wealthy on its claims stands, Cornelia's anti-SLAPP motion remains revived, and the mandatory fee award against Wealthy proceeds. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning on either the anti-SLAPP question or the public-figure determination; it simply leaves those rulings undisturbed without deciding the legal questions Wealthy raised.
