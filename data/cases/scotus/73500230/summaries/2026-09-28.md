## In brief

Wealthy, Inc. asks the Court to resolve how state anti-SLAPP laws apply in federal defamation suits and to review its public-figure ruling against it.

## What happened

Wealthy, Inc. and Dale Buczkowski sued Spencer Cornelia and others over YouTube videos alleging Buczkowski lied about his education and was involved in crimes, bringing defamation and false-advertising claims. A federal district court in Nevada granted summary judgment against them, partly on the ground that they were "limited-purpose public figures," and denied Cornelia's motion to dismiss under Nevada's anti-SLAPP law (a law letting defendants quickly end lawsuits targeting protected speech). The Ninth Circuit affirmed the summary judgment but reversed course on the anti-SLAPP motion, granting it and triggering a mandatory fee award against Wealthy.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions. First, whether federal court procedural rules override state anti-SLAPP laws entirely, whether those state laws should apply exactly as written, or whether some blended approach — like the one the appeals court used here — is correct; federal appeals courts currently disagree on this. Second, whether the appeals court wrongly labeled Wealthy and Buczkowski as limited-purpose public figures, a status that makes defamation claims harder to win, without properly finding they had voluntarily entered a public controversy connected to the specific accusations against them.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026, after the Court requested a response from Cornelia and received Wealthy's reply. No decision on whether to hear the case has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Wealthy and Buczkowski are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide these questions itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands: Cornelia's anti-SLAPP motion succeeds, the mandatory fee award proceeds, and the public-figure designation against Wealthy and Buczkowski remains in place. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning, only that its decision continues to govern this case.
