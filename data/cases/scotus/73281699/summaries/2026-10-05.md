## In brief

The Court declined to hear a former Pennsylvania judge's challenge to his suspension over partisan Facebook posts made while on the bench.

## What happened

Mark B. Cohen, a former Philadelphia judge, was disciplined by Pennsylvania's Court of Judicial Discipline for Facebook posts that promoted Democratic politicians and causes while identifying him as a sitting judge. The Court of Judicial Discipline suspended him without pay for the remainder of his term, and he lost his pension as a result. Cohen argued the sanctions violated his First Amendment rights. The Supreme Court of Pennsylvania unanimously upheld the discipline, ruling that a more flexible balancing test for public employee speech, rather than the toughest level of judicial review, applied to restrictions on a sitting judge's speech, and that Cohen's conduct failed even under the tougher standard.

## What the Court is being asked

Cohen's petition asked the Court to decide whether restrictions on what sitting judges may say must meet the toughest standard of judicial review, the one used for direct limits on speech content, rather than the more flexible balancing test courts use for public employees generally. He argued that lower courts are split on this question. The Judicial Conduct Board, defending the discipline, answered that there is no real conflict among the courts and that this case would be a poor vehicle for deciding the issue even if the split were real.

## Where it stands

The petition went through more than one conference, with responses requested and an extension granted along the way, before the Court denied it.

## What each outcome would mean

Cohen, as the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's ruling in place, meaning Cohen's suspension and the loss of his pension stand, and the Judicial Conduct Board's position prevails. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Pennsylvania court's reasoning or its choice of legal standard; it simply leaves that decision as the final word in this case, without resolving the broader question Cohen raised about how courts should review restrictions on judges' speech.
