## In brief

A former Pennsylvania judge asks the Court to review whether stricter First Amendment rules should have applied to his suspension for partisan Facebook posts.

## What happened

Mark Cohen, a former state legislator, was elected to the Philadelphia Court of Common Pleas in 2018 and kept posting on a personal Facebook page that identified him as a judge. Pennsylvania's Judicial Conduct Board charged him with violating several rules of judicial conduct based on posts it said showed partisan political activity. The Court of Judicial Discipline found violations and suspended him without pay for his final three months in office before mandatory retirement. The Pennsylvania Supreme Court unanimously affirmed, holding that a balancing test used for public employees' speech, rather than the toughest level of First Amendment review, applied to a sitting judge's off-the-bench speech.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide what level of First Amendment protection applies when a state disciplines a sitting judge for speech outside an election campaign — specifically, whether such restrictions must meet the toughest standard of judicial review, which requires the government to show a compelling reason and a narrowly tailored rule, or whether a more flexible balancing test, weighing the judge's speech interest against the government's interest in efficient and impartial courts, is enough.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, and the Court has requested and received a response from the Judicial Conduct Board along with Cohen's reply. The docket shows no action yet on the petition itself.

## What each outcome would mean

Cohen, who lost below, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full briefing and argument and decide which First Amendment standard applies, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's decision upholding Cohen's suspension stands, and the case ends there. Either action concerns only whether the Court will hear the case, not how the underlying dispute should come out.
