## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review a ruling that a federal prosecutor's appointment, and the grand jury subpoenas he obtained, were unlawful.

## What happened

After the Senate-confirmed U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of New York left office, the Justice Department eventually named John Sarcone III as First Assistant U.S. Attorney and also gave him broad delegated authority, after which he began acting as the office's head. The New York Attorney General's Office, served with grand jury subpoenas Sarcone obtained in an investigation touching its enforcement cases, argued he had no lawful authority to seek them. A district court agreed, quashed the subpoenas, and barred Sarcone from further involvement in the investigations. A divided Second Circuit panel affirmed, holding Sarcone could not become acting officer because he was not already First Assistant when the vacancy arose, and that delegating him all the office's duties improperly circumvented federal vacancy law.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions about a federal law governing temporary leadership of vacant federal offices. First, whether someone appointed to a deputy role only after the top position becomes vacant can still automatically step into the vacant role under the law's default succession rule. Second, whether a separate provision barring agencies from using general delegation powers to install an unofficial substitute prevents an agency head from delegating essentially all the vacant office's powers to one person who is not formally serving in an acting capacity.

## Where it stands

The government's petition was filed and a response was originally due in mid-October 2026; the Court granted the New York Attorney General's Office more time, extending its response deadline to November 2, 2026. The government's own petition asks the Court to hold this case pending its decision on a similar petition in another case rather than act on it now.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, which lost below, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions, which would not by itself determine who wins. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving in place the decision that Sarcone's appointment and the resulting subpoenas were invalid, a result favoring the New York Attorney General's Office. The government has also suggested the Court could simply hold the petition until it resolves a related case before deciding what to do with this one.
