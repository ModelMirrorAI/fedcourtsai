## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review a ruling that its acting U.S. Attorney lacked authority to obtain subpoenas against New York's Attorney General's office.

## What happened

After the Senate-confirmed U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of New York resigned and a temporary successor's term expired, the Justice Department named John Sarcone III as First Assistant U.S. Attorney, after which he began serving as Acting U.S. Attorney and, separately, received a broad delegation of the office's duties. In that role he obtained grand jury subpoenas against the New York Attorney General's office over its lawsuits against the National Rifle Association and Donald Trump. New York's office challenged the subpoenas, arguing Sarcone's appointment was unlawful. A district court quashed the subpoenas and barred Sarcone from the investigations; a divided Second Circuit panel affirmed, holding a law called the Federal Vacancies Reform Act did not allow Sarcone to become acting officer or receive such a sweeping delegation of duties.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a person who is named a federal agency's "first assistant" only after the top position becomes vacant can still automatically take over as the acting head of that office, and whether a law governing temporary officials bars giving someone who is not validly an acting official essentially all the duties of the vacant position through a separate delegation of authority.

## Where it stands

The government filed its petition on September 15, 2026, and asked the Court to hold it pending a decision on a related petition in another case, United States v. Jackson, rather than to grant or deny it now. The response deadline was extended, at New York's request, to November 2, 2026, and the Court has not yet acted on the petition itself.

## What each outcome would mean

The United States, which lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide the legal questions, which would resolve nothing about who wins until a later ruling. If it denies the petition, or holds it pending Jackson and later disposes of it without granting review, the Second Circuit's ruling against the government stands, meaning Sarcone's appointment and the resulting subpoenas remain invalid and New York's position prevails.
