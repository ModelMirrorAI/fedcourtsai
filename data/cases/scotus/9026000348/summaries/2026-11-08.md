## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review a ruling that an acting U.S. Attorney was unlawfully appointed and improperly obtained grand jury subpoenas against New York's Attorney General's office.

## What happened

After the Senate-confirmed U.S. Attorney for the Northern District of New York left office, the Attorney General eventually named John Sarcone III as First Assistant, asserting he automatically became Acting U.S. Attorney, and separately designated him a "Special Attorney" with all the powers of a U.S. Attorney. Sarcone then obtained grand jury subpoenas against the Office of the New York State Attorney General, seeking records about its past enforcement cases against the National Rifle Association and Donald Trump. New York's Attorney General's office moved to quash the subpoenas, arguing Sarcone lacked lawful authority. The district court agreed, quashed the subpoenas, and disqualified Sarcone from the investigations; the Second Circuit affirmed, over one judge's dissent.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a person named "first assistant" only after a vacancy begins can still automatically become the acting head of the office under the federal vacancies law, and whether a rule making that vacancies law the only way to install an acting official blocks an agency head from separately delegating all of the office's powers to someone who was never validly made the acting officer.

## Where it stands

The government filed its petition on September 15, 2026, and after an extension, New York's Attorney General's office filed its brief opposing review on November 2, 2026. The brief in opposition says the Court should deny review, or if it grants review, should also take up a related case called Jackson. The docket does not yet show further action on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, which lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide the legal questions, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands: Sarcone's appointment and the delegation to him were invalid, the subpoenas against New York's Attorney General's office remain quashed, and Sarcone stays disqualified from the investigations.
