## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review a ruling that a New York federal prosecutor was serving unlawfully when he obtained grand jury subpoenas.

## What happened

A U.S. Attorney position in New York's Northern District became vacant, and after a series of personnel moves, John Sarcone III began serving as Acting U.S. Attorney and, in that role, obtained grand jury subpoenas directed at the New York Attorney General's office in connection with its lawsuits against the National Rifle Association and Donald Trump. New York's Attorney General's office asked a court to quash the subpoenas, arguing Sarcone had no lawful authority to seek them. A district court agreed and barred Sarcone from further involvement in the investigations. The Second Circuit affirmed, over one judge's dissent, joining other appeals courts that had reached similar conclusions in other cases.

## What the Court is being asked

The case involves a federal law governing who may temporarily fill a vacant government post requiring Senate confirmation. The Court is asked whether someone who is named first assistant to an office only after that office becomes vacant can still automatically become the acting head of that office under the law's default rule, and separately, whether a different provision of that law stops an agency head from handing all of a vacant office's duties to someone through an ordinary internal delegation, when that person is not serving as the lawful acting officer.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed on September 15, 2026, with the respondent's response due October 15, 2026. The government's petition itself asks the Court to hold this case pending its decision on a related petition in another case, United States v. Jackson, rather than to decide it now.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Second Circuit's ruling against it. If the Court grants review and eventually rules for the government, that would validate the use of the disputed appointment and delegation practices, reviving the subpoenas and Sarcone's role. If it rules for the New York Attorney General's office, the appeals court's decision would stand, meaning Sarcone lacked authority. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's decision quashing the subpoenas and barring Sarcone from the investigations remains in effect.
