# Predicted reasoning — what the Court will do with No. 25-1231

**Disposition: denied**, most likely by an unadorned order after the October 16, 2026 conference or the one after it.

**Relists.** The docket shows two distributions: the first (for the June 25, 2026 conference) ended in a call for a response after both respondents had waived, and the second (for October 16, 2026) is the first conference at which the Court will see the petition fully briefed. I expect no further distribution in most worlds. A single relist is the live alternative, for one of two reasons: a chamber that prompted the call for response wanting a second look, or a Justice preparing a short statement respecting denial. More than one further relist is unlikely.

**CVSG.** None. The dispute is between private plaintiffs and a State over a state statute and a state institution; no federal program, statute, or federal-government interest is implicated, so the Solicitor General's views will not be invited.

**Which question, if any.** If the Court were to grant, it would take question 1 (whether the 1878 Act created contractual obligations enforceable under the Contract Clause, and the reach of the reserved-powers doctrine), most likely reformulated to isolate the reserved-powers holding of the Court of Appeal and to set aside the damages theory the state courts treated as forfeited. Question 2, the Bill of Attainder Clause theory on behalf of a man dead since 1893 and his descendants, is novel and would not carry a grant on its own; if question 1 were granted the Court would probably leave question 2 behind.

**Route.** Plenary review is the only realistic grant route. There is no intervening decision of this Court bearing on either question, so a GVR is not on the table, and the questions are too novel and fact-bound for a per curiam summary reversal.

**Separate writing on denial.** Most likely none. There is a modest chance of a short statement or dissent respecting denial from a Justice interested in reviving Contract Clause scrutiny or in the Bill of Attainder Clause's separation-of-powers dimension, but the forfeiture findings below and the absence of any amicus support make this an unattractive vehicle for such a writing.

**Conditional merits note.** If granted, the likely ground would be that the reserved-powers doctrine does not categorically bar every claim arising from a legislature's conditional acceptance of a private donation, with the case remanded for a conventional Contract Clause analysis; the Court would be unlikely to hold that the State must restore the name.
