## Why 8%, rather than the 17.2% anchor

**There is a real reason for attention.** The petition's first question concerns an alleged exchange of $100,000 for perpetual naming and hereditary governance rights. It argues that the State may not keep the consideration while escaping its promises, and distinguishes compensatory damages from an injunction restricting sovereign action. Its discussion of Winstar, vested rights, and the statute's perpetuity language gives the Contract Clause issue substance beyond a mere dispute over institutional branding (petition pp. 14-23). The June 22 response request after initial waivers is affirmative evidence of interest. Those considerations keep my forecast materially above a routine low-information petition.

**But the vehicle presents several obstacles.** Both oppositions emphasize the absence of a developed conflict among appellate courts; the petition principally alleges conflict with this Court's own precedents and exceptional importance. The unusual statute establishing a public college makes it uncertain how broadly a ruling would control ordinary donor contracts. The College respondents distinguish public governance from contracts concerning facilities and defend the decision as narrow (College opposition pp. 11-14, 30-33). I do not simply adopt their claim that novelty defeats review, but I give the absence of demonstrated recurring disagreement meaningful weight.

The more promising damages argument faces an express waiver ruling. Petitioners acknowledge that ruling but challenge its adequacy, invoking their pleading, briefing, oral argument, and rehearing efforts (petition pp. 21-23). California explains why the argument should have been developed earlier and why a footnote, oral argument, or rehearing did not suffice (California opposition pp. 10-13). This is a substantial vehicle concern, not a finding here that the Court necessarily lacks jurisdiction. Contract formation is a separate complication: respondents point to the removal of express covenanting language from a draft founding bill, while petitioners rely on the enacted exchange and perpetuity language (College opposition pp. 20-22; petition pp. 19-20).

I checked the historical Newton opinion through CourtListener, opinion 90058, specifically its discussion of public subjects and legislative authority. That material supports treating the public/private distinction as an existing doctrinal obstacle rather than an obviously invented exception. It does not resolve this petition, whose factual and remedial distinctions still matter. The initial citation search returned an unrelated case; I discarded it and constrained the next search to Newton in the Supreme Court.

The second question adds constitutional interest but not a clearly better vehicle. Petitioners argue that historical attainders could reach deceased persons and their descendants, and that naming, governance, and reputational injuries count as punishment (petition pp. 23-28). Respondents contest punishment and emphasize the statute's nonpunitive institutional purposes; the College also raises standing and preservation concerns (California opposition pp. 16-19; College opposition pp. 23-29). I treat those as contested arguments, not established additional holdings. This fact-specific disagreement is less compelling for review than a demonstrated split on a recurring punishment rule.

Finally, two distribution entries are not two completed merits-like examinations of the petition. The June response request came before the first scheduled conference, and the September redistribution follows the completed opposition briefing. The pack expressly warns that distribution counts can overstate substantive relists. I retain the harness's elevated classification but discount the inference that this particular sequence reflects sustained grant-oriented deliberation. Together, these considerations support the judgmental adjustment to **P(any grant) = 0.08**, with denial the categorical forecast; the adjustment is not a fitted statistical model.

## Other probabilities and stakes

- **Another distribution: 0.25.** A requested response and a potentially interesting constitutional issue support further examination, but I expect the newly complete briefing usually to permit disposition without a long sequence.
- **New CVSG: 0.02.** There is no invitation yet, and no identified federal-program interest making one especially useful. This is not a claim that state-law disputes can never draw a CVSG.
- **Summary route given grant: 0.20.** A narrow correction remains possible, but no intervening-decision trigger is identified and the procedural and contractual disputes favor plenary treatment if granted.
- **Dissent or statement given denial: 0.08.** The historical constitutional questions could interest an individual Justice, but I have no case-specific basis to predict a writing or author.
- **Stakes: 0.46.** A merits ruling could affect public-donor arrangements and the constitutional limits of retrospective legislative condemnation. The public law school's identity adds cultural significance, while the singular founding statute, hereditary seat, and waiver issues limit breadth. This number measures significance if decided, not grant probability.

The principal uncertainty is whether one or more Justices view the damages issue as a sufficiently clear departure from precedent to overlook the vehicle complications. A missing reply could sharpen that argument. Generic web retrieval attempts returned no usable content, so no web-derived proposition entered this forecast; the provisioned filings, committed pack, and historical Newton excerpts carry the substantive assessment.
