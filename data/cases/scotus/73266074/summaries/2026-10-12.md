## In brief

Descendants of a law school's 1878 founder ask the Court to revive their challenge to a California law that renamed the school and removed their reserved board seat.

## What happened

In 1878, California authorized S.C. Hastings to found a public law school, naming it after him and reserving a board seat for his heirs, in exchange for his $100,000 payment to the state. In 2022, after findings that Hastings had financed violence against Native Americans, the state enacted a law renaming the school and removing the reserved seat. Hastings's descendants and an alumni group sued California and the school's leaders, claiming the law violated the Constitution's Contract Clause and its ban on bills of attainder (laws that punish specific people without a trial). California courts dismissed the suit, ruling the 1878 law was not a binding contract and that the renaming did not punish anyone. The state's highest court declined to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a 19th-century state law establishing a public college's name and board makeup, passed in exchange for a payment, created contract rights that a later legislature could not undo. It is also asked whether a law that renames a public institution and changes its board, based on findings about a long-dead founder's conduct, improperly punishes him or his descendants without a court trial.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply, and was distributed for the Court's conference on October 16, 2026. No order on the petition has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The descendants and alumni group, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the renaming law violated the Contract Clause or the ban on bills of attainder, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the California courts' decision rejecting the challenge stands, leaving the renaming and board changes in place; a denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right.
