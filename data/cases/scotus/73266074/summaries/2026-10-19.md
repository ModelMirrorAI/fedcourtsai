## In brief

The Court declined to hear descendants of a law school's founder challenge a state law renaming the school and ending their reserved board seat.

## What happened

In 1878, California passed a law letting S.C. Hastings found a public law school, naming it after him and reserving a board seat for his heirs. In 2022, after findings that Hastings had financed violence against Native Americans, the legislature passed a law renaming the school and eliminating the reserved seat. Hastings's descendants and an alumni group sued California and the school's leaders, arguing the change broke a contract and punished them without a trial. California courts ruled against them at every stage, holding the 1878 law was not a binding contract and that the renaming was not an unconstitutional punishment. The state supreme court denied review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state can be bound by an old law's promise about a public institution's name and governance, or whether the state's authority to manage its own institutions cannot be permanently signed away. The Court is also asked whether a law that reassesses a long-dead historical figure's conduct and removes benefits tied to his name amounts to punishing him and his descendants without a court trial.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court asked for a response from the respondents. After full briefing, including a reply from the petitioners, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The descendants and alumni group asked the Court to take up the case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the California Court of Appeal's ruling in place: the renaming and removal of the reserved board seat stand, and the descendants' claims for breach of contract and unconstitutional punishment remain rejected. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision as the final word in this case.
