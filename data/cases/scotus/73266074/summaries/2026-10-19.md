## In brief

The Court declined to hear descendants of a law school's 1878 founder challenge a 2022 law that renamed the school and ended their reserved board seat.

## What happened

In 1878, California passed a law letting S.C. Hastings found a public law school in exchange for $100,000, providing the school would be named for him and that one board seat would always go to his heir or representative. In 2022, after findings that Hastings had financed violence against Native Americans, the legislature renamed the school and eliminated the reserved board seat. Hastings's descendants and an alumni group sued, claiming the 1878 law was a binding contract the state could not break and that the new law amounted to unconstitutional punishment of Hastings and his family. California's trial court, appeals court, and supreme court all ruled against them, finding no enforceable contract and no punishment of anyone.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a 19th-century state law establishing a public college's name and board structure, passed in exchange for a donor's payment, created contract rights that a later legislature could not undo. It is also asked whether a law that renames a public institution and changes its board eligibility rules, based on findings about a long-deceased founder's conduct, improperly punishes that person and his descendants without a trial, which would violate the ban on legislative punishment aimed at specific people.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court denied it on October 19, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Hastings College Conservation Committee and the individual descendants, as petitioners, asked the Court to overturn the California courts' rulings in favor of the state and the college's leaders. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the California Court of Appeal's decision in place: the renaming and board changes stand, and the descendants' contract and constitutional claims remain rejected. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning, only that it will not review the case further.
