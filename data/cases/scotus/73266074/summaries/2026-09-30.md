## In brief

Descendants of a law college's founder ask the Court to revive their claims that California broke a 19th-century contract and unlawfully punished their ancestor by renaming the school.

## What happened

In 1878, California authorized Serranus Clinton Hastings to found a public law college after he paid the state $100,000, on terms saying the college would always bear his name and reserve his heirs a board seat. In 2022, the state passed a law finding that Hastings had financed violence against Native Americans, renamed the college, and removed his heirs' board seat. The Hastings College Conservation Committee and several descendants sued, arguing this violated the constitutional bar on states impairing contracts and the ban on legislatures punishing specific people without a trial. California courts dismissed the suit, and the California Supreme Court declined further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The descendants ask the Court to decide two things: whether the 1878 law created a binding contract that the state could not later break, even though it involved the college's name and governing structure, and whether a law that officially blames a named, deceased individual for past wrongdoing and takes away benefits from him and his heirs amounts to the kind of punishment-by-legislation that the Constitution forbids.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference, and the Court asked for and received responses from the state and college officials, followed by the descendants' reply. The docket shows no ruling yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The Hastings College Conservation Committee and the descendants are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide whether the 1878 law created enforceable contract rights and whether the 2022 law punished Hastings and his heirs unconstitutionally; a grant decides nothing by itself about who wins. If the Court denies review, the California Court of Appeal's decision upholding the dismissal of the descendants' lawsuit stands, leaving the college's new name and board structure in place without further review by the Supreme Court.
