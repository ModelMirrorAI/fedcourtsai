## What happened

In 1878, California passed a law letting Serranus Clinton Hastings found a public law college, on the condition he pay the state $100,000. The law said the college would forever bear his name and that one board seat would always go to his heirs. In 2022, California enacted a new law finding that Hastings had financed violence against Native Americans, renaming the college and eliminating the heirs' board seat. A group of alumni and Hastings descendants sued, arguing the change broke a binding agreement and unfairly punished Hastings and his family without a trial. California courts rejected the claims, ruling that the state could not permanently give up its power to run and name a public institution, and that the new law did not punish anyone. California's supreme court declined further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks the Court to decide two things. First, whether an old state law promising a specific name and board structure for a public college, adopted in return for a payment, legally binds the state the way an ordinary contract would, so that changing it later could be an unconstitutional interference with contracts. Second, whether a legislature naming someone as having committed wrongdoing after that person's death, and then taking away legal benefits from that person and their descendants on that basis, amounts to a legislature punishing them without a trial, rather than the courts making that decision.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference, and the Court asked for a response, which the respondents (the state and the college's leadership) later filed after two extensions of time. The petitioners have since filed a reply, and the docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.
