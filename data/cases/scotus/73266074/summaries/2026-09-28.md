## In brief

Descendants of Hastings College's founder ask the Court to revive their constitutional challenge to a California law that renamed the school and removed their family's board seat.

## What happened

In 1878, California authorized S.C. Hastings to found a public law college in exchange for $100,000, promising the school would always bear his name and reserve a board seat for his heirs. In 2022, California passed a law declaring Hastings responsible for violence against Native Americans, renaming the college and eliminating the reserved board seat. Hastings's descendants and an alumni group sued, arguing the change broke a binding contract and unconstitutionally punished the family without a trial. California courts dismissed the suit, ruling that renaming and restructuring the college were sovereign powers the state could not sign away, and that the law did not "punish" anyone. California's supreme court declined further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state law promising a specific name and governance structure for a public college, passed in return for a payment, creates a binding contract that the state cannot later break without violating the Constitution's protection against laws impairing contracts. The Court is also asked whether a law that declares a now-deceased person guilty of serious wrongdoing, and strips benefits from him and his descendants on that basis, amounts to the kind of legislative punishment the Constitution forbids without a court trial.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference, and the Court asked for a response, which California and the college officials filed opposing review; the petitioners have since filed a reply. The docket shows no other action since then.

## What each outcome would mean

The Hastings College Conservation Committee and Hastings's descendants are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments and decide whether the renaming law violated the Contract Clause or the Bill of Attainder Clause. If the Court denies the petition, the California Court of Appeal's ruling stands, leaving the law's changes to the college's name and board in place and the descendants' claims dismissed.
