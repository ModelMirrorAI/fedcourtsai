## What happened

In 1878, California authorized Serranus Clinton Hastings to found a public law college after he paid the state $100,000, with a law promising the college would always bear his name and reserve a board seat for his heirs. In 2022, California enacted a new law declaring that Hastings had financed violence against Native Americans, renaming the college and eliminating the heirs' board seat. Hastings's descendants and an alumni group sued, arguing the 1878 law was a binding contract and that the 2022 law unfairly punished Hastings and his family without a trial. California courts ruled against the petitioners at every level, holding that the state could not permanently give up its authority over the college's name and structure, and that the 2022 law did not punish anyone. The California Supreme Court declined further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state law promising a specific name and governance structure for a public institution, adopted in exchange for a payment, creates a binding agreement that limits the state's later lawmaking power. The Court is also asked whether a law that declares someone committed wrongdoing after their death, and on that basis takes away legal benefits from that person and their descendants, amounts to the kind of punishment that only courts, not legislatures, may impose.

## Where it stands
