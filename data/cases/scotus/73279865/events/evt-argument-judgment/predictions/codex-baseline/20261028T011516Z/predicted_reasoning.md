# Forecast of the Court's judgment and reasoning

I predict reversal of the Eleventh Circuit's judgment and a remand, most likely by six votes to three. The modal majority is Roberts, Alito, Sotomayor, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Jackson; the modal dissenters are Thomas, Kagan, and Barrett. The membership is substantially less certain than the result.

The Court will hold that Section 511(a) does not withdraw district-court jurisdiction over a standalone challenge to the constitutionality of a statute governing veterans' benefits. It will distinguish deciding benefits in accordance with a statute from deciding whether Congress could constitutionally enact that statute. Robison's distinction between statutory validity and agency administration will remain operative after the VJRA. The statutory discussion will center on the retained decision-under-a-law language, read in its historical and structural setting, rather than a free-standing application of the Thunder Basin factors.

The majority will reject the proposition that creating eventual review in the Veterans Court and Federal Circuit necessarily made that route exclusive for statutory-validity challenges. It will treat the agency's longstanding disclaimer of authority over such challenges, and the consequences of requiring Board members to decide them, as reasons not to adopt the government's broader reading. Constitutional avoidance may reinforce that construction, but the Court will not hold the Board itself unconstitutional or announce a general prohibition on executive consideration of constitutional questions.

The holding will extend to constitutional attacks on the validity of veterans' benefits statutes as a category; it will not be confined to this veteran, incarceration, or Section 5313. It will not create district-court jurisdiction over ordinary disagreements with VA interpretation, benefits calculations, factfinding, or agency administration. That boundary is a distinction based on the target of the challenge, not a rule that every claim called facial goes to district court and every claim called as-applied does not.

The Court will not decide whether the incarceration-based reduction in Section 5313 violates equal protection or the Bill of Attainder Clause. Congress's immunity will not disappear: the remand will permit further proceedings addressing the proper defendant and amendment of the complaint, not an immediate award of full benefits or an order compelling Congress to defend the merits.

Gorsuch is my most likely opinion author, with Kavanaugh next. Gorsuch's emphasis on the express preclusion provision and the statute's established setting fits the expected ground; Kavanaugh's attention to preserving a workable longstanding practice fits a narrower coalition opinion. If there is a dissent, Kagan is my leading author candidate, advancing the argument that deciding a benefits statute's constitutionality is a necessary legal precondition to applying it, with eventual judicial review addressing the concern that drove Robison. A separate Gorsuch concurrence on express versus implied preclusion is plausible if another Justice writes for the Court. These assignment forecasts are uncertain, not claims about known assignments.

A broader affirmance remains the main alternative: the majority could find the revised text and specialized appellate route sufficient to channel this claim. A DIG or other procedural exit is possible but not my expected resolution; there is no provisioned post-argument development supporting such an exit.
