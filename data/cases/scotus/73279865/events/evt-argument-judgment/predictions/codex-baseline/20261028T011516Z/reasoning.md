## Why I move above that anchor

The petitioner offers a reasonably administrable distinction between attacking what Congress enacted and attacking how the VA administered it. The statutory starting point is Section 511(a)'s linked duty-to-decide and bar-to-review, which use the same class of questions. The official statutory text confirms both the linkage and the specified exceptions. [R1] Robison supplied a pre-VJRA distinction between constitutional challenges to legislation and review of agency benefits decisions. [R2] Whether Congress preserved that distinction, not whether veterans can ever obtain any judicial review, is the contested issue here.

The briefs supply a substantial structural argument for the petitioner: imposing a duty on the Board to decide statutory validity would depart from its predominant practice and raises questions about inferior officers' final decisions that the agency cannot itself appeal to the Veterans Court. This is an argument made in the provisioned briefing, not an independent finding that the Board is unconstitutional. I expect it to reinforce a statutory construction, not become a sweeping Article II holding.

The argument gives unusually concrete support for a cross-ideological reversal coalition. Gorsuch challenged the government's account of existing practice and its reliance on an implied-preclusion framework where Congress wrote an express clause. Sotomayor defended the continued district-court route and questioned the need for a prolonged agency process on a pure statutory-validity issue. Jackson developed the petitioner's distinction between applying a given law and evaluating its validity, and pressed the implications of making constitutional adjudication mandatory for the Board. These are the strongest three reversal signals.

Kavanaugh is a softer fourth: his initial concern favored the government's available-review argument, but later he asked why a close statutory question should unsettle established practice and what practical harm required doing so. Alito initially gave the petitioner a difficult textual examination, but later explored an opinion allowing district-court claims not seriously dependent on disputed facts. That question identifies a possible narrowing route; it is not a commitment to take it. Together with the general reversal prior, these exchanges justify moving from roughly 0.70 to 0.78, not to near-certainty.

## Why I stop at 0.78

The government's textual case is serious. Constitutional validity can be understood as a necessary precondition to applying a benefits statute, and the revised text does not repeat the old statutory formulation verbatim. The specialized appellate scheme expressly permits the Federal Circuit to decide statutory-validity and constitutional questions in cases under that scheme. [R3] Robison therefore does not mechanically answer this dispute: it addressed the predecessor law without the same judicial-review route. [R2, R3] The official texts support that distinction; my prediction that it will not carry the day is an inference, not something established by those sources.

Kagan forcefully developed the government's necessary-precondition reading against petitioner. Her later questions about whether the amendments clearly displaced Robison make her less firm than her early exchange alone suggests. Barrett emphasized that the veteran still has a judicial route and asked for the Article II response. Roberts supplied little substantive questioning, and Thomas's few interventions do not yield a reliable direction. A five-Justice affirmance coalition remains possible. The sovereign-immunity/proper-defendant posture also limits the scope of a petitioner win; the merits briefs explain that the Eleventh Circuit treated amendment as futile because of the jurisdictional holding. I do not equate a reversal on that issue with an immediate right to relief.

My point lineup is six to three, with Thomas, Kagan, and Barrett dissenting. Kagan's placement has the clearest case-specific support; Barrett's is a weaker channeling inference. Thomas is a low-confidence textual/channeling inference, not a directional conclusion from his short questions. Roberts's placement is also weak. An author distribution is necessarily much less certain than the judgment forecast. An actual separate writing by any one Justice is not required by these marginal probabilities.

The 0.61 stakes score reflects the district-court access rule's reach across veterans' statutory challenges and its implications for agency duties, tempered by the fact that this case is about forum rather than invalidating the incarceration provision or redesigning the entire benefits system.
