# Forecast of the Court's Decision in *Johnson v. United States Congress* (No. 25-735)

## I. Predicted Holding and Disposition

The Supreme Court will **reverse** the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Eleventh Circuit (and remand for further proceedings) by a projected **9–0 or 8–1** vote (with a 7–2 vote as the main alternative if Justices Thomas and Alito dissent). The Court will hold that 38 U.S.C. § 511(a), as amended by the Veterans' Judicial Review Act of 1988 (VJRA), does not strip federal district courts of federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 to hear standalone facial challenges to the constitutionality of acts of Congress affecting veterans' benefits.

Although the Eleventh Circuit correctly noted that the United States Congress itself enjoys sovereign immunity, it erred in holding that granting Petitioner Floyd D. Johnson leave to amend his *pro se* complaint (or construing it to name a proper Executive Branch defendant under 5 U.S.C. § 702) would be futile on the theory that the VJRA channels all facial constitutional challenges to federal statutes exclusively through the Board of Veterans' Appeals (BVA), the Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (Veterans Court), and the Federal Circuit.

## II. Doctrinal Reasoning of the Majority Opinion

### A. Statutory Text of 38 U.S.C. § 511(a): "Necessary to a Decision by the Secretary Under a Law"

The majority opinion—most likely authored by Justice Kagan or Justice Kavanaugh—will anchor its holding in the text of 38 U.S.C. § 511(a). Section 511(a) contains two linked sentences:

1. **The Mandate Clause:** "The Secretary shall decide all questions of law and fact necessary to a decision by the Secretary under a law that affects the provision of benefits by the Secretary to veterans or the dependents or survivors of veterans."
2. **The Preclusion Clause:** "Subject to subsection (b), the decision of the Secretary as to any such question shall be final and conclusive and may not be reviewed by any other official or by any court, whether by an action in the nature of mandamus or otherwise."

The preclusion of district-court review in the second sentence is strictly coextensive with the Secretary's mandatory duty to decide in the first sentence ("any such question"), as well as the BVA's appellate jurisdiction under 38 U.S.C. § 7104(a) ("All questions in a matter which under section 511(a) of this title is subject to decision by the Secretary shall be subject to one review on appeal to the Secretary. Final decisions on such appeals shall be made by the Board.").

The text excludes standalone facial challenges to the constitutionality of an act of Congress for two textual reasons that reinforce one another:

1. **"**Under a law**":** As the Court held in *Pereira v. Sessions*, 585 U.S. 198, 215 (2018), and *Johnson v. Robison*, 415 U.S. 361, 367 (1974), a decision made "under" a statute is a decision made "in accordance with" or "in the interpretation or application of" that statute. It takes the statute enacted by Congress as a given and applies it to a particular claimant's facts. A facial challenge to the constitutional power of Congress to enact the statute itself (here, 38 U.S.C. § 5313's reduction of disability compensation for incarcerated felons) challenges a decision *of Congress* under the Constitution, not a decision *of the Secretary under* Title 38.
2. **"**Necessary to a decision by the Secretary**":** Deciding whether Congress violated the Constitution when it enacted § 5313 is not "necessary" to the Secretary's decision under § 5313. Indeed, the VA's own binding regulation provides that "[i]n the consideration of appeals, the Board is bound by applicable statutes, the regulations of the Department of Veterans Affairs, and precedent opinions of the General Counsel of the Department of Veterans Affairs." 38 C.F.R. § 20.105 (codifying former 38 C.F.R. § 19.5). Because the BVA is legally bound to follow and apply federal statutes as written and has consistently disclaimed jurisdiction to adjudicate facial constitutional challenges to statutes, resolving the constitutionality of a statute cannot be "necessary" to the Secretary's or the Board's decision under that statute.

As several Justices emphasized at oral argument, the Government's reading of § 511(a) creates an inescapable statutory contradiction: if facial constitutional challenges to statutes are "questions of law ... necessary to a decision by the Secretary under a law," then the first sentence of § 511(a) ("The Secretary *shall decide* all questions...") and § 7104(a) ("Final decisions on such appeals *shall be made by the Board*") command the BVA to adjudicate the constitutionality of acts of Congress—meaning that 38 C.F.R. § 20.105 and thousands of BVA decisions since 1988 have systematically violated § 511(a). Conversely, the Government's fallback theory—that the BVA cannot declare a statute unconstitutional on its own, yet "decides" the question by following whatever precedent opinion higher-level VA officials or the Secretary might issue under § 7104(c)—collapses against the text of § 7104(a) (which vests final decision-making authority on § 511(a) questions in the BVA, not the General Counsel) and the reality that in over 35 years the VA has never operated such a referral mechanism.

### B. Continuity with *Johnson v. Robison* and the Prior-Construction Canon

The textual reading of § 511(a) is cemented by *Johnson v. Robison*, 415 U.S. 361 (1974), and the prior-construction canon (*Helsinn Healthcare S.A. v. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc.*, 586 U.S. 123, 131 (2019)).

In *Robison*, the Court construed § 511(a)'s predecessor, 38 U.S.C. § 211(a) (1970), which barred judicial review of "the decisions of the Administrator on any question of law or fact under any law administered by the Veterans' Administration providing benefits for veterans." The Court unanimously held that § 211(a) did not bar federal-question jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1331 over a facial constitutional challenge to a veterans' benefits statute, resting first on the plain text—that a decision "under" a statute means interpreting or applying the statute, not adjudicating its constitutional validity, and that the BVA disclaimed authority to pass on the constitutionality of legislation—and second on the absence of clear legislative intent to restrict judicial review.

When Congress enacted the VJRA in 1988, fourteen years after *Robison* and months after *Traynor v. Turnage*, 485 U.S. 535 (1988), Congress targeted *Traynor*'s holding regarding cross-cutting statutes not "administered by" the VA (such as the Rehabilitation Act) by replacing "under any law administered by the Veterans' Administration providing benefits" with "under a law that affects the provision of benefits." Crucially, however, Congress retained the operative phrase—"a decision by the Secretary *under a law*"—that *Robison* had authoritatively construed to exclude facial constitutional challenges to statutes. And contemporaneously with the VJRA's enactment in 1989, the VA repromulgated its regulation stating that the BVA is "bound by applicable statutes." Had Congress intended in 1988 to abrogate *Robison* and strip district courts of their existing § 1331 jurisdiction over facial constitutional challenges to statutes, it would not have carried forward the very phrase *Robison* interpreted and left the BVA bound to enforce statutes without authority to review their validity.

### C. Structure of Chapter 72 and *Elgin v. Department of the Treasury*

The Court will reject the Government's and the Eleventh Circuit's reliance on 38 U.S.C. §§ 7261(a) and 7292(c) and on *Elgin v. Department of the Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1 (2012).

First, §§ 7261(a) and 7292(c) are not left superfluous if district courts retain concurrent § 1331 jurisdiction over standalone facial challenges to statutes:
- Under § 7252(a), the Veterans Court's "exclusive jurisdiction" is "to review decisions of the Board." Under § 7292(c), the Federal Circuit's "exclusive jurisdiction" to "review and decide any challenge to the validity of any statute or regulation or any interpretation thereof brought under this section, and to interpret constitutional and statutory provisions, to the extent presented and necessary to a decision" is expressly limited to challenges "brought under this section"—that is, appeals from decisions of the Veterans Court.
- Those provisions do full work across (1) challenges to the validity of VA *regulations* (which § 511(b)(1) and § 502 channel directly to the Federal Circuit), (2) *as-applied* constitutional challenges to VA decisions and procedures, and (3) constitutional challenges to statutes that a veteran chooses to raise alongside ordinary benefits arguments in an appeal coming up through the BVA and Veterans Court. Giving the Veterans Court and Federal Circuit authority to resolve constitutional questions when a case is properly before them does not expand what § 511(a) forces into the BVA in the first place.

Second, *Elgin* is inapposite. *Elgin* applied the implied-preclusion framework of *Thunder Basin Coal Co. v. Reich*, 510 U.S. 200 (1994), to the Civil Service Reform Act (CSRA), which contains no express jurisdiction-stripping provision comparable to § 511(a). Here, Congress enacted an express preclusion clause in § 511(a) with specific textual boundaries, and both parties agree that the case turns on the scope of § 511(a) rather than free-standing *Thunder Basin* implied preclusion. Moreover, even if *Thunder Basin* and *Axon Enterprise, Inc. v. FTC*, 598 U.S. 175 (2023), were consulted as a structural check, they cut against preclusion: the BVA has no institutional competence or legal authority to adjudicate the constitutionality of acts of Congress (*Carr v. Saul*, 593 U.S. 83, 92 (2021); *Free Enterprise Fund v. PCAOB*, 561 U.S. 477, 491 (2010)), and forcing a veteran bringing a purely facial challenge to a statute to spend years in a backlogged administrative queue before an agency tribunal that must rubber-stamp the statute serves no exhaustion purpose.

## III. Vote Breakdown and Separate Writings

- **Predicted Lineup:** **9–0** or **8–1** (most likely **8–1** or **9–0**, with **7–2** possible if both Thomas and Alito dissent).
  - **Solidly for Reversal (7 Justices):** Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson all expressed clear skepticism of the Government's position at oral argument.
    - **Justice Kagan** repeatedly dismantled the Government's textual argument, pointing out that the Government's reading of the first sentence of § 511(a) would require the BVA to decide facial constitutional challenges despite 38 C.F.R. § 20.105 forbidding it, and characterized the Government's newly minted "higher-level VA officials" theory as implausible when Congress knew the BVA could not decide statutory constitutionality.
    - **Justice Gorsuch** pressed the Government heavily on the anomaly of requiring futile exhaustion before an agency that lacks power to declare a statute unconstitutional, invoking *Carr v. Saul* and questioning how an Article II official could refuse to enforce an act of Congress absent a court order.
    - **Justice Kavanaugh** emphasized the prior-construction canon under *Robison*, observing that Congress amended the statute in 1988 specifically to address *Traynor* while keeping the "under a law" language from *Robison*, and noted the real-world harm of multi-year BVA delays when the BVA cannot decide the issue.
    - **Justice Barrett** highlighted that the Government's historical support consisted of only a handful of outlier BVA decisions over 35 years that violated the VA's own regulation (38 C.F.R. § 20.105).
    - **Justice Sotomayor** challenged the Government on why a veteran who raises *only* a facial constitutional challenge should be forced through years of pointless administrative delay, and noted that the Government had never previously articulated its merits-brief reading of § 511(a).
    - **Chief Justice Roberts** underscored the practical futility of forcing a veteran with a pure constitutional challenge into a non-adversarial benefits bureaucracy that cannot address the claim.
    - **Justice Jackson** agreed that requiring veterans to go through a futile multi-year BVA process is anomalous in a pro-veteran scheme, while probing whether §§ 7261 and 7292(c) create structural tension.
  - **Swing / Potential Dissenters (Justices Thomas and Alito):**
    - **Justice Thomas** authored *Elgin* and asked petitioner's counsel why the facial/as-applied line was not "hazy" and why Congress's grant of jurisdiction to the Veterans Court and Federal Circuit over statutory validity in §§ 7261 and 7292(c) did not distinguish *Robison*. However, he also probed the Government on how the BVA handles constitutional challenges in practice. Because *Robison* is a textual precedent on the meaning of "under a law" and § 511(a) is an express preclusion clause rather than an implied *Thunder Basin* scheme, there is a substantial chance Justice Thomas joins the majority (or concurs), though he is the likeliest dissenter.
    - **Justice Alito** questioned petitioner's counsel on how the statutory words "under a law" distinguish between facial and as-applied challenges and on the legislative history of the VJRA, but later pressed the Government on what record a court would even have to review if the BVA does not address the constitutional claim.

- **Opinion Assignment and Separate Writings:**
  - **Majority Author:** **Justice Kagan** (24%) or **Justice Kavanaugh** (20%) are the strongest candidates to write the majority opinion, given their commanding textual and doctrinal interventions at argument, followed by **Chief Justice Roberts** (14%) and **Justice Barrett** (13%).
  - **Concurrences:** **Justice Gorsuch** is a strong candidate to write a concurrence emphasizing separation-of-powers principles (that executive agencies neither possess authority to set aside acts of Congress nor may trap litigants in futile administrative exhaustion on pure constitutional questions under *Carr* and *Axon*). **Justice Jackson** may also write separately to address how Chapter 72's review provisions (§§ 7261 and 7292(c)) interact with § 511(a) and the legislative history of the VJRA.
  - **Dissents:** If there is a dissent, **Justice Thomas** (potentially joined by **Justice Alito**) would author it, arguing along the lines of *Elgin* and the Eleventh Circuit's opinion that § 511(a) and § 7292(c) channel all constitutional challenges—facial and as-applied alike—through the VJRA's specialized appellate review scheme.
