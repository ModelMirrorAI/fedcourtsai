# Predictor Reasoning: *Floyd D. Johnson v. United States Congress* (No. 25-735)

## 1. Case Posture and Target Event

- **Case:** *Floyd D. Johnson v. United States Congress*, No. 25-735 (11th Cir. below, 151 F.4th 1287 (2025)).
- **Event:** `evt-argument-judgment` (Stage: `merits`, Moment: `argued`, Target: `judgment`).
- **Question Presented:** "Did the Veterans' Judicial Review Act strip district courts of the jurisdiction, recognized by this Court in *Johnson v. Robison*, 415 U.S. 361 (1974), to hear challenges to the constitutionality of acts of Congress affecting veterans' benefits?"
- **Procedural History:**
  - Petitioner Floyd D. Johnson, an Army veteran with an 80% service-connected PTSD disability rating, is serving a 40-year felony sentence in Florida state prison. Pursuant to 38 U.S.C. § 5313(a)(1), which caps disability compensation at the 10% rate (half of that if under 20%) for veterans incarcerated for more than 60 days following a felony conviction, the VA reduced his monthly benefits to the 10% rate.
  - Proceeding *pro se* in the U.S. District Court for the Middle District of Florida, Johnson filed suit naming the "United States Congress" as defendant, alleging that 38 U.S.C. § 5313 violates the Bill of Attainder Clause and the Fifth Amendment's Equal Protection component.
  - The district court assumed jurisdiction without deciding it and dismissed the complaint on the merits under 28 U.S.C. § 1915A as frivolous.
  - On appeal, the Eleventh Circuit (Chief Judge William Pryor, joined by Judges Luck and Brasher) appointed counsel and then held: (1) the district court erred under *Steel Co.* by assuming hypothetical jurisdiction; (2) sovereign immunity barred the suit against the United States Congress; and (3) Johnson's motion on appeal to construe the complaint as naming the VA or for leave to amend to name a proper defendant must be denied as **futile** because 38 U.S.C. § 511(a) and the VJRA's exclusive review scheme (38 U.S.C. §§ 7104, 7252, 7261, 7292) strip federal district courts of jurisdiction over facial constitutional challenges to veterans' benefits statutes, relying heavily on *Elgin v. Department of the Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1 (2012). The Eleventh Circuit vacated and remanded with instructions to dismiss without prejudice for lack of jurisdiction.

## 2. Analysis of the Legal Merits

### A. Text and Structure of 38 U.S.C. § 511(a)
Section 511(a) provides:
> "The Secretary shall decide all questions of law and fact necessary to a decision by the Secretary under a law that affects the provision of benefits by the Secretary to veterans or the dependents or survivors of veterans. Subject to subsection (b), the decision of the Secretary as to any such question shall be final and conclusive and may not be reviewed by any other official or by any court, whether by an action in the nature of mandamus or otherwise."

The statutory architecture links three things tightly together:
1. What the Secretary **shall decide** (first sentence of § 511(a));
2. What the Board of Veterans' Appeals **shall decide** on final administrative appeal under 38 U.S.C. § 7104(a) ("All questions in a matter which under section 511(a) of this title is subject to decision by the Secretary shall be subject to one review on appeal to the Secretary. Final decisions on such appeals shall be made by the Board."); and
3. What district courts **may not review** (second sentence of § 511(a): "the decision of the Secretary as to *any such question*").

Petitioner's textual argument is exceptionally strong:
- First, a "decision by the Secretary *under a law* that affects the provision of benefits" means a decision applying or interpreting the statute enacted by Congress ("in accordance with" the statute, *Pereira v. Sessions*, 585 U.S. 198, 215 (2018); *Johnson v. Robison*, 415 U.S. 361, 367 (1974)). It takes the statute as valid and applies it. Adjudicating whether Congress had constitutional authority to enact 38 U.S.C. § 5313 is not "necessary to a decision by the Secretary under" § 5313.
- Second, VA regulations expressly forbid the BVA from invalidating statutes: "In the consideration of appeals, the Board is bound by applicable statutes, the regulations of the Department of Veterans Affairs, and precedent opinions of the General Counsel of the Department of Veterans Affairs." 38 C.F.R. § 20.105. And the BVA routinely rules in decision after decision that it lacks jurisdiction to consider challenges to the constitutionality of federal statutes.
- This puts the Government in a fatal bind: if facial constitutional challenges to statutes are "questions of law ... necessary to a decision by the Secretary under a law," then § 511(a) and § 7104(a) *require* the BVA to decide them, which would mean 38 C.F.R. § 20.105 is unlawful and the BVA has been violating § 511(a) for 38 years.
- Attempting to escape that bind in its merits brief, the Solicitor General argued for the first time that while the BVA itself cannot hold a statute unconstitutional, "higher-level VA officials" (the Secretary or General Counsel issuing a precedent opinion under § 7104(c)) could decide that a statute is unconstitutional, and the BVA would then apply that decision. Yet the Government conceded it could not point to a single example in history where the BVA referred a constitutional challenge to the Secretary or General Counsel under § 511(a), and § 7104(a) assigns final decision-making on § 511(a) appeals to the BVA, not the General Counsel.

### B. *Johnson v. Robison* (1974) and the 1988 VJRA Amendments
In *Robison*, 415 U.S. at 367, this Court interpreted 38 U.S.C. § 211(a)—which barred judicial review of "the decisions of the Administrator on any question of law or fact under any law administered by the Veterans' Administration providing benefits for veterans"—and held that a facial constitutional challenge to a benefits statute is a challenge "to a decision of Congress," not a decision of the Administrator "under" a statute.

When Congress enacted the VJRA in 1988, it responded to *Traynor v. Turnage*, 485 U.S. 535 (1988) (which had allowed district-court suits challenging VA regulations under the Rehabilitation Act on the theory that the Rehabilitation Act was not a law "administered by" the VA), by changing "under any law administered by the Veterans' Administration" to "under a law that affects the provision of benefits." Congress kept the key phrase "decision by the Secretary *under a law*." Under the prior-construction canon (*Helsinn*, 586 U.S. at 131), carrying forward "decision ... under a law" carried forward *Robison*'s construction of that phrase. Indeed, following the VJRA, the Second, Sixth, Ninth, and D.C. Circuits (as well as Judge Randolph Moss in *Prewitt v. McDonough*, 633 F. Supp. 3d 195 (D.D.C. 2022)) recognized that *Robison*'s rule for facial constitutional challenges to statutes survived the VJRA, until the Eleventh Circuit broke ranks here.

### C. Why Chapter 72 (§§ 7261 and 7292(c)) and *Elgin* Do Not Rescue the Government
The Government's strongest counterargument is structural: in 1988, Congress created the Veterans Court and vested the Federal Circuit with "exclusive jurisdiction to review and decide any challenge to the validity of any statute or regulation or any interpretation thereof brought under this section, and to interpret constitutional and statutory provisions, to the extent presented and necessary to a decision." 38 U.S.C. § 7292(c); see also § 7261(a)(1), (3)(B). And in *Elgin v. Department of the Treasury*, 567 U.S. 1 (2012), the Court held that the CSRA's review scheme (MSPB to Federal Circuit) impliedly precluded district-court jurisdiction over facial constitutional challenges to statutes, rejecting a facial/as-applied distinction as "hazy."

However:
1. Section 7292(c) grants the Federal Circuit exclusive jurisdiction over challenges to the validity of a statute "brought under this section" (i.e., appeals from the Veterans Court)—it defines the allocation of authority between the Veterans Court and the Federal Circuit and bars other circuits from reviewing Veterans Court decisions, rather than expanding the scope of § 511(a)'s initial channeling into the BVA. And §§ 7261 and 7292(c) still have ample work to do when a veteran raises a constitutional challenge alongside administrative benefits claims inside the VJRA system, or brings an as-applied challenge or regulatory challenge.
2. *Elgin* was an implied-preclusion case under *Thunder Basin* where the CSRA had no express preclusion statute like § 511(a); here, § 511(a) expressly defines what is precluded, and *Robison* already construed its operative text.

## 3. Oral Argument Analysis

Oral argument (held October 5, 2026; petitioner represented by Easha Anand, respondent represented by Assistant to the Solicitor General Kevin J. Barber) went overwhelmingly in Petitioner's favor.

### Quantitative Signal (`argument-features.json`)
- **Total Justice engagement:** 90 turns / 2,426 words during Petitioner's argument vs. 87 turns / 2,216 words during Respondent's argument.
- Normally, heavier questioning of Petitioner indicates skepticism of Petitioner, **but here the content of the turns during Petitioner's time was largely friendly or clarifying**, whereas the questioning of Respondent was a sustained, skeptical barrage from seven Justices.
- Specifically, during Respondent's time:
  - **Justice Kagan** (18 turns, 558 words to Respondent vs. only 3 turns, 49 words to Petitioner) thoroughly dismantled the Government's reading of § 511(a) and 38 C.F.R. § 20.105, telling Mr. Barber: "That would be a crazy thing for Congress to think, right? Because we had already said in *Robison* that the BVA didn't do this and didn't have authority to do it," and "It is a strange argument you're making, Mr. Barber, honestly... You're saying § 511(a) obligates the BVA to do this, and for 35 years the BVA has essentially been ignoring its statutory obligation?"
  - **Justice Gorsuch** (22 turns, 344 words to Respondent vs. 5 turns, 63 words to Petitioner) repeatedly challenged the Government on why anyone would read § 511(a) to require years of futile exhaustion before an executive board that cannot declare a statute unconstitutional, citing *Carr v. Saul* and questioning whether the Executive Branch even claims power to set aside statutes in administrative adjudications.
  - **Justice Kavanaugh** (8 turns, 240 words to Respondent) stated his bottom-line view explicitly: "Look, big picture, *Robison* sets a rule... Congress comes back in '88 and doesn't touch the language from *Robison*, really addresses *Traynor*. The VA keeps the same reg pretty much on the books. And why not just stick with the background rule, especially given the delays in the BVA process where the BVA can't decide this anyway?"
  - **Justice Barrett** (7 turns, 195 words to Respondent) pointed out that the Government's four cited BVA decisions over 35 years were outliers that violated the VA's own binding regulation (38 C.F.R. § 20.105), and asked why, if a veteran wants only to bring a facial constitutional challenge, he shouldn't be able to go straight to district court.
  - **Justice Sotomayor** (8 turns, 247 words to Respondent) noted that the Government had never previously advanced its current interpretation of § 511(a) and pressed why a veteran bringing a pure facial challenge must wait five to seven years in the BVA.
  - **Chief Justice Roberts** (4 turns, 129 words to Respondent) emphasized that a veteran who simply wants to challenge the constitutionality of an act of Congress "has to go through a whole process that isn't going to address that at all before he gets to the question of whether the limit on his recovery is unconstitutional."
  - **Justice Jackson** (11 turns, 286 words to Respondent; 25 turns, 829 words to Petitioner) probed both sides carefully. With Ms. Anand, she explored why Congress wrote "validity of any statute" into § 7292(c) and whether the legislative history showed Congress thought constitutional challenges would go through the VJRA. With Mr. Barber, however, she pushed back forcefully on why Congress would have wanted veterans to go through a futile multi-year BVA process when the BVA was known to be bound by statutes.
  - **Justice Thomas** (7 turns to Petitioner, 3 turns to Respondent) and **Justice Alito** (17 turns to Petitioner, 5 turns to Respondent) were the most skeptical of Petitioner's textual distinction between facial and as-applied challenges under "under a law," though both also asked pointed practical questions of the Government about what the BVA actually does.
