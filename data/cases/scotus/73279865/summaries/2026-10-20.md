## In brief

A veteran asks the Court to revive his suit challenging a federal statute that cut his disability benefits during incarceration.

## What happened

Floyd Johnson, an Army veteran, received disability benefits for PTSD connected to his service. A federal law, 38 U.S.C. § 5313, cuts benefits for veterans incarcerated more than 60 days after a felony conviction, and the Department of Veterans Affairs reduced Johnson's payments accordingly. Representing himself while in prison, Johnson sued in federal district court, naming Congress as defendant and arguing the law was unconstitutional. The district court dismissed the case. On appeal, the Eleventh Circuit ruled that Congress could not be sued, and that even naming the right defendant would not help: it held that a separate law, the Veterans' Judicial Review Act, requires such constitutional challenges to go through the VA's own appeals system and specialized courts, not ordinary federal district courts.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether that specialized veterans' review system is the only place a veteran can challenge the constitutionality of a benefits statute, or whether veterans may instead bring such challenges directly in a regular federal district court, as this Court had allowed under the review system that existed before 1988.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on October 5, 2026. It now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Johnson, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to reverse that ruling. If the Court reverses, Johnson's case would go back to a district court, which could hear his constitutional challenge to the benefits-reduction law directly. If the Court affirms, veterans with similar claims would have to pursue them through the VA's internal appeals board and specialized veterans' courts rather than ordinary federal district courts, and Johnson's suit would remain dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. The Court could also vacate the ruling and send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
