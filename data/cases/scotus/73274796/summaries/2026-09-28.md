## In brief

Members of a Christian health-sharing ministry ask the Court to revive their challenge to New Mexico's order that the ministry register as an insurer or stop operating there.

## What happened

Breanna Renteria and Laura Smith belong to Gospel Light, a health care sharing ministry whose members share medical costs according to shared religious beliefs instead of buying insurance. New Mexico's insurance regulator found that Gospel Light was operating as an unlicensed insurance company and ordered it to stop operating in the state or comply with state insurance law, also imposing a fine. Renteria, Smith, and others sued, arguing this violated their religious-exercise rights and was preempted by federal health care law, and sought a preliminary injunction. The district court denied the injunction, and the Tenth Circuit affirmed, with one judge dissenting.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to clarify how courts decide whether a law that burdens religious practice counts as neutral and generally applicable, including whether plaintiffs must prove officials acted from religious hostility and whether exemptions for secular organizations must be treated as comparable to religious ones. It is also asked whether hostile statements by government officials alone can establish a violation, and whether federal health care law prevents New Mexico from requiring the ministry to register as an insurer or cease operating.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, and the Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views; that brief has been filed, along with a supplemental brief from the petitioners, and the case was distributed again for a later conference. The docket does not show any decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Renteria and Smith, who lost below, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full briefing and argument and decide the legal questions itself, without presuming either side is right. If it denies review, the Tenth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the ruling that New Mexico's order likely did not violate the Free Exercise Clause and was not preempted by federal law, and the case would continue in the lower courts on that basis.
