## Why denial remains the leading disposition

The petition, pp. 13–22, argues that McDonnell Douglas displaced Rule 56 by allowing the panel to discount circumstantial evidence and credit disputed employer explanations. Its pp. 23–29 separately invoke Bostock and Abercrombie, linking potentially mixed motives to supervisors' treatment of Cross's hijab. Those are substantial arguments for correcting the analysis, but their factual predicates remain disputed advocacy, not established findings in this prediction.

The opposition, pp. 7–11 and 20–24, supplies serious certworthiness and vehicle objections. It identifies no accepted rule permitting resolution of genuine factual disputes against the employee; rather, it disputes what the record could prove. It emphasizes that the memorandum is unpublished, that alternative methods of proof remain available under circuit law, and that removing the framework would not change the result given the employer's asserted grounds. Its account also disputes the petition's termination timeline, the comparator's similarity, and the significance of the police assessment and Cross's contemporaneous admissions. I cannot resolve those factual disagreements from advocacy alone.

The appended memorandum, pp. 2a–8a, matters independently of the opposition's characterization. It recognizes both direct proof and a prima facie framework permitting other circumstances to support an inference, distinguishes the principal comparator, and analyzes retaliation and hostile environment separately. At the same time, its treatment of an alternative dress-code explanation and its reliance on the employer's collision assessment give the petition a concrete basis for complaining that disputed inferences were drawn against Cross. Thus there is a nontrivial supervisory-correction possibility, but the question is tangled with record sufficiency and multiple theories. The preservation objection to a distinct accommodation claim, noted in the opposition at pp. 10–11, further limits a direct Abercrombie vehicle. I do not treat failure of one theory as an independent bar to reviewing every other theory.

Supreme Court Rule 10, in the rules effective March 16, 2026, distinguishes compelling cert questions from ordinary complaints about factual findings or application of correctly stated law. That distinction supports denial here; unpublished status alone is not a jurisdictional obstacle. The official Ames opinion, decided June 5, 2025, rejects a heightened prima facie requirement for majority-group plaintiffs, not McDonnell Douglas wholesale. Its separate concurrence confirms real interest in revisiting the framework, but it is not a majority holding doing so. The opposition's reliance on the analogous Hittle denial, pp. 1 and 11–12, is additional pre-decision context, not a binding rejection of this petition's question. Taken together, the material supports a modest grant probability rather than either near-zero odds or a presumption of review.
