## What the filings establish

Question 1 asks whether section 2255(f)(1)'s one-year period waits for all codefendants' separate certiorari petitions to be decided. Question 2 asks whether novelty makes the procedural issue debatable enough to require a certificate of appealability. The petition's main submission is the absence of a case on precisely these facts, not disagreement between courts of appeals. Its body repeatedly acknowledges the narrow circumstances. See petition pp. 4–10.

The record makes the ordinary individual-finality rule a substantial obstacle. Green's own direct-review petition was denied January 23, 2023; Selgas's was denied March 27, 2023; Green filed his section 2255 motion March 25, 2024. The petition thus needs the second date to displace the first. The district court explains that the later notice's inclusion of both trial numbers did not change whose petition was denied, and distinguishes the rehearing-based certiorari deadline from the effect of separately completed Supreme Court review. See appendix B, pp. 8a–11a, and the actual notices in appendices C and D. The later Fifth Circuit letter says that the judgment had previously issued as mandate; it does not purport to reopen Green's Supreme Court proceeding.

Clay v. United States, 537 U.S. 522, 527 (2003), supplies the familiar postconviction finality framework. The provisioned lower-court opinion applies it individually. An additional CourtListener lookup confirmed Clay's finality discussion; Clay's precise holding concerned a defendant who did not petition for certiorari, so I do not represent it as a holding on Green's exact facts. The petition's analogy to Supreme Court Rule 13.3 addresses the common deadline following rehearing, not necessarily the proposed extension after Green's own petition was denied. Its historical codefendant-relief example does not itself establish that the limitations clock remains open in every separate petition.

For Question 2, Slack v. McDaniel, 529 U.S. 473, 484–85 (2000), requires debatability of both the procedural ruling and an underlying constitutional claim. A CourtListener excerpt confirmed that a plain procedural bar can foreclose a certificate. Novel factual circumstances alone do not establish that either requirement was misapplied. Although the petition describes the Fifth Circuit denial as unexplained, appendix A, pp. 1a–2a, actually states the Slack standard and concludes that Green has not made the requisite showing. It is brief and lacks detailed application, not literally devoid of a stated legal standard. The district court gives a reasoned timeliness analysis. Its separate rejection of the professional-confidentiality impediment argument under section 2255(f)(2), appendix B, pp. 11a–14a, is not a substitute for answering Question 1, but underscores that the petition is not supported by a clearly overlooked alternative accrual date.

## Other probabilities and uncertainty

I put an additional distribution at 6% and a new CVSG at 0.1%. A request for a response from the United States would be a distinct procedural step, not a CVSG. No such response request is in the snapshot. If this unlikely grant occurs, I put 65% on disposition in the cert order: the petition expressly requests, in the alternative, a remand for issuance of a certificate. That conditional number means about 0.65% unconditional summary relief, not a 65% chance of intervention. No intervening decision is identified to support a routine hold-and-GVR route; summary correction of the certificate ruling is the more plausible summary scenario.

Conditional on denial, I assign 1.2% to a dissent or statement respecting denial. The independent per-Justice writing probabilities are correspondingly small and are not a distribution summing to one; more than one Justice can write. No writing is my modal forecast.

The principal uncertainty is whether the Court finds the certificate threshold materially misapplied despite the finality obstacle. I have only the petition and its appended opinions, not the full collateral record or a substantive opposition. I retain a nonzero chance of intervention for that reason. The stakes score of 0.18 reflects a potentially consequential access-to-court rule for a small procedural subset, not the grant probability.
