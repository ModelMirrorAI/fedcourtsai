## In brief

GEO Group asks the Court to review a ruling that let Washington apply health and safety rules to its privately run immigration detention facility.

## What happened

GEO Group operates a private detention facility in Tacoma, Washington, under contract with federal immigration authorities. Washington passed a law applying health and safety standards, already used for other confined-population facilities, to private detention facilities. GEO sued, arguing the law unconstitutionally targets the federal government through its contractor and is preempted by federal law. A federal district court agreed in part and blocked several provisions. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals rejected GEO's argument that the law directly regulates the federal government or is preempted, but sent back to the district court the question whether the law discriminates against GEO compared to similar facilities. GEO then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state may apply detailed health and safety requirements to a company that runs a detention facility under federal contract, without those requirements counting as improper regulation of the federal government itself, and whether the way the lower court compared this facility to other state-regulated facilities was correct.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for November 13, 2026, after earlier conferences in October; the docket does not show that the Court has yet acted on it.

## What each outcome would mean

GEO Group, which lost on these issues at the Ninth Circuit, is asking the Court to reverse that ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the state law improperly regulates or discriminates against a federal contractor; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, meaning the state's law remains in effect and the case returns to the lower courts, where a related discrimination claim is already being litigated.
