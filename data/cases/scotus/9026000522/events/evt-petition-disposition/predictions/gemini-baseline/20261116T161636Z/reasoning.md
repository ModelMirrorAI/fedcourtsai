## Adjustments From the Anchor

Starting from the `5.17%` `baseline [reached]` anchor, the November 2 **Call for a Response** after respondents' October 20 waiver and the presence of **one amicus brief** (with petitioner's October 26 letter noting other potential amici before the November 18–19 Rule 37.2 window closes) would ordinarily lift a generic paid petition into the ~8–10% range, especially given Justice Thomas's solo statement respecting denial in *MacRae v. Mattos*, 606 U.S. 937 (2025), expressing interest in *Pickering*'s disruption standard for off-duty social-media speech.

However, four powerful case-specific and comparative factors—which respondents' experienced appellate counsel (City of Chicago Appeals Division) has not yet put before the Court because she initially waived—drive my forecast back **down to 3.5% (`0.035`)**:

1. **Severe Vehicle Defects (*Monell* Waiver + Preserved Qualified Immunity)**: Petitioner portrays this case as a "perfect vehicle" (Pet. 23), omitting two critical procedural obstacles apparent in the appendix:
   - First, the Seventh Circuit held that Inendino **waived his *Monell* claim against the City of Chicago** by raising it for the first time in his reply brief (Pet. App. 7a n.2), and the petition does not challenge that waiver ruling. Under *Los Angeles County v. Humphries*, 562 U.S. 29 (2010), *Monell* governs both damages and prospective relief against a municipality, leaving judgment for the City intact.
   - Second, the only remaining respondents—former Fire Commissioner Annette Nance-Holt and Assistant Commissioner Brian Casey—are sued **solely in their individual capacities** (Pet. iii) and **preserved qualified immunity** at summary judgment (Pet. App. 45a; District Judge Kennelly expressly declined to reach qualified immunity and *Monell* only because he found no First Amendment violation). Because petitioner's own core premise is that *Connick* and *Waters* "left unclear what evidence of disruption is necessary" and fractured the circuits five ways (Pet. 9–21), Nance-Holt and Casey have a virtually insurmountable qualified-immunity defense on remand or as an alternative ground for affirmance.
2. **Unresolved *Connick* Step-One and *Mt. Healthy* Thresholds**: District Judge Kennelly held that Inendino's posts failed *Connick* step one (`753 F. Supp. 3d at 647–48`), and the unanimous Seventh Circuit panel (Judges Ripple, Lee, and Pryor) explicitly noted `"there may be room for debate"` before **assuming arguendo** that all thirteen posts addressed matters of public concern (Pet. App. 16a–20a). Where a plaintiff posted thirteen distinct items—including comments on fatal-shooting and police-assault videos (`"aim for the torso!!!"`, `"scumbag kids that you welfare fucks keep having"`), wishing the Mayor `"chokes on something"`, calling the Mayor a `"dirty hoodrat"` from `"the ghetto"`, calling Black protesters `"animals"` going `"to go get some free shit"`, and posting an `"All Lives Splatter"` car-ramming meme—both *Connick* step one and *Mt. Healthy* same-decision causation cloud whether the *Pickering* question is even outcome-determinative.
3. **Weak Split Fit and Unsympathetic Public-Safety Record**: Reading the Eighth Circuit's opinion in *Melton v. City of Forrest City*, No. 23-3288 (8th Cir. Aug. 13, 2025), via CourtListener confirms there is no square conflict on the legal standard for first responders: *Melton* expressly recognized that a `"reasonabl[e] belie[f]"` in `"the potential for disruption"` is `"usually enough when the government entity is a public-safety organization,"` reversing summary judgment only because a firefighter deleted a single misunderstood anti-abortion post immediately and the mayor's one-day inquiry and hypothetical deposition answer suggested viewpoint pretext. Here, by contrast, Inendino prominently displayed himself in Chicago Fire Department gear on a fire truck next to every public Facebook comment while serving as an EMT in predominantly Black Englewood, prompted two citizen complaints to the Office of Inspector General, underwent a 27-page OIG investigation and labor arbitration, and lost before a cross-ideological panel that *rejected* the City's unsupported internal-harmony claim (Pet. App. 22a–24a) while crediting concrete EMT patient-trust testimony (Pet. App. 25a–28a).
4. **Pending First Circuit En Banc Percolation and Recent Denials of Stronger Vehicles**: As Pet. 17 n.2 acknowledges (and CourtListener docket `68475025` confirms as of November 16, 2026), the First Circuit granted rehearing en banc and heard argument in April 2026 in *Hussey v. City of Cambridge*, 165 F.4th 638 (1st Cir. 2026), which remains pending on off-duty public-safety social-media speech and community-trust balancing. Even more tellingly, corpus priors show that just six months ago (May 18, 2026) the Court denied certiorari without recorded dissent in *Jeanne Hedgepeth v. James A. Britton*, No. 25-819 (`scotus/73279958`)—another Seventh Circuit *Pickering* Facebook termination case litigated by **Paul D. Clement** with **seven** flagship institutional amici (Cato, PLF/FIRE, Manhattan Institute, ACLJ, Liberty Justice Center, Buckeye Institute, Equal Protection Project)—after denying *MacRae v. Mattos*, No. 24-355 (`scotus/70670981`), in June 2025 after 16 distributions with only Justice Thomas writing a statement. *Inendino* is a markedly weaker, more procedurally encumbered vehicle than both *Hedgepeth* and *MacRae*.
