# Predicted Reasoning of the Court: *George Anibowei v. Todd Blanche, Attorney General, et al.* (No. 26-208)

## I. Predicted Disposition: Petition for a Writ of Certiorari Denied

The Court is predicted to **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari via a standard summary order on the order list:

> *26-208 ANIBOWEI, GEORGE V. BLANCHE, ATT'Y GEN., ET AL.*  
> *The petition for a writ of certiorari is denied.*

### 1. Further Trajectory Before Resolution (Relist and CVSG)
- **CVSG:** No Call for the Views of the Solicitor General (CVSG) will issue because the Federal Respondents (the Attorney General, the Secretary of Homeland Security, CBP, ICE, and their heads) are already parties represented by the Solicitor General, who will file a Brief in Opposition on behalf of the government.
- **Relist:** Once the Solicitor General files the Brief in Opposition (following the extension to October 19, 2026) and Petitioner files his reply brief, the petition will be distributed for a November 2026 Conference. While the most likely path (~65%) is a denial at the first conference following the Brief in Opposition, there is a meaningful (~35%) chance that the petition is relisted once or twice if Justice Gorsuch or Justice Sotomayor circulates a draft statement or dissent from denial or examines the record more closely.

### 2. Dissent or Statement Respecting Denial
- Most likely (~92%), the denial will issue without any noted dissent or separate statement, matching the Court's unannotated denials in *United States v. Cano*, 141 S. Ct. 2877 (2021) (No. 20-1043), *Merchant v. Mayorkas*, 141 S. Ct. 2858 (2021) (No. 20-1505), and *Anibowei v. Mayorkas*, 144 S. Ct. 551 (2024) (No. 23-199).
- If a separate writing respecting or dissenting from the denial of certiorari is filed (~8% conditional probability), its most likely author is **Justice Gorsuch** or **Justice Sotomayor** (potentially joined by **Justice Jackson**), highlighting the tension between *Riley v. California*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014), and lower-court decisions extending the traditional physical-luggage border-search exception of *United States v. Flores-Montano*, 541 U.S. 149 (2004), to comprehensive forensic downloads of travelers' smartphones, while noting that further percolation in the Second Circuit or a case presenting both the warrant and reasonable-suspicion questions may provide a more comprehensive vehicle.

## II. Contingency Forecast: If the Court Grants Certiorari

If the Court were to grant certiorari (14% probability):

- **Plenary Review vs. Summary Disposition:** The Court would set the case for plenary briefing and oral argument (~92% conditional probability) rather than a summary disposition (~8% conditional probability).
- **Question Presented:** The Court would take the single Question Presented—whether the Fourth Amendment permits border agents to search the digital contents of a traveler's cell phone without a warrant—though the Court could also consider whether, short of a probable-cause warrant, the Fourth Amendment requires individualized reasonable suspicion or limits border searches of electronic devices to searches for ongoing border-related contraband rather than general law-enforcement evidence gathering (the distinction drawn in *United States v. Cano*, 934 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2019)).
