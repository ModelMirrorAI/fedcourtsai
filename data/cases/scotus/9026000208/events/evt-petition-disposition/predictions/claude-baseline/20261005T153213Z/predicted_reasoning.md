# Forecast: what the Court will do with No. 26-208, Anibowei v. Blanche

**Bottom line.** The petition is more likely denied than granted, but this is a
genuinely live grant candidate: P(any grant) = 0.25. If the Court acts, it will
be plenary review of the question presented as written, not a summary
disposition.

## Procedural path from here

- **State forecast from.** The docket shows **one** distribution ("DISTRIBUTED
  for Conference of 10/9/2026", Sep 23) that has been overtaken: the government
  waived on Sep 14 and the Court **requested a response on Sep 29** (due
  Oct 29, 2026). The petition will not be considered on Oct 9.
- **Relist increment: near-certain.** After the brief in opposition (likely
  filed at or near the Oct 29 deadline, possibly after one extension into
  November or December) and a reply, the petition will be **redistributed**,
  most likely for a conference in December 2026 or January 2027. That
  redistribution alone resolves the increment; I expect it with probability
  0.97 (the residual is a dismissal or withdrawal before redistribution, which
  nothing on this docket suggests). Beyond that first redistribution I expect
  the petition to be considered at **two to four conferences** in all: a
  petition with a Court-requested response, ten cert-stage amici, and an
  acknowledged split is the kind the Court relists at least once before acting,
  whether it ends in a grant or in a denial with a separate writing.
- **CVSG: no.** The United States is the respondent and the Solicitor General
  is already counsel of record, so there is no one to invite. P = 0.01.
- **Timing.** A grant, if it comes, lands between mid-December 2026 and
  late January 2027 (argument in April 2027 at the earliest, otherwise
  OT2027). A denial would come on the same schedule, or a few weeks later if a
  Justice is writing.

## The merits of the cert question

- **Which question.** If the Court grants, it takes the QP essentially **as
  written**: "whether the Fourth Amendment permits border agents to search the
  digital contents of a traveler's cell phone without a warrant." The Fifth
  Circuit itself framed the case as turning "squarely and solely" on that
  question, and the APA posture (a challenge to the CBP and ICE directives
  rather than a suppression motion) means there is no narrower
  suppression-remedy question to retreat to. The one reformulation I would not
  rule out is a split into the manual/basic search and the forensic/advanced
  search, because the circuits divide differently on the two (6-2 on whether a
  warrant is ever required; 2-1 on whether forensic searches need individualized
  suspicion).
- **Why denial is still the modal outcome.** The Court has passed on this exact
  question repeatedly: Cano (No. 20-1043, with the United States as petitioner
  and calling the conflict entrenched), Merchant/Alasaad (No. 20-1505), and
  this petitioner's own earlier petition (No. 23-199, denied Jan 2024). The
  Solicitor General will oppose, and the brief in opposition will argue (i)
  the split has narrowed, with the Fourth Circuit in Belmonte Cardozo (2026)
  joining every other circuit on suspicionless manual searches; (ii) the
  Second Circuit has warrant-requirement appeals pending (Smith, Robinson), so
  percolation is ongoing; and (iii) vehicle problems particular to a facial APA
  challenge to agency directives: the 5 U.S.C. 704 adequate-remedy ground the
  district court relied on and the Fifth Circuit left unresolved, standing for
  prospective relief, and the facial-challenge standard (the directives already
  require reasonable suspicion for advanced searches). The Court generally
  prefers to settle Fourth Amendment rules on a concrete search in a criminal
  case, and the opinion below is a short unpublished per curiam.
- **Why the grant probability is well above the baseline band.** The Court
  called for a response after a waiver, which means at least one chambers
  wants the petition examined; ten amicus briefs at the cert stage (ABA, Cato,
  CAC, Fourth Amendment scholars, Rutherford, PPSA and others) is an unusually
  large coalition; counsel (Tutt, Jones, Elwood, Pacific Legal Foundation) are
  repeat players who choose vehicles carefully; and the Court's own recent
  work (Carpenter, and Chatrie in 2026 on geofence location data, as the
  petition recounts) shows continued appetite for extending Riley's method to
  new digital contexts. The civil, policy-level posture is a double-edged
  feature: it removes every exclusionary-rule complication, which the petition
  rightly stresses, at the cost of inviting the threshold objections above.
- **Summary disposition: very unlikely.** No intervening decision of this Court
  bears on the question, so there is nothing to GVR in light of, and the Court
  will not summarily reverse six circuits. Conditional on a grant, P(cert-order
  disposition) = 0.04; the route is briefing and argument.
- **If denied: a separate writing is plausible but not expected.** Conditional
  on denial, P(some dissent from or statement respecting denial) = 0.30. The
  three prior denials on this question drew no noted dissent, which holds the
  number down; what pushes it up is the response request, the amicus
  coalition, and a cleaner vehicle than the Court has seen before. If anyone
  writes, the likeliest author is Justice Gorsuch (a statement stressing that
  the question deserves resolution in a better vehicle) or Justice Sotomayor,
  possibly joined by one other Justice. This is context only; no cert vote is
  scored.

## Conditional merits sketch (only if granted)

If granted and argued, the likelier outcome is a ruling that at least
**forensic/advanced** searches of a phone's contents require a warrant or, at
minimum, individualized suspicion, with the Court more hesitant to require a
warrant for a manual look at the device. A ruling that the border-search
exception reaches digital contents without limit (the Fifth Circuit's rule) is
the less likely merits result given Riley and Carpenter, but this is
conditional on a grant that I rate at one in four.
