## In brief

A Texas attorney asks the Court to decide whether border agents may search the digital contents of travelers' cell phones without a warrant.

## What happened

George Anibowei, a naturalized citizen and immigration attorney, says border agents searched his cell phone without a warrant five times after he crossed into the United States, once copying all its data, which the government still holds. He sued to have federal border-search policies thrown out, arguing the Fourth Amendment requires a warrant for such searches. The district court dismissed his claim, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding its own prior rulings required it to reject the argument that warrants are generally necessary for cell phone searches at the border.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the constitutional protection against unreasonable searches requires border agents to get a warrant before examining the data stored on a traveler's cell phone, rather than relying on the long-recognized authority to search people and belongings at the border without one.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference on October 9, 2026. After that conference, the Court asked for a response, due October 29, 2026; the docket does not show any further action since then.

## What each outcome would mean

Anibowei, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear the case in full and decide whether warrantless cell phone searches at the border are constitutional, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's ruling against Anibowei stands, and border agents in that circuit may continue warrantless searches under existing policy; a denial would not mean the Court agrees that ruling was correct, only that it lets it stand.
