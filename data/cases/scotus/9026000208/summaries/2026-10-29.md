## In brief

An immigration attorney asks the Court to decide whether border agents may search a traveler's cell phone without a warrant.

## What happened

George Anibowei, a naturalized citizen and immigration lawyer, had his phone seized and copied by border agents in 2016 without a warrant, and agents searched it four more times since. He sued, eventually pursuing only a claim that government directives allowing these searches violate the Constitution. The district court dismissed the claim, reasoning Anibowei had an adequate alternative remedy and, alternatively, that circuit precedent permits such searches. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding its prior decisions foreclosed Anibowei's argument that a warrant is required.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Fourth Amendment's usual warrant requirement applies when border agents want to examine the data stored on a traveler's cell phone, or whether the traditional rule allowing warrantless searches at the border extends to a phone's digital contents.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference; the federal government waived its right to respond, but the Court then asked for a response, due November 30, 2026 after an extension. Numerous amicus briefs have been filed supporting Anibowei.

## What each outcome would mean

Anibowei, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will decide the warrant question itself, deciding nothing in advance for either side. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling stands, leaving in place the rule that border agents in that circuit may search phones without a warrant, and Anibowei's challenge to the directives fails.
