## In brief

George Anibowei asks the Court to decide whether border agents need a warrant to search the digital contents of a traveler's cell phone.

## What happened

Anibowei, a naturalized citizen and immigration lawyer, had his phone seized and copied by border agents in 2016 and searched several more times since, without a warrant. He sued the federal government, challenging agency policies that allow such searches, under the law governing review of agency actions. The district court dismissed his claim, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding that its own prior decisions already established that the Fourth Amendment does not generally require a warrant for cell phone searches at the border.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether the rule allowing searches at the border without a warrant extends to the data stored on a traveler's phone, or whether, given how much personal information a phone holds, agents must instead get a warrant before examining its contents.

## Where it stands

The federal government waived its right to respond to the petition, and numerous outside groups filed briefs supporting Anibowei. The Court asked for a response anyway, and the government has since requested more time to file it; the case was distributed for the October 9, 2026 conference, and the docket does not yet show further action.

## What each outcome would mean

Anibowei, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse that ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether a warrant is required, resolving a disagreement among federal appeals courts on the question. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, meaning border agents in that circuit may continue searching travelers' phones without a warrant, and Anibowei's challenge to the policies ends.
