## In brief

An immigration attorney asks the Court to decide whether border agents may search a traveler's cell phone without a warrant.

## What happened

George Anibowei, a naturalized citizen and immigration attorney, had his cell phone seized and searched without a warrant by border agents at least five times since 2016, including data copied from his phone that the government still holds. He sued, challenging CBP and ICE policies that allow warrantless phone searches at the border. The district court dismissed his claim, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding that circuit precedent forecloses any argument that the Fourth Amendment requires a warrant for cell phone searches at the border.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Fourth Amendment's usual rule against searches without a warrant applies to the digital contents of a traveler's cell phone when agents search it at the border, an area where searches have traditionally been allowed without a warrant. Anibowei's petition says federal appeals courts are split: most hold no warrant is ever needed, while two require one in some circumstances, and he argues the Court's reasoning in a past case about searches of phones should extend to the border.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed, the government waived its right to respond, and several outside groups filed briefs supporting Anibowei. The Court distributed the case for its October 9, 2026 conference but then asked for a response, due October 29, 2026; the government has since asked for more time to file it. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Anibowei, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether a warrant is required for phone searches at the border, resolving a dispute among federal appeals courts; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling against Anibowei stands, and the government's warrantless search policies remain in effect in that circuit, leaving the split among appeals courts unresolved for now.
