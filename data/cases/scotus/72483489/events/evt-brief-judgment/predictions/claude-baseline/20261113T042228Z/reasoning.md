## Adjustments up
- The Second Circuit is, on the petitioner's account (12–1) and effectively on the SG's, an outlier in calling inherent strength a legal question; the SG's cert-stage brief said outright that the court of appeals "erred," while recommending denial on vehicle grounds. The Court granted anyway, which usually signals a wish to correct.
- *Hana Financial* (unanimous, 2015) says consumer-impression questions go to the factfinder; *Booking.com* ties distinctiveness to consumer perception. The doctrinal path to disturbing is short.
- The United States supports vacatur at the merits stage.
- The respondent's brief itself offers a fallback of vacatur (final footnote), a sign of where it thinks the risk lies.
- Most amici filed "in support of neither party," none plainly for the respondent on the caption.

## Adjustments down
- PepsiCo (Kirkland; Michel/Cendali) reframes well: inherent strength is not the same as distinctiveness, it is about scope of protection, and the petitioner did not challenge the holding that the ultimate likelihood-of-confusion question is legal. The Court could hold the sub-factor has a legal component and affirm, or affirm on the "no reasonable jury" ground (as in *Monasky*, which the respondent cites).
- The decision below is an unpublished summary order resting partly on law-of-the-case from the 2022 published opinion and on an unchallenged dissimilarity finding; that makes harmless-error affirmance or a DIG more plausible than usual. The SG's cert-stage vehicle objections point the same way.
- Neutral amici (law scholars, IPO) appear to voice concern about sending more trademark cases to juries.
