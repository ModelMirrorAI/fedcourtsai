## Why modestly above the anchor

**The core error is unusually exposed.** The decision below matches the petition's judgment date, parties, and Second Circuit docket 23-1176. At pp. 5–6 it explicitly calls inherent strength a legal question and treats the earlier panel's assessment of RISE as binding law. That is not merely an adverse factual inference. Rise's merits brief, pp. 10–13, 21–24, persuasively ties inherent strength to how ordinary consumers understand a mark. Its reliance on *Hana Financial, Inc. v. Hana Bank*, 574 U.S. 418, 422–425 (2015), is stronger than an abstract demand for juries: that decision assigns a comparable consumer-impression inquiry to the factfinder, while expressly preserving judgment as a matter of law. I independently checked the opinion text. The law/fact methodology in *U.S. Bank N.A. v. Village at Lakeridge, LLC*, 583 U.S. 387, 395–396 (2018), also appears in both sides' briefing.

**PepsiCo's competing characterization is substantial but not controlling.** Its merits brief, pp. 18–35, treats inherent strength as the legal scope of an exclusionary right, distinguishing that scope from initial validity and acquired consumer recognition. Patent claim construction, competition, and speech concerns make this more than a semantic defense. But trademark infringement asks about marketplace confusion, not the construction of an issued patent instrument. Judges can define the legal framework without converting its application to a particular mark into a pure legal question. Rise's tendency to equate all conceptual strength with placement on the distinctiveness spectrum is nevertheless an overstatement the majority can avoid endorsing.

**The main downside is affirmance despite correction.** PepsiCo's merits brief, pp. 51–56, identifies serious alternative grounds: the ordinary association of RISE with caffeinated energy, extensive third-party use, weak evidence of acquired strength, dissimilar packaging, and unchallenged aspects of the other confusion factors. The decision below, pp. 8–10, itself applies a reasonable-jury formulation to parts of the analysis. Thus agreement with Rise on the question presented is not the same as a 100% chance of disturbing the judgment. The lower court's overall analysis, however, repeatedly relies on the legally prescribed weakness of the mark when assessing its narrow protection. Vacating for the Second Circuit to reconsider under a corrected framework is more natural than this Court determining the entire record itself. Vacatur would not award Rise damages or require a jury trial.

These competing considerations keep the forecast at 76%, rather than translating the strong doctrinal case into a near-certain petitioner victory. The retained undisturbed probability includes a genuine harmless-error/alternative-ground affirmance path and a small vehicle-related DIG risk, not a second cert prediction.
