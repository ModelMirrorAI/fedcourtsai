## In brief

The Court affirmed a lower court ruling that struck down President Trump's executive order denying automatic citizenship to children born in the United States to certain noncitizen parents.

## What happened

President Trump signed an executive order in January 2025 declaring that children born in the United States would not receive automatic citizenship if their mother lacked permanent immigration status and their father was neither a citizen nor a lawful permanent resident. A group of affected babies and their parents, identified by pseudonyms including "Barbara," sued in New Hampshire federal court and won a preliminary injunction blocking the order, based on both the Constitution's citizenship guarantee and a federal citizenship statute. While the government's appeal was pending, the First Circuit Court of Appeals ruled against the government in a related case, unanimously holding the order unlawful on both grounds. The government then asked the Supreme Court to hear this case before the appeals court ruled on it.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal statute and a constitutional provision guaranteeing citizenship to people born in the United States protect children born here to parents who are not citizens or permanent residents, or whether the government may treat such children as not automatically becoming citizens at birth.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the government's petition in December 2025, heard argument in April 2026, and on June 30, 2026 issued a decision affirming the lower court's ruling against the executive order, with several justices writing separate opinions.

## What each outcome would mean

The Trump administration, as petitioner, asked the Court to uphold the executive order restricting birthright citizenship. The Court's affirmance means the lower court's injunction against the order stands: the families and children who sued, along with others in the same position, keep their citizenship rights, and the order cannot be enforced as written. Had the Court instead reversed, the executive order could have taken effect, denying citizenship to children of the parents it targets. A vacate-and-remand, which did not happen here, would have sent the case back without resolving the question for either side.
