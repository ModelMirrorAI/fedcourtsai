## Why this petition is stronger than the baseline

The question presented is concrete: whether federal common law or state law supplies equitable-estoppel rules when a nonsignatory seeks to enforce an arbitration agreement governed by the New York Convention. The provisioned QP and petition describe GE Energy as allowing nonsignatory enforcement without deciding the governing-law issue. The petition presents a **claimed 4–1 split**, identifying the First, Second, Fourth, and Ninth Circuits on the federal-law side. Its discussions of Smith/Enron, InterGen, Setty, and Aggarao appear at petition pages 16–20. These are petitioners' characterizations; independent verification was unavailable.

The appendix gives firmer evidence of the other side: the Fifth Circuit's opinion, appendix pages 9a–11a, expressly applies state contract law to nonsignatory estoppel and treats Louisiana's prohibition as defeating that theory. Thus the petition is not merely asking for factbound correction of an insurer's loss. Choosing the governing body of law potentially affects international arbitration outside Louisiana insurance disputes. The response request is a real act of attention, but not a finding that the alleged split warrants plenary review.

## Reasons not to make grant the modal outcome

The Fifth Circuit first holds that each insurer had a separate contract and that the remaining domestic insurers' own arbitration agreements lacked a foreign party; the foreign insurers had been dismissed. Its distinct estoppel analysis then rejects the effort to enforce the foreign insurers' agreements through Louisiana law. These are **distinct theories**, not proof that correcting the estoppel choice-of-law holding could never change the result. Nevertheless, they make the case less straightforward than an ordinary international contract with an undisputed Convention gateway. The delegation argument also fails under the lower court's contract-validity analysis. These points come from the appendix's appellate opinion, not an imagined opposition.

The petition's own examples also qualify the conflict: InterGen involved binding a nonsignatory, Setty's quoted rule addresses arbitrability of federal claims, and Aggarao is described as applying federal principles. Moreover, the First and Ninth Circuit examples denied enforcement even under federal law. Selecting federal law therefore does not itself establish that these insurers can compel arbitration. Petition pages 21–23 rely on allegations of concerted conduct and an analogy to Bufkin; the appendix explains that Bufkin had predicted Louisiana law rather than squarely established an independent federal governing-law rule. Petition page 35 asserts preservation, dispositiveness, and an ideal vehicle; those assertions deserve weight but are advocacy, not neutral findings of an uncomplicated case.

The seven consolidated appeals and the Louisiana insurance-law overlay add complexity. I do not assert a jurisdictional defect, a forfeiture, or an independent unreviewable ground, because the record I read establishes none. My discount is for uncertainty over comparability and vehicle cleanliness, not for a made-up bar to review.

- **New CVSG, 0.08:** the treaty and federal implementation provide a possible federal-interest rationale, but there is no federal party and ordinary adversarial briefing is the more likely next step. No Solicitor General recommendation is on this record.
- **Summary route conditional on grant, 0.08:** resolving a contested governing-law question usually calls for briefing and argument, and nothing in this record identifies a new intervening decision requiring a mechanical GVR. This is a conditional probability, not 8% of all outcomes.
- **Dissent or statement conditional on denial, 0.06:** an unexplained denial is much more likely than a separate writing. The per-Justice order-writer probabilities are unconditional, independent marginals, not votes or a distribution summing to one. Their small total permits overlap and occasional writings on grant-side orders; Thomas and Gorsuch receive the largest, still small, probabilities as judgmental possibilities rather than evidence of any known interest in this petition.

I set **big_case_score to 0.48** for moderately broad commercial-law stakes: the legal rule could matter across international arbitration, while the immediate dispute concerns specialized insurance contracts and does not resolve the underlying loss claims. That number measures importance if decided, not grant likelihood.
