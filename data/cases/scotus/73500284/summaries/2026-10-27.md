## In brief

American insurers ask the Court to decide whether federal or state law governs when non-signatory insurers can use equitable estoppel to compel arbitration under an international treaty.

## What happened

Louisiana public entities sued American insurers over disputed hurricane-damage payments under property insurance policies that also included two foreign insurers. The policies contained arbitration clauses, but Louisiana law bars arbitration clauses in insurance policies. The American insurers argued they could still compel arbitration under an international treaty covering foreign arbitration agreements, by invoking equitable estoppel to enforce the foreign insurers' arbitration agreements, even though the public entities had dropped or never sued the foreign insurers. The district court and the Fifth Circuit rejected this, ruling that each insurer had a separate contract with each public entity, that no foreign party remained in the dispute, and that Louisiana law—not federal common law—determined whether equitable estoppel could apply, barring it here.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in cases governed by this international arbitration treaty, courts should apply a uniform federal rule or each state's own law when deciding if a company that did not sign an arbitration agreement can still force the other side into arbitration on the theory that its claims are bound up with a signer's claims.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief in opposition and a reply, and was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026; it was then distributed again for the conference of November 6, 2026, with no action on it recorded since.

## What each outcome would mean

The insurers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the lower court's ruling that state law governs. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Louisiana law controls and the insurers cannot compel arbitration against the public entities through equitable estoppel. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide which body of law applies; a decision for the insurers would make a uniform federal rule govern such disputes, while a decision for the public entities would leave state law in control. The Court could also grant review and send the case back without deciding the question itself.
