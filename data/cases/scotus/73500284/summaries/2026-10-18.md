## In brief

A group of insurers asks the Court to decide whether federal or state law governs non-signatories' use of estoppel to enforce arbitration agreements under an international treaty.

## What happened

Several Louisiana public entities—a town, a parish school board, a recreation district, and a police jury—sued insurers after hurricanes damaged their property, claiming the insurers underpaid their claims. Each entity had separate insurance contracts with eight American insurers and two foreign insurers, but the foreign insurers were dismissed or never sued. The American insurers sought to force arbitration by invoking the foreign insurers' arbitration agreements through "equitable estoppel," a doctrine letting a non-signatory enforce a contract in some circumstances. Louisiana law bars arbitration clauses in insurance policies, and the Louisiana Supreme Court held that estoppel cannot override that rule. The district court and the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals both ruled against the insurers, holding that state law, not federal common law, controls and blocks arbitration here.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, under a treaty governing international arbitration agreements, courts should apply a single federal rule for deciding when a company that didn't sign an arbitration agreement can still force arbitration based on estoppel, or whether each state's own contract law controls that question instead.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference after the respondents waived their right to respond, but the Court then asked for a response, which was filed, followed by amicus briefs and a reply from the petitioners. The case has not yet been scheduled for a new conference in the record shown.

## What each outcome would mean

Indian Harbor Insurance Company and the other insurers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide which body of law governs, with no outcome favored by the grant itself. If the Court denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's ruling stands, meaning state law continues to control in that circuit, and these insurers cannot use estoppel to force the Louisiana public entities into arbitration under the foreign insurers' agreements.
