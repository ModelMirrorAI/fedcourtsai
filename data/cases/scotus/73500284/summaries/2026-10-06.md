## In brief

Insurers ask the Court to decide whether federal or state law governs when a company can use arbitration agreements belonging to someone else.

## What happened

Four Louisiana public entities bought hurricane insurance from several American and two foreign insurers, with each insurer holding a separate contract. After hurricanes damaged their property, the public entities sued the American insurers in state court, alleging mishandled claims. The insurers tried to force arbitration under an international treaty, arguing they could borrow the foreign insurers' arbitration clauses even though no live claims existed against those foreign insurers. Louisiana law bars arbitration clauses in insurance policies, and the Louisiana Supreme Court has held that insurers cannot use a contract-law doctrine called equitable estoppel to get around that rule. The Fifth Circuit agreed, applying Louisiana law and rejecting arbitration. The insurers argue this conflicts with rulings from four other appeals courts that apply federal rules instead.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in international arbitration cases, courts should apply a uniform federal rule or each state's own law to decide if a company that did not sign an arbitration agreement can still rely on it against someone who did. The insurers say federal rules should control to keep international arbitration consistent; the public entities respond that no real conflict exists among the lower courts and that ordinary state contract law should apply, as this Court's precedent allows.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026, after the respondents filed a brief in opposition and the petitioners replied. The docket shows no action taken on the petition since that distribution.

## What each outcome would mean

Indian Harbor Insurance Co. and the other insurer petitioners ask the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide which body of law governs; a grant would not by itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision applying Louisiana law stands, leaving the public entities' lawsuits in court rather than arbitration. Either way, the decision affects only whether the Court takes the case, not the outcome of the underlying insurance dispute.
