## In brief

A group of insurers asks the Court to decide whether federal or state law governs when a non-signatory can use equitable estoppel to force arbitration under an international arbitration treaty.

## What happened

Several Louisiana public entities sued their American property insurers, claiming underpayment of hurricane damage claims. Each entity's policy involved both American and foreign insurers, but a contract clause stated each insurer had a separate agreement with the policyholder. The American insurers sought to compel arbitration by invoking the foreign insurers' arbitration clauses through "equitable estoppel," a doctrine letting a non-signatory sometimes enforce or be bound by another's contract. Lower courts rejected this, applying Louisiana law barring arbitration clauses in insurance policies. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding state law, not federal common law, governs the estoppel question, and that Louisiana law blocked the insurers' approach.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, when an arbitration agreement falls under an international treaty on enforcing arbitration awards, courts should apply a uniform federal rule for deciding if a company that did not sign the agreement can still invoke it, or instead apply the law of the state where the case arises, which in Louisiana disfavors arbitration in insurance contracts.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, with a brief in opposition and a reply filed, and the case has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for November 6, 2026. No order on the petition has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Indian Harbor Insurance Company and the other insurers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling, which favored the Town of Vinton and other Louisiana public entities. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide whether federal or state law applies to this estoppel question, which could determine whether the insurers can force arbitration instead of litigation. If the Court denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, leaving the public entities free to pursue their claims in court rather than arbitration.
