## In brief

American insurers ask the Court to decide whether federal or state law governs when a non-signatory can use equitable estoppel to force arbitration under an international treaty.

## What happened

Several Louisiana public entities sued American insurers over disputed hurricane-damage payments on surplus-lines insurance policies that also involved two foreign insurers. The policies' contract terms made each insurer's agreement a separate contract with the policyholder, and under those terms, no foreign party was a signatory to the American insurers' arbitration clauses. The American insurers tried to invoke the Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, a treaty requiring enforcement of certain arbitration agreements, by arguing that equitable estoppel let them enforce the foreign insurers' arbitration clauses instead. Louisiana courts had ruled equitable estoppel cannot override Louisiana's ban on arbitration clauses in insurance policies. The Fifth Circuit agreed with the lower courts, holding state law, not federal common law, controls this kind of estoppel question, and affirmed denial of arbitration.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the rules governing when a company that did not sign an arbitration agreement may still invoke it under this treaty should come from federal common law or from the law of the state where the dispute arose. The insurers argue federal law should apply to keep international arbitration rules uniform nationwide; the public entities argue state contract law should continue to govern, as it does under similar domestic arbitration law.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply, and was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026. The docket shows the case was "rescheduled" on November 3, 2026, with no indication yet of further action.

## What each outcome would mean

The insurers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling, which left in place the lower court decisions denying arbitration. If the Court grants review and hears the case, it would decide which body of law governs this estoppel question, with no outcome assumed in advance; if it later ruled for the insurers, the public entities could be required to arbitrate rather than litigate their claims. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, meaning the public entities' lawsuits against the American insurers continue in court rather than arbitration.
