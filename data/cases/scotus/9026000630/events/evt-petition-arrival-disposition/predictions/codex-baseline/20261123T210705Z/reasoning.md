The Court granted *Rhoney* on October 1, six weeks before this petition was docketed. Its published question asks whether 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b)(2)(A) mandates detention pending removal for a person already in the United States who has never been admitted. The First Circuit’s judgment here turns on precisely that question. In *Padula v. Cirrus Rojas*, another petition from a loss on the same issue, the Solicitor General asked the Court to hold the petition for *Rhoney* and then dispose of it in light of the decision. [Rhoney question presented](https://www.supremecourt.gov/qp/26-00104qp.pdf) · [Padula petition, pp. 11–12](https://www.justice.gov/sites/default/files/2026-11/cirrus_rojas_final.pdf)

The direction of the ruling in *Rhoney* drives the number. Section 1225(a)(1) expressly deems someone present without admission an “applicant for admission.” Judge Dunlap’s dissent explains why that status could carry through to the mandatory detention provision even years after entry. The Fifth and Eighth Circuits have accepted the government’s reading. I give more weight to that route, which would undercut the First Circuit’s basis for requiring a bond hearing. But the majority points to the statute’s additional requirement that an applicant be “seeking admission.” Guerrero Orellana had entered about thirteen years before his arrest in Massachusetts. The majority read that language and the separate bond authority in § 1226(a) to preserve eligibility for a hearing. If *Rhoney* adopts that reading, denial would leave a consistent judgment in place. A narrower ruling could still require the First Circuit to reconsider the class relief. [Guerrero Orellana v. Moniz, Nos. 25-2152 and 26-1094, slip op. pp. 4, 9–11, 19–21, 29–30, 55–58](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10947821/guerrero-orellana-v-moniz/)
