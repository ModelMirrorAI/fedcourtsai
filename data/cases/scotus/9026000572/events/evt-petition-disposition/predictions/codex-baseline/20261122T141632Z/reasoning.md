My modest downward adjustment from 73.1% reflects a substantial path to denial even though the Court has agreed to resolve the split. The Seventh Circuit and the Second Circuit in *Rhoney* both rejected mandatory detention on this ground. The government's best argument is that § 1225(a)(1) deems a person present without admission an applicant for admission, and that § 1225(b)(2)(A) commands detention of such applicants pending removal proceedings. Judge Sykes's dissent explains how that fits the description of the provision as a catchall in *Jennings v. Rodriguez*. The Fifth and Eighth Circuits adopted the government's reading. I give a modest edge to a ruling that changes the law the Seventh Circuit applied or qualifies it enough to require another look. The strongest reason for denial is the majority's textual point that the detention provision also says “seeking admission.” If *Rhoney* gives those words independent effect and preserves bond eligibility for people in Cirrus Rojas's position under § 1226(a), the Court can leave his judgment in place. (Petition pp. 10–11 and App. 3a n.1, 14a–19a, 39a–47a. The staged decision-below.txt matches the July 30 judgment in No. 25-3127.)

There is also a practical reason to avoid a second argument. Cirrus Rojas was released on bond. The Seventh Circuit held that the government's stated intent to detain him again kept the dispute live, and on remand the district court ordered a bond hearing to the extent one had not already occurred. Changes in his removal proceedings could affect the relief still available. The clearest uncertainty is the scope of the lead ruling. Even a decision that leaves some bond claims open could justify sending this judgment back. (Petition App. 7a–8a, 82a–83a.)
