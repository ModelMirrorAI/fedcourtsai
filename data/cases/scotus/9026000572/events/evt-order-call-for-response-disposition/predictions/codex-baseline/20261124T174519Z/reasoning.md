The judgments in the Second and Seventh Circuits both went against the government's detention policy. Section 1225(a)(1) treats a person present without admission as an applicant for admission. Judge Sykes's dissent identifies the government's strongest point in the lead case. Congress made that definition applicable throughout the chapter, and *Jennings v. Rodriguez*, 583 U.S. 281, 287 (2018), described § 1225(b)(2) as the catchall for applicants outside § 1225(b)(1), subject to specified exceptions. That gives the Court a textual path to a ruling that would require the Seventh Circuit to reconsider. *Jennings* addressed detention once a person falls within the statute. The scope of the words “seeking admission” remains disputed. Judge Kolar's majority gave those words independent effect for someone arrested in the interior years after entry. Decades of agency practice and decisions in several other circuits support that reading. The majority's due process discussion concerned constitutional avoidance, so its judgment turns on the statutory question the lead case presents. The panel also found the controversy live despite Cirrus Rojas's release on bond because DHS intended to detain him again. A later change in his removal proceedings could affect whether a remand would accomplish anything. (Petition pp. 6–11 and App. 7a–8a, 37a–47a, 82a–83a.
