I put the probability of any grant at 71%, almost all of it on an order granting review, vacating the Seventh Circuit’s judgment and remanding (GVR). The strongest evidence is the relief the Solicitor General actually requests. Pages 11–12 of the petition ask the Court to hold this case for *Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha*, No. 26-104. The Court granted that petition on October 1, before this one was filed. Both appellate judgments reject mandatory detention for people arrested inside the country after entering without admission. A ruling in *Rhoney* favoring the government, or drawing a new boundary for mandatory detention, would give the Seventh Circuit reason to reconsider. If the Court endorses the lower courts’ reading, denial here would leave the Seventh Circuit’s judgment in place.

The principal uncertainty is how much of the Seventh Circuit’s reasoning survives *Rhoney*. The government can point to § 1225(a)(1), which deems a person present without admission an applicant for admission for purposes of the immigration statute, and to *Jennings v. Rodriguez*’s description of § 1225(b)(2) as a catchall. Judge Sykes adopted that reading in dissent, and the Fifth and Eighth Circuits accepted it. I favor at least some reconsideration on that record. The contrary reading has substantial support. Judge Kolar’s majority gave “seeking admission” independent force, found Cirrus Rojas eligible for a bond hearing under § 1226(a), and noted the government’s longstanding practice of providing those hearings. The majority’s due-process concerns reinforced its statutory analysis. The panel found a live dispute despite his release because DHS intended to detain him again if the bond decision was reversed. His individual custody and removal proceedings leave some risk that circumstances change during a hold. I therefore put the grant chance slightly below the federal anchor despite the close fit with *Rhoney*.
