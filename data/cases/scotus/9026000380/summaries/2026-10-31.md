The federal government asks the Court whether a detained noncitizen may challenge his detention in habeas when his claims also attack his removal's validity.

## What happened

Badar Khan Suri, a researcher at Georgetown University, was detained by immigration authorities after the Secretary of State determined his presence posed foreign-policy concerns. Suri filed a habeas petition claiming his detention was retaliation for his speech and associations, violating the First and Fifth Amendments. A federal district court ordered him released and blocked his removal while the case proceeded. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, rejecting the government's argument that immigration law stripped the district court of jurisdiction, while acknowledging its ruling conflicted with decisions from the Third and Second Circuits.

## What the Court is being asked

Immigration law generally requires that challenges to a removal be brought only through a specific review process in the courts of appeals, not through a habeas lawsuit in district court. The Court is asked whether that rule also blocks a detained person from using habeas to challenge his detention, when the arguments he raises would also undercut the legality of removing him.

## Where it stands

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, which lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse. If the Court denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Suri's release and the block on his removal remain in effect, and detained noncitizens in that circuit could bring similar habeas challenges. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the jurisdictional question itself, ruling for neither side simply by agreeing to hear it.
