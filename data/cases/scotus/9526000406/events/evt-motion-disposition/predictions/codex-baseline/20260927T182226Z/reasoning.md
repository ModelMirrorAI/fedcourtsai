## Why the forecast moves to 86%

The strongest upward evidence is unusually close procedural history already disclosed in the application. It reports that the Court stayed the preliminary injunction on June 23, 2025 and clarified on July 3, 2025 that enforcement orders could not circumvent that stay (pp. 13–14). Those are earlier proceedings under 24A1153, not the disposition being predicted under 26A406. They are legitimate pre-decision evidence supplied in the record. I neither sought nor encountered this target application's outcome, and I do not claim independent verification of its present docket state.

The government now challenges a final judgment resting partly on similar restrictions. According to its brief, the First Circuit largely affirmed on September 18, 2026, then dissolved its stay on September 23 before issuance of the mandate (pp. 15–16). The government alleges concrete flight cancellations, removal delays, and diplomatic disruption, including an already canceled flight carrying 70 people (pp. 2, 38–40). These are applicant assertions, not independently established facts. If credited, however, they strengthen the urgency argument beyond an abstract disagreement with the judgment.

The main legal route is the government's argument that the revised declaratory relief and vacatur still restrain operation of the removal statutes, alongside its challenges to district-court jurisdiction and the derivation of procedural rights under section 1231 (pp. 17–31). I expect enough Justices to view those issues as warranting review and interim preservation of the operational status quo. The general stay framework is verified in *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010), through CourtListener: a reasonable prospect of certiorari, a fair prospect of reversal, likely irreparable harm, and relative harms in close cases. That framework does not make a stay automatic for the government.

## Why not nearer certainty

An earlier preliminary stay is not a final merits ruling. The final judgment changes the remedial form and adds statutory grounds. The application itself acknowledges that *Biden v. Texas* reserved the reach of section 1252(f)(1) over declaratory relief and vacatur (p. 19). The First Circuit's reasoning, as described in the brief, distinguishes a challenge to third-country procedures from review of the removal order itself and invokes statutory protection obligations and constitutional avoidance (pp. 24–30, 36–37). Those distinctions could matter.

The strongest equitable counterweight is the risk of removal to persecution or torture before a meaningful opportunity to contest a new destination. I do not adopt the applicant's assertion that respondents face no cognizable harm: the adequacy of assurances and the opportunity to raise destination-specific fears are disputed. A final record and a response could support denial or narrower relief. I reserve 14% for all ungranted outcomes, principally denial and mixed relief, with withdrawal or dismissal much less likely. The numeric adjustment is a judgment informed by the unusually close prior proceedings, not a fitted subgroup rate or a measured federal-applicant effect.
