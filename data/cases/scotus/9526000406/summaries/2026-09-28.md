## What happened

DHS has a policy letting it deport people to countries other than the one named in their removal order, if that country is willing to accept them. When the receiving country has given assurances it will not persecute or torture anyone, DHS may proceed without further steps; otherwise it gives notice and a chance to raise a fear claim. D.V.D. and other immigrants with final removal orders sued, arguing the policy lacks adequate notice and process. A federal district court in Massachusetts ruled for them, declaring the policy unlawful and setting requirements for notice and hearings. The First Circuit largely affirmed. The government had previously won stays from the Supreme Court of similar orders in this same case.

## What the Court is being asked

DHS is asking the Court to pause the lower court's ruling while it appeals. The application raises whether federal courts have authority to issue broad, class-wide orders limiting how the government carries out third-country removals, whether these claims can be reviewed in this kind of lawsuit rather than through the normal immigration appeals process, and whether the Constitution or immigration law requires more individualized notice and hearing procedures than the government's policy already provides before someone is sent to a third country.

## Where it stands

The application for a stay was submitted to Justice Jackson on September 24, 2026. That same day, Justice Jackson requested a response from the respondents, due by September 28, 2026, the snapshot date. No ruling on the stay request has yet been recorded.
