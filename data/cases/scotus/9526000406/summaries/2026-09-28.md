## In brief

The federal government asks the Supreme Court to pause a court order limiting how it removes immigrants to countries not named in their removal orders.

## What happened

Four immigrants with final removal orders sued, claiming they were denied adequate notice and a chance to raise fears of persecution or torture before being sent to "third countries" not listed in their orders. A district court ruled for them, declaring the government's policy unlawful and requiring more procedural steps before such removals. The First Circuit largely affirmed that judgment. The First Circuit had initially paused the order while the appeal proceeded, but late on September 23, 2026, it lifted that pause without giving the government a chance to respond, letting the district court's order take immediate effect. The government says this is the third time it has had to seek emergency relief in this case, having twice already won stays from the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The government wants the Supreme Court to pause the lower court's order while it prepares to ask the Court to fully review the case. It argues that federal law strips lower courts of power to issue broad, class-wide orders limiting these removals, that other laws route such disputes exclusively through a different review process, and that neither the Constitution nor immigration statutes require the extra notice-and-hearing procedures the lower courts imposed before removing someone to a third country.

## Where it stands

This is an emergency application submitted to Justice Jackson, who has asked the immigrants' side to respond by September 28, 2026. No ruling on the stay request has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as applicant, is asking the Court to pause the district court's order. If granted, the government could resume third-country removals under its existing policy while the case continues in the lower courts, without the additional notice and hearing procedures the district court required. If denied, that order would remain in effect, requiring the government to follow the added procedures before removing class members to third countries. Either way, the pause decides nothing about who is ultimately right; that question would be resolved later, including through any subsequent request that the Court hear the case fully.
