## In brief

The Supreme Court stayed a district court order limiting third-country deportations and agreed to hear the government's full appeal in December.

## What happened

DHS adopted guidance letting it deport people with final removal orders to countries other than those named in their case, sometimes with only brief notice and a chance to raise fears of torture or persecution. Several immigrants challenged the policy in a class action; the district court ruled the guidance unlawful, ordered DHS to give greater notice and more time to raise fears, and vacated the policy nationwide. The First Circuit largely affirmed, though it rejected one claim for lack of standing. After the First Circuit briefly dissolved its own stay of that ruling, DHS asked the Supreme Court to step in, arguing the lower courts lacked authority to grant such broad relief and that the guidance is lawful.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court will decide whether the district court had authority to hear this lawsuit at all, whether it could issue relief covering the whole class of affected immigrants rather than just individuals, and whether the guidance on deporting people to third countries violates immigration law, constitutional due process protections, or treaty-based torture protections.

## Where it stands

The Court granted DHS's request to pause the district court's order while the case proceeds, and treated the application itself as a petition for review, which it granted. The case will be briefed and argued in December 2026; three justices noted they would have denied the stay.

## What each outcome would mean

DHS, as applicant, sought the stay and asked the Court to take up the case. The stay the Court granted means DHS's third-country removal guidance stays in effect, and the district court's order requiring added notice and procedures is paused, while the case continues. Because the Court also granted review, it will later decide the case on the merits: affirming would leave the lower courts' limits on the guidance in place, reversing would uphold DHS's guidance without those added procedures, and vacating would send the case back without resolving the dispute for either side.
