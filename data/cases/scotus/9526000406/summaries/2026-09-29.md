## In brief

The government asks the Supreme Court to pause a court order limiting how it removes immigrants to countries other than those named in their removal orders.

## What happened

Four immigrants with final removal orders sued, arguing they needed more notice and a chance to raise fears of torture or persecution before being sent to a "third country" not listed in their case. A federal district court in Massachusetts agreed, ordering the Department of Homeland Security to provide written notice, a waiting period, and a way to reopen proceedings. The Supreme Court twice paused earlier versions of this order. The district court then issued a final judgment largely repeating its earlier requirements, and the First Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed. The First Circuit briefly paused that judgment but then lifted the pause overnight, without letting the government respond, prompting this request.

## What the Court is being asked

The government asks the Court to pause the lower court's order while the case continues. It argues that federal law bars courts from issuing broad, class-wide orders limiting how the government executes removals, that courts lack authority to review these particular claims at all, and that the notice and waiting-period rules the lower courts required go beyond what the law or the Constitution demands. The immigrants dispute all of this, though their response is not detailed here.

## Where it stands

This is an emergency application. Justice Jackson, who handles emergency matters from this circuit, asked the immigrants to respond, and they did, along with supporting briefs from outside groups on both sides. The docket does not yet show a ruling on the stay request.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as applicant, is asking the Court to pause the lower court's order. If granted, the government could resume removing people to third countries under its existing policy while the case continues, without providing the additional notice and waiting periods the lower courts required. If denied, the district court's order requiring that extra notice and process would remain in effect while the case proceeds. Either way, this ruling would be temporary and would not decide the underlying legal questions.
