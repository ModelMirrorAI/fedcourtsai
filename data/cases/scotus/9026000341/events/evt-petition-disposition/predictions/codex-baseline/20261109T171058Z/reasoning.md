## Questions presented and reasons not to go higher

**Question 1: the securities-transaction nexus.** Petitioners ask whether nonpublic statements to FINRA or DTC can satisfy the Section 10(b)/Rule 10b-5 connection requirement without evidence that investors received them. Their petition, especially pages 17–25, alleges conflict with dissemination and transactional-nexus decisions from several circuits. That framing presents an important limit on enforcement against securities-market intermediaries.

But the matched January 16, 2026 Eleventh Circuit opinion does not rest merely on statements to a regulator being securities-related. At approximately pages 42–46, it treats the misrepresentations as integral to obtaining quotation clearance, notes Spartan's subsequent market-making, and separately identifies evidence that investors could access the forms. It also cites authority recognizing a transaction nexus when intermediaries, rather than investors directly, receive the misrepresentation. Those factual and alternative grounds weaken the petition's categorical nonpublic-statement premise. The cited public-dissemination decisions are not automatically conflicting holdings about the same gatekeeping conduct in an SEC enforcement action. This looks less clean than a square, acknowledged conflict on identical facts.

**Question 2: disgorgement without identified victims, paid to the Treasury.** The petition's pages 26–33 distinguish lack of financially injured investors from the absence of any legally cognizable victim, and challenge the statutory and equitable basis for Treasury-directed disgorgement. Its discussion of Hallam, Ahmed, Govil and the Ninth Circuit identifies competing approaches to Section 78u(d)(7), but those characterizations are advocacy; I did not independently read every circuit precedent or treat their pre-Sripetch disagreement about pecuniary loss as still unresolved.

I checked the primary opinion in **Sripetch v. SEC**, decided **June 4, 2026**, CourtListener opinion **11337516**. Its syllabus and opening discussion confirm that proof of pecuniary loss is unnecessary, that it did not settle the full relationship between Sections 78u(d)(5) and (d)(7), and that the questions about infeasible investor distributions remained open. This makes the Treasury question materially stronger than an attempt simply to relitigate a financial-loss prerequisite. It also means the argument that investors suffered no financial loss is not itself a reason for review after Sripetch. The distinction between no pecuniary loss and no invasion of a protected interest is essential.

The vehicle is nevertheless imperfect. The Eleventh Circuit's discussion, approximately pages 63–70, supplies both a statutory recipient analysis and an alternative equitable justification. The parties **stipulated that distributing the disgorgement to investors would be infeasible**. That stipulation limits a challenge to the proof of infeasibility and complicates a request to resolve the statutory issue alone: a favorable answer to one legal question may leave another ground supporting the judgment. The appellate opinion also describes actual fraudulent issuers and conduct enabling securities sales. Petitioners' assertion of no cognizable victims is therefore disputed framing, not an established finding that nobody's protected interests were invaded. A recent unanimous decision leaving an issue open is not a commitment to take the next petition presenting it.

The decision-below match is reliable on case number 22-13129, caption and January 16 judgment date. Its provisioned text is expressly truncated, ending before the full 211-page opinion and dissent. The petition and appendix are also expressly truncated, although the petition's argument is available. I used the lower-court docket only for the existence of a rehearing petition and its limited coverage, not as proof that no rehearing disposition or dissent existed. The dissent's full text was not read.
