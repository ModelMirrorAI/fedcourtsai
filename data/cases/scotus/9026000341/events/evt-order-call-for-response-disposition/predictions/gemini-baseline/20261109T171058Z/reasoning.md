## Detailed analysis

### 1. Factual and Procedural Background

Petitioners Spartan Securities Group, Ltd. (a registered broker-dealer), Island Capital Management LLC d/b/a Island Stock Transfer (a registered transfer agent), Carl E. Dilley, and Micah J. Eldred operated as a "one-stop shop" in Clearwater, Florida, taking microcap companies public (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 2, 11–12). Between 2009 and 2014, Petitioners assisted two groups of undisclosed control persons—Alvin Mirman and Sheldon Rose (14 "Mirman/Rose Companies") and Michael Daniels, Andy Fan, and Diane Harrison (5 "Daniels Companies")—in manufacturing 19 sham public shell companies for bulk sales and pump-and-dump schemes (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 10–17). Spartan submitted Form 211 applications and deficiency responses to the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority (FINRA) under SEC Rule 15c2-11 falsely representing that nominal straw officers managed the issuers, that Spartan had conducted independent due diligence, and that no undisclosed control persons or merger plans existed; meanwhile, Island submitted false applications to the Depository Trust Company (DTC), issued false transfer-agent letters representing that shares were unrestricted, and executed bulk transfers totaling over $3.7 million for the Mirman/Rose shells (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 11–16, 47–51).

In February 2019, the SEC brought a 14-count civil enforcement action in the Middle District of Florida. After a three-week jury trial in July 2021, the jury found for the defendants on 13 counts but found Spartan, Island, Dilley, and Eldred liable on Count VI for making material misrepresentations in connection with the purchase or sale of securities in violation of Section 10(b) of the Exchange Act, 15 U.S.C. § 78j(b), and Rule 10b-5(b), 17 C.F.R. § 240.10b-5(b) (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 20–21). At the remedies stage, the district court imposed injunctions, penny-stock bars, Tier Two civil penalties ($250,000 each against Spartan and Island; $150,000 each against Dilley and Eldred), and ordered Island to disgorge $114,520 in net transfer-agent fees (plus $39,874.05 in prejudgment interest) to the U.S. Treasury after deducting legitimate expenses (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 24–26; `petition.txt` at 244a–250a). Crucially, before the district court, the SEC and Island stipulated that "[a] distribution to investors of the disgorgement amount requested would be infeasible" (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 23, 70; `petition.txt` at 244a).

On January 16, 2026, a divided Eleventh Circuit panel (Judges Branch and Luck, per curiam) affirmed across the board (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 1–80). Note on provisioned documents: `record/documents/decision-below.txt` was capped at 150,000 characters (ending at page 74 of 211), and `documents.json` listed CourtListener cluster ID `10775540` in the `opinion_id` field; we retrieved and read the remainder of the 211-page slip opinion via CourtListener `opinion_id=11242127`. Judge Tjoflat authored a 69-page dissent (plus a 62-page appendix reproducing the complaint) arguing that the SEC's 14-count complaint was an impermissible "shotgun pleading" under Rules 8(a) and 9(b) and that the district judge's handling of the pleadings warranted sua sponte vacatur and disqualification under 28 U.S.C. § 455(a)—issues the majority noted the defendants had not raised on appeal (`opinion_id=11242127`, slip op. at 79–149).

### 2. Question Presented 2: Disgorgement to the Treasury After *Sripetch v. SEC*

Question Presented 2 is the clear driver of the Court's October 23, 2026 Call for Response. To evaluate whether the Court will grant plenary review or GVR after receiving the Solicitor General's Brief in Opposition, we compare the Eleventh Circuit's January 16, 2026 analysis with the Supreme Court's June 4, 2026 opinion in *Sripetch v. SEC*, 146 S. Ct. 1403 (2026) (`opinion_id=11337516`), which we also verified in the local corpus via `uv run fedcourts query --court scotus --docket-number "25-466" --full` (`scotus/73215957`).

In *Liu v. SEC*, 591 U.S. 71, 74–75, 87–90 (2020), the Supreme Court held that a disgorgement award permissible as "equitable relief" under 15 U.S.C. § 78u(d)(5) must not exceed a wrongdoer's net profits and must be "awarded for victims" pursuant to § 78u(d)(5)'s requirement that equitable relief be "appropriate or necessary for the benefit of investors." *Liu* rejected the SEC's routine practice of depositing disgorged funds into the Treasury without attempting to pay victims, but expressly left open "whether, and to what extent," depositing funds with the Treasury may still satisfy § 78u(d)(5) and equity practice "where it is infeasible to distribute the collected funds to investors." 591 U.S. at 88–89. Six months after *Liu*, Congress enacted the FY2021 NDAA, adding 15 U.S.C. § 78u(d)(7) ("In any action or proceeding brought by the Commission under any provision of the securities laws, the Commission may seek, and any Federal court may order, disgorgement") and § 78u(d)(8) (establishing a 10-year limitations period for scienter-based disgorgement claims).

When the Eleventh Circuit decided *Spartan Securities* on January 16, 2026, it rejected Island's challenge to the $114,520 Treasury disgorgement order on two distinct grounds:
1. **Statutory Exception Holding (Part 1, slip op. at 64–68):** Invoking the general/specific canon (*RadLAX Gateway Hotel, LLC v. Amalgamated Bank*, 566 U.S. 639, 644–45 (2012)), the Eleventh Circuit treated §§ 78u(d)(3)(A)(ii) and 78u(d)(7) as specific disgorgement provisions that operate as an exception to § 78u(d)(5)'s general investor-benefit requirement, concluding: "`[A]ny equitable relief` ordered under the Exchange Act—except disgorgement—must still `be appropriate or necessary for the benefit of investors.` 15 U.S.C. § 78u(d)(5). ... These provisions unambiguously allow the SEC to seek, and district courts to order, disgorgement—without any requirement that it benefit investors" (`opinion_id=11242127`, slip op. at 65–68).
2. **Alternative Equitable Holding (Part 2, slip op. at 68–70):** The Eleventh Circuit then held that even under traditional equitable principles and *Liu*, the district court did not abuse its discretion in ordering disgorgement to the Treasury because the SEC and Island had stipulated that distributing the $114,520 to harmed investors was infeasible, leaving the district court with only two options: let Island retain ill-gotten profits from conscious wrongdoing or direct the funds to the Treasury (`opinion_id=11242127`, slip op. at 68–70).

Five months later, on June 4, 2026, the Supreme Court decided *Sripetch v. SEC*, 146 S. Ct. 1403 (`opinion_id=11337516`). Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Gorsuch held that disgorgement under § 78u(d)(5) and § 78u(d)(7) does not require the SEC to prove that investors suffered pecuniary harm. In doing so, however, the Court dismantled the statutory premise of the Eleventh Circuit's Part 1 analysis:
- Justice Gorsuch emphasized that "both parties before us proceed on the premise that 'disgorgement' in § 78u(d)(7) shares the same meaning the term bears under § 78u(d)(5)" and that "both provisions are governed by traditional equitable principles," explaining that Congress enacted § 78u(d)(7) and (d)(8) in the 2021 NDAA to give the SEC a 10-year limitations window after *Kokesh v. SEC*, 581 U.S. 455 (2017), rather than to sever disgorgement from § 78u(d)(5)'s equitable limits (`opinion_id=11337516`, slip op. at 6–7, 13–14).
- Furthermore, addressing Mr. Sripetch's reliance on *Liu*'s victim-benefit requirement, Justice Gorsuch reiterated that *Liu* reserved the open question whether Treasury deposits are permissible when distributing funds to discrete victims is infeasible, and added: "Admittedly, questions could arise if the SEC fails to identify *anyone* who has a plausible common-law (or equitable) restitutionary claim and seeks to deposit gains with the Treasury anyway. In a case like that, a defendant might argue that the SEC cannot satisfy § 78u(d)(5)'s 'for the benefit of investors' requirement. And that contention would look a lot like the argument inside *Liu*—an argument, again, on which the Court reserved judgment" (`opinion_id=11337516`, slip op. at 11–12).

Why, then, do we price `probability = 0.16` (9% plenary grant + 7% GVR, so `summary-disposition-route = 0.44` conditional on grant) and predict **denial** (84%) once the Solicitor General responds?
1. **The Eleventh Circuit's alternative holding and Petitioners' stipulation create a formidable vehicle barrier:** Unlike the hypothetical posited at page 12 of *Sripetch*, Island did not litigate and win a finding that no downstream investors had a plausible restitutionary claim; rather, Mirman and Rose pled guilty to criminal securities fraud for manufacturing and selling the shells into the public market (`opinion_id=11242127`, slip op. at 17, 157–58), and Island *stipulated* at the remedies phase that "[a] distribution to investors of the disgorgement amount requested would be infeasible" (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 23, 70; `petition.txt` at 244a). Both the district court and Part 2 of the Eleventh Circuit's opinion rested independently on that stipulation to hold that equity permits a Treasury deposit when distribution to victims is infeasible.
2. **No post-*Sripetch* circuit split on infeasible investor distributions:** While the Second Circuit in *SEC v. Ahmed*, 72 F.4th 379 (2d Cir. 2023), and *SEC v. Govil*, 86 F.4th 89 (2d Cir. 2023), held that § 78u(d)(7) remains subject to *Liu*'s equitable limitations (with *Sripetch* subsequently abrogating *Govil*'s pecuniary-harm holding), no court of appeals has held that a district court is forbidden from ordering disgorgement to the Treasury when the parties stipulate that distributing the ill-gotten gains to investors is infeasible (`same-question.json` also confirms zero pending same-question petitions above the 0.5 similarity threshold).
3. **The Eleventh Circuit already considered *Sripetch* on rehearing:** Petitioners submitted *Sripetch* to the Eleventh Circuit via a Rule 28(j) letter on June 6, 2026, and the Eleventh Circuit denied rehearing en banc on June 12, 2026 (`petition.txt` at 13, 287a; `lower-court-docket.txt`). The Solicitor General will point out that a GVR is unnecessary where the court of appeals already had the opportunity to consider *Sripetch* before denying rehearing and where its alternative holding in Part 2 does not depend on treating § 78u(d)(7) as an exception to § 78u(d)(5).

### 3. Question Presented 1: "In Connection With" Under Section 10(b)

Question Presented 1 asks whether Section 10(b)'s "in connection with the purchase or sale of any security" requirement encompasses misrepresentations made in a nonpublic Form 211 submitted to FINRA before public quotations begin (`questions-presented.txt`). This question adds minimal cert-grant weight:
- **No circuit conflict:** Petitioners cite cases from the Second, Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, Ninth, and D.C. Circuits (*e.g.*, *SEC v. Texas Gulf Sulphur Co.*, 401 F.2d 833, 862 (2d Cir. 1968); *SEC v. Rana Research, Inc.*, 8 F.3d 1358, 1362 (9th Cir. 1993)) holding that *public dissemination* of false statements is *sufficient* in an SEC enforcement action to satisfy the "in connection with" element without proof of individual investor reliance (`petition.txt` at 17–21). None of those circuits held that public dissemination is the *exclusive* way to satisfy Section 10(b) or that deceiving a market gatekeeper (FINRA and DTC) to obtain quotation and clearing eligibility for a fraudulent stock sale falls outside Section 10(b). On the contrary, *United States v. Naftalin*, 441 U.S. 768, 772–76 (1979), and *SEC v. Zandford*, 535 U.S. 813, 819–25 (2002), confirm that frauds targeting market intermediaries or coinciding with securities sales violate the securities laws even when not communicated to retail investors (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 44–56).
- **Distinction from *Troice*:** In *Chadbourne & Parke LLP v. Troice*, 571 U.S. 377 (2014), the fraud induced purchases of uncovered bank certificates of deposit rather than covered securities. Here, Petitioners' false statements to FINRA and DTC directly enabled the actual bulk sales and public trading of registered microcap securities (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 51–53).
- **General verdict on multiple acts:** As the Eleventh Circuit detailed (`decision-below.txt`, slip op. at 47–51), the Count VI verdict was supported not only by Spartan's Form 211 filings with FINRA, but also by Island's false DTC eligibility applications, false transfer-agent non-restriction letters sent directly to buyers' counsel to close the bulk sales, and Spartan's publication of quotes on OTC Link. Thus, even a ruling for Petitioners on Form 211 would not disturb the Count VI liability judgment.
