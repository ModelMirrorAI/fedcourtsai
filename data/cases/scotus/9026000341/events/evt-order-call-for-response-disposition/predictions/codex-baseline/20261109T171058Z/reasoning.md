# Case-specific evidence

I read `questions-presented.txt`, the petition's introduction and reasons for review (especially printed pp. 17–33), and the relevant liability and disgorgement portions of `decision-below.txt`. The lower opinion matches the caption, Eleventh Circuit docket 22-13129, and January 16, 2026 judgment identified in the petition and snapshot. The petition includes an appendix, but pipeline notices disclose substantial appendix omissions; the lower opinion is also truncated, ending during printed p. 74. I do not treat either omission as the parties' silence. Neither text is marked OCR-derived.

**Question 2 is the stronger review candidate.** It asks whether SEC disgorgement may be ordered without identified harmed investors and paid to the Treasury. The petition invokes disagreement among Hallam, Ahmed, Govil, and the Ninth Circuit, and describes Sripetch as leaving these particular issues open while rejecting a pecuniary-loss prerequisite. That combination makes this more than an isolated complaint about a small award. The lower opinion itself analyzes the statutory provisions authorizing disgorgement and expressly permits payment to the Treasury without an investor-benefit requirement (printed pp. 60–68). This supplies a legal issue capable of recurring in SEC enforcement.

However, I do not accept the petition's conflict characterization wholesale. The lower opinion's footnote 14 distinguishes Ahmed and Govil because they did not resolve the disposition of money when distribution to victims is impracticable. The lower court also sustains the exercise of equitable discretion on the particular facts (pp. 68–70). A disagreement about whether the remedy is legal or equitable need not produce conflicting judgments on this precise Treasury-payment issue. A question expressly left open is not itself a reason the Court must immediately decide it.

The petition's account of the recent Sripetch decision was not independently verified: web tools returned no usable content and a CourtListener reporter-citation search returned no results. I therefore attribute that account to the petition rather than claim to have read Sripetch. This uncertainty reduces confidence in both the asserted post-Sripetch conflict and any summary-remand theory.

**Question 1 is weaker.** The petition argues that nonpublic communications to FINRA and DTC cannot satisfy the securities-fraud connection requirement without investor receipt, citing cases involving public dissemination. But the lower opinion links the communications to opening the securities for trading and actual market-making (pp. 42–46). It also identifies evidence supporting public availability of Forms 211. The proposed categorical question consequently rests on a contested factual premise, while the claimed split compares somewhat different kinds of communications. Review would risk becoming fact-specific error correction rather than resolution of a clean same-facts conflict.

The government's initial waiver is a modest negative, not a prediction of its eventual position. The requested government brief is an important unknown. One amicus filing offers some evidence of institutional interest, but I have not read its unprovisioned text.
