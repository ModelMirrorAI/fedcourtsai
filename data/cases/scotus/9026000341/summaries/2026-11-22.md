## In brief

Securities firms ask the Court to review a ruling that found them liable for misleading private regulatory filings and ordered one firm to repay fees to the Treasury.

## What happened

The SEC sued Spartan Securities Group, Island Capital Management, and two of their officers, alleging they helped take shell companies public through fraudulent schemes. A jury rejected 13 of the SEC's 14 claims but found the defendants liable on one count involving statements made to FINRA, a private regulator that screens stocks for public trading, and to DTC, a clearinghouse that handles stock transfers. The trial court ordered Island to pay civil penalties and disgorge over $150,000 in fees to the U.S. Treasury, even though no harmed investors were identified. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed. Petitioners sought rehearing, which was denied, then asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions. First, whether making a misleading statement only to a private regulator or clearinghouse, never seen by any investor, counts as fraud "in connection with" buying or selling securities under the relevant securities law. Second, whether courts may order a company to give up profits to the government when no investor was harmed and there is no one to repay, or whether that only serves as a punishment that the law does not allow under ordinary fairness principles governing this kind of remedy.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference. The government waived its right to respond, but the Court later asked for a response, which is now due December 23, 2026, after the government was granted an extension.

## What each outcome would mean

Spartan and the other petitioners are asking the Court to overturn the Eleventh Circuit's decision. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide whether the lower court's rulings on liability and on repaying profits to the Treasury were correct, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies the petition, the Eleventh Circuit's ruling stands, leaving the liability finding and the order requiring Island to repay over $150,000 in place.
