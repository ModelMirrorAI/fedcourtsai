## In brief

The United States asks the Court to review a ruling rejecting its late attempt to take over Trump's defense in E. Jean Carroll's defamation suit.

## What happened

E. Jean Carroll sued Donald Trump for defamation over statements he made in 2019 denying her account of a decades-old sexual assault. In 2020 the Attorney General certified that Trump was acting within the scope of his federal employment, which under the Westfall Act would substitute the United States as the defendant. That certification was later withdrawn, the case went to trial, and a jury awarded Carroll $83.3 million. After Trump returned to office, a new Attorney General tried to recertify the same conduct and substitute the United States, but only after trial and appeal briefing were complete. The Second Circuit refused to allow it, citing the statute's timing rules, an earlier waiver of the right to seek substitution, and fairness to Carroll. The United States, joined by a related petition from Trump himself, asks the Supreme Court to reverse.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal law letting the government step in as the defendant for employees sued over job-related conduct allows the Attorney General to certify that conduct was official, and take over the case, even after a trial has already happened — or whether the law requires that step to happen before trial.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply from the United States, and was distributed for the Court's conference on December 4, 2026. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

The United States, as petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's decision; Carroll, who won the Westfall Act dispute below, opposes review. If the Court denies the petition, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, the government cannot substitute itself as defendant, and Trump remains personally liable for the jury's damages award. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide the timing question itself; a decision favoring the government could let the United States replace Trump as defendant, which would likely end Carroll's defamation claim because the government has not waived immunity from defamation suits under the law that would then apply. A decision favoring Carroll would leave the judgment against Trump intact.
