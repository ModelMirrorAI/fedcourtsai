## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to revive its bid to substitute itself for President Trump as defendant in E. Jean Carroll's defamation lawsuit.

## What happened

Carroll sued Trump for statements he made in 2019 denying her assault allegations. An Attorney General initially certified the statements as within Trump's official duties, moving the case to federal court, but a later Attorney General withdrew that certification, and the case proceeded to trial, where a jury awarded Carroll $83.3 million. After Trump was reelected, a new Attorney General recertified the statements as official conduct and moved to substitute the United States as defendant. The Second Circuit denied that motion, finding it barred by statute, waived, and unfair at this stage, and later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The government is asking the Court to decide whether a federal law shielding government employees from certain lawsuits allows the Attorney General to certify, after a trial has already happened, that an employee's conduct was official business, when an earlier Attorney General had withdrawn that same certification before trial. The appeals court read the law as barring certification at that late stage; the government argues the law places no such time limit once a case is already in federal court.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in July 2026, and the response deadline was extended to October 30, 2026. An amicus brief has been filed. The docket does not yet show the case distributed for a conference or any Court action on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The United States, joined by Trump, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it will decide the certification question on the merits, which could ultimately end Carroll's defamation suit if the government is substituted as defendant, since federal law does not allow defamation claims against the government. If the Court denies the petition, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Trump as the defendant and the jury's verdict in Carroll's favor intact.
