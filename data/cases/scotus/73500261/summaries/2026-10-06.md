## In brief

Louisiana officials ask the Supreme Court, only if it takes up a related Texas case, to review a ruling that blocked Louisiana's classroom Ten Commandments law.

## What happened

Louisiana passed a law requiring public schools to display the Ten Commandments in every classroom. Public-school parents sued, arguing the law violates the First Amendment's religious liberty protections. A federal district court blocked the law, and a panel of the Fifth Circuit agreed. The full Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, then set aside that injunction, ruling the parents' challenge was premature because too much about how the displays would actually look and be used remained unknown. A related Texas law was separately struck down on the merits by the same en banc court. Louisiana now asks the Supreme Court to weigh in, but only conditionally.

## What the Court is being asked

Louisiana's filing asks the Court to take up the case only if it agrees to hear the separate Texas dispute, and even then argues review is unwarranted. The underlying questions are whether the parents' lawsuit was too early to decide, and whether the Louisiana law violates constitutional protections against government establishment of religion or on religious free exercise. Louisiana contends the lower court was right to reject the challenge and that no court conflict requires the Supreme Court's attention now.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, after which the Court requested a response from the parents rather than ruling. The deadline for that response has since been extended twice, most recently to October 19, 2026, and no conference action has been recorded since.

## What each outcome would mean

Louisiana officials, who lost the preliminary ruling before the Fifth Circuit panel but prevailed when the full appeals court vacated the injunction on procedural grounds, filed this conditional petition. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision lifting the injunction stands, and the parents' lawsuit would continue in the lower courts without Supreme Court intervention. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on the timing and constitutional questions and decide them itself, deciding nothing in advance about who wins.
