## In brief

Louisiana education officials ask the Court to take up a case over the state's classroom Ten Commandments law only if it also takes up a similar Texas case.

## What happened

Louisiana passed a law requiring public schools to display the Ten Commandments in every classroom. Parents sued, arguing the law violates the First Amendment's religion protections. A federal district court blocked the law, and a Fifth Circuit panel agreed. But the full Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, vacated that injunction, ruling only that the challenge was premature because too much about how schools would actually display the posters remained unknown. The court did not decide whether the law itself was constitutional. Louisiana officials, though they won below, filed what they call a "conditional" petition asking the Supreme Court to review the case only if it agrees to hear a related Texas case, where a different appeals panel upheld a similar law on the merits.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked, if it takes up the Texas case, whether this Louisiana case should also be reviewed, including whether the parents' challenge was premature to bring and whether the law violates constitutional protections against government establishment of religion or restrictions on religious exercise.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference, and the Court later asked the respondents to respond. After two extensions, the respondents filed a brief opposing review on October 19, 2026. The case has not yet been scheduled for a new conference in this snapshot.

## What each outcome would mean

Louisiana officials, who won the ripeness ruling below, are asking the Court to act, but only alongside the separate Texas case. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling that the parents' challenge was premature stands, leaving the law's constitutionality undecided for now. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and could decide the premature-challenge question, the constitutional questions, or both; a grant would not by itself decide who wins. The respondents, the parents challenging the law, oppose review entirely, arguing the case is a poor vehicle for deciding the law's constitutionality since the lower court never reached that issue.
