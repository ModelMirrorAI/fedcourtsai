## In brief

Louisiana officials ask the Supreme Court to review a ruling that threw out, on procedural grounds, a challenge to the state's law requiring Ten Commandments displays in public school classrooms.

## What happened

Louisiana passed a law requiring every public school classroom to display the Ten Commandments, with a required size, context statement, and content, though local school boards retain some choice over the display's design. Parents sued, arguing the law violates the First Amendment's bans on establishing religion and on burdening free exercise of religion. A federal district court agreed and blocked the law. A Fifth Circuit panel affirmed, but the full appeals court, rehearing the case, later ruled the parents' challenge could not yet be decided because no actual display had been created or shown to anyone, and vacated the injunction without ruling for either side on the merits. A companion case about a similar Texas law was decided on the merits in the state's favor. Louisiana's petition asks the Supreme Court to step in only if it agrees to hear the Texas case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the parents' lawsuit can go forward at all before any specific display has been put up, given that the law leaves many details to local officials, or whether it is premature because courts need to see an actual display to judge it. The Court is also asked, if the case can go forward, whether the law is unconstitutional in every possible form it could take under either the religion-establishment rule or the rule protecting religious exercise.

## Where it stands

Louisiana's petition is conditional: it asks the Court to take up this case only if the Court agrees to hear a petition from the related Texas case. The respondents waived their right to respond, the Court then requested a response, and the deadline for that response has been extended twice, most recently to October 19, 2026. No conference date or decision on the petition has yet occurred.

## What each outcome would mean

Louisiana officials, who lost the procedural ruling they wanted (outright dismissal) even though the injunction against them was lifted, are the ones asking the Court to act, but only conditionally. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision vacating the injunction without deciding the merits stands, and the parents' case could potentially continue in the lower courts. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the procedural and constitutional questions itself, which could result in affirming, reversing, or sending the case back without resolving it for either side.
