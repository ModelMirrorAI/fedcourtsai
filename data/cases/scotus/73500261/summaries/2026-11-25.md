## In brief

Louisiana education officials ask the Court to review a ruling that blocked, on procedural grounds, enforcement of a law requiring Ten Commandments displays in public school classrooms.

## What happened

Louisiana's H.B. 71 requires every public school classroom to display the Ten Commandments, with minimum size and content requirements and a mandatory historical context statement. Public school parents and their children sued, arguing the law violates the First Amendment's religion clauses. A federal district court blocked the law, and a Fifth Circuit panel agreed. But the full Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, vacated that injunction, holding only that the challenge was premature because too much about how the displays would actually look and be used remained unknown. It did not rule on whether the law is constitutional. Louisiana then filed this petition, framed as conditional on the Court also taking up a related Texas case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether the parents' challenge to the Louisiana law was premature, and, if the Court takes up a related case involving a similar Texas law, whether the Louisiana law's requirement to display the Ten Commandments in every classroom violates the First Amendment's protections against government establishment of religion or its protection of free religious exercise.

## Where it stands

Louisiana filed this petition conditionally, asking the Court to take the case only if it also agrees to hear a related Texas case. The respondents filed a brief opposing review, Louisiana filed a reply, and the petition was distributed for the Court's conference on December 11, 2026, after an earlier conference and a rescheduling. The docket does not yet show any decision on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Louisiana, which lost the ripeness ruling it wanted overturned but won on the ultimate outcome below, is asking the Court to step in only conditionally. If the Court denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, meaning the case returns to the lower courts without a ruling on whether the law is constitutional. If the Court grants the petition alongside the related Texas case, it would hear full arguments on the procedural and constitutional questions and could affirm, reverse, or send the case back without deciding the merits for either side. Louisiana has said it does not want the Court to grant review unless it also takes the Texas case; the Roake respondents urge denial.
