# Forecast: Hoffmann v. WBI Energy Transmission, Inc. (No. 25-159) — the merits judgment

**Judgment class: affirmed.** The Court will hold that federal law, not borrowed
state law, supplies the measure of compensation in private condemnations under
15 U.S.C. § 717f(h), and will affirm the Eighth Circuit's judgment denying the
landowners' claim to attorney's fees under North Dakota's condemnation
fee-shifting statute. P(judgment below disturbed) = 0.45 — this is close to a
coin flip, and the affirmance call is a narrow one.

**The ground.** The majority will build on PennEast v. New Jersey, 594 U.S. 482
(2021), which characterized § 717f(h) as a delegation of the *federal*
eminent-domain power to certificate holders. From that premise, the
compensation owed when that power is exercised is a federal question governed
by the Fifth Amendment standard of the United States v. Miller line, and the
Kimbell Foods borrowing analysis the Third, Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuits
applied never gets started because there is no gap in federal law to fill. Two
subsidiary federal rules close the case: United States v. Bodcaw Co. holds that
attorney's fees are not part of constitutional just compensation, and Alyeska
Pipeline's American Rule bars fee awards in a federal cause of action absent
express congressional authorization — which § 717f(h) does not contain.

**Breadth.** Categorical, not fact-bound: the Court granted the broad question
presented after a CVSG flagged an entrenched 4–1 split, and a holding confined
to North Dakota's particular fee statute would not resolve it. Expect a uniform
nationwide rule for all § 717f(h) condemnations, abrogating the
state-law-borrowing rule in the four majority circuits.

**Vote lineup: 6–3.** Majority: Roberts, Alito, Kagan, Kavanaugh (writing),
Barrett, Jackson. Dissent: Gorsuch (writing), joined by Thomas and Sotomayor.
The dissent's theme will be the one Gorsuch pressed in Rodriguez v. FDIC —
federal courts should not displace state law absent congressional direction —
joined to property-rights federalism (compensation rights are traditionally
state-defined) and the forty years of settled practice behind the majority
circuits' rule. The lineup is my lowest-confidence claim: the choice-of-law
question cuts across the usual blocs (PennEast itself split 5–4 on unusual
lines), and a Kagan- or Sotomayor-side switch in either direction would not
surprise me.

**Which question the Court reaches.** The QP as granted — whether just
compensation in NGA private condemnations is determined by reference to state
law. The Court will answer the choice-of-law question rather than resolving the
case on the respondent's narrower vehicle theory (that North Dakota's
fee-shifting provision is not part of "just compensation" even under state
law), though that narrower ground may appear as a fallback in the opinion or in
a concurrence.

**Separate writings.** Beyond the Gorsuch dissent, a short concurrence
distinguishing federal-condemnor cases from private-condemnor cases is
plausible but not likely enough to commit to.

**Procedural exits.** A DIG is conceivable — the BIO's vehicle argument (this
is a fees case, not a compensation-measure case) is the kind that occasionally
persuades after argument — but the Court granted with that argument fully
briefed and a CVSG in hand, so I put it below 5%. No recusal is apparent, so an
equally divided affirmance is a remote possibility only.

**Timing** (not scored, stated for discipline): argument is set for November 9,
2026; expect the decision between March and June 2027, most likely late in the
Term given the cross-cutting coalition.
