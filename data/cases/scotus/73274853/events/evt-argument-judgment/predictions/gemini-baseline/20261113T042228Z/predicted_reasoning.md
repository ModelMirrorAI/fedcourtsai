# Predicted Court Reasoning and Disposition

## Judgment and Vote Lineup

The Court will **affirm** the judgment of the Eighth Circuit (`affirmed`), leaving intact the vacatur of the district court's North Dakota state-law attorney-fee award. The primary expected lineup is a unanimous **9–0** decision in which all nine participating Justices—Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson—join the judgment and opinion of the Court. The main secondary lineup scenario is an **8–1** affirmance with Justice Gorsuch (or, less likely, Justice Thomas) dissenting on textualist/federalism grounds tied to the parallel Federal Power Act language and pre-1947 state-law practice.

Neither procedural exit is live. All nine Justices participated actively at oral argument with no recusal, ruling out an affirmance by an equally divided Court barring an unexpected post-argument absence. Nor will the Court dismiss the writ as improvidently granted: following settled practice of deferring to shared lower-court constructions of state law, the Court will assume without deciding that the district court and Eighth Circuit properly treated North Dakota's condemnation fee-shifting statute as part of North Dakota's substantive measure of just compensation, and it will resolve the federal question presented squarely on that premise.

## Question Reached, Doctrinal Ground, and Breadth

The Court will reach the single question presented in full and answer it in the negative: in private condemnations under Section 7(h) of the Natural Gas Act, 15 U.S.C. § 717f(h), the substantive measure of just compensation is governed by federal Fifth Amendment law rather than state law. It will leave unaddressed respondent's alternative state-law contention that North Dakota makes attorney fees discretionary procedural costs rather than a component of just compensation.

### Doctrinal Ground (`majority-ground`)

Extending *PennEast Pipeline Co. v. New Jersey*, 594 U.S. 482 (2021), together with *United States v. Miller*, 317 U.S. 369 (1943), *United States v. 93.970 Acres of Land*, 360 U.S. 328 (1959), and *United States v. Bodcaw Co.*, 440 U.S. 202 (1979), the majority will hold that a FERC certificate holder condemning property under 15 U.S.C. § 717f(h) exercises the federal eminent-domain power itself rather than a naked federal cause of action detached from federal compensation rules. Relying on *Kohl v. United States*, 91 U.S. 367 (1875), and *Monongahela Navigation Co. v. United States*, 148 U.S. 312 (1893), the Court will explain that the sovereign power to take property and the obligation to pay just compensation are inseparable incidents of the same federal eminent-domain authority, and that under *Miller* and *93.970 Acres* federal law sets the baseline measure of compensation—fair market value, excluding indirect litigation expenses such as attorney fees under *Bodcaw*—whenever federal eminent-domain authority is exercised unless Congress affirmatively adopts state law.

The majority will reinforce that reading with two textual contrasts: first, Section 717f(h) itself expressly borrowed state "practice and procedure" (a clause later superseded by Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 71.1) while saying nothing to adopt state substantive compensation rules; second, just one year before enacting Section 717f(h) in 1947, Congress expressly directed in the General Bridge Act of 1946, 33 U.S.C. § 532, that compensation be ascertained and paid according to state law, demonstrating that Congress knew how to incorporate state valuation rules into private federal condemnations when it wished to do so and modeled Section 717f(h) instead on the Condemnation Act of 1888 construed in *Miller*. Because federal Fifth Amendment law supplies the governing standard directly, there is no statutory gap to fill through federal common law under *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 440 U.S. 715 (1979), abrogating the contrary pre-*PennEast* approach of the Third, Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuits. Finally, the majority will reject petitioners' argument (and distinguish *Hencely v. Fluor Corp.*) that *Miller* and *Bodcaw* rest on federal sovereign immunity, explaining that state compensation laws fail to govern federal condemnations under the Supremacy Clause because States cannot regulate the exercise of federal eminent-domain authority, not because the condemnor asserts sovereign immunity.

### Breadth of Holding (`ground-breadth`)

The majority will announce a **categorical rule** governing all private condemnations under 15 U.S.C. § 717f(h) nationwide: federal Fifth Amendment compensation principles displace all state-law measures that depart from federal fair-market-value standards (including state valuation multipliers, heritage-property premiums, and litigation fee-shifting), rather than limiting the decision to North Dakota's attorney-fee statute or remitting pipeline certificate holders to case-by-case obstacle/frustration preemption. At the same time, the Court will cabin its holding in two respects: it will assume without deciding the lower courts' interpretation of North Dakota law, and it will specify that certificate holders step into the United States' shoes only to exercise the delegated federal eminent-domain power and do not acquire general federal sovereign immunity from otherwise applicable state laws.

## Authorship and Separate Writings

Chief Justice Roberts will assign the opinion for the November 2026 sitting. **Chief Justice Roberts** (who authored *PennEast*) and **Justice Jackson** (who laid out the opinion's full doctrinal and textual architecture during questioning, from *93.970 Acres* and *Miller* to the 1946 General Bridge Act contrast) are the two likeliest majority authors, closely followed by **Justice Kavanaugh** (who synthesized the *Miller*–*Bodcaw*–*PennEast* triad) and **Justice Barrett** (whom the Chief Justice may assign to cement institutional unanimity after her Eleventh Amendment dissent in *PennEast*).

A single unanimous opinion with no separate writings is well within reach, as the majority opinion itself can clarify that private delegees do not possess federal sovereign immunity. If a separate writing issues, **Justice Thomas** (concurring to underscore why *Hencely* and sovereign immunity are distinct from delegated federal eminent-domain authority, or noting open questions around originalist Takings Clause valuation) and **Justice Gorsuch** (either concurring or filing a lone dissent focused on property-owner protections and the Federal Power Act backdrop) are the primary candidates.
