## Competing readings

The landowners' strongest argument is that **a constitutional minimum does not by itself establish a statutory maximum**. Their opening brief and reply distinguish the federal government's immunity from the compensation liabilities of a private delegee. They invoke traditional state control of property, narrow construction of private delegations, borrowing of Federal Power Act language against earlier lower-court practice, and the alternative Kimbell Foods framework. The statute expressly discusses state practice and procedure but never expressly rejects state substantive compensation law. Their account of decades of pipeline construction under state-law compensation rules is a serious answer to claims that the federal program cannot function under those rules. This is the principal reason not to assign near-zero reversal probability.

WBI's stronger response is about the **source of the taking power**, not an inheritance of every governmental immunity. Section 717f(h) authorizes a federal condemnation, and its compensation standard can be read against the established federal eminent-domain background. State law may define the property interest without also supplying the remedy for its federally authorized taking. On this view the Fifth Amendment standard is the applicable default Congress left in place, while the Bridge Act comparison shows Congress could expressly choose state compensation rules. There is then no unresolved choice-of-law gap requiring Kimbell Foods policymaking.

The **United States supports respondent and affirmance** in its merits amicus brief and argument. I follow its principal statutory/default-rule position, including the separation of supremacy from sovereign immunity, but not a claim that all private federal contractors are immune from ordinary state laws. Its alternative federal-uniformity argument reinforces rather than independently determines my number. The government's acknowledgment at argument that state compensation laws have not yet stopped a pipeline weakens an empirical necessity claim; it does not answer what default Congress selected.

The external precedent checks are limited in what they establish. **PennEast Pipeline Co. v. New Jersey, 594 U.S. 482, 498 (2021)** calls this statutory delegation categorical, but decided condemnation of state interests and sovereign immunity, not the compensation question here. **United States v. Bodcaw Co., 440 U.S. 202, 203–204 (1979)** excludes ordinary litigation/appraisal expenses from constitutionally required compensation and recognizes Congress's power to authorize more; its footnote also discusses limits on costs assessed against the United States. That footnote gives the landowners a real distinction to press. Neither precedent alone mechanically decides this case. The forecast is that the Court combines the federal delegation and compensation precedents through statutory construction rather than equating a constitutional floor with an invariable ceiling for every taking.

## Argument: content first, counts second

The added evidence at this moment is unusually one-sided in substance:

- **Roberts** repeatedly challenged why a federal taking followed by conveyance to the pipeline should differ from a direct delegation. His final question to the United States exposed a qualification: FERC would need congressional authority to condemn directly. I therefore treat his hypothetical as an interpretive analogy, not an independently available workaround established on this record.
- **Sotomayor** invoked Monongahela's connection between taking and compensation and PennEast's treatment of the delegation. Those questions directly attack the landowners' effort to separate the powers. Her later procedural-language question leaves room for a different route but not an evident vote for reversal.
- **Jackson** repeatedly questioned the proposed state-law default, using the federal condemnation cases, the Bridge Act contrast, and congressional purpose. This is affirmative resistance to the petitioners' rule, not simply liberal-Justice alignment assumed from the subject matter.
- **Kagan** questioned whether the taking power normally includes its compensation condition and tested the state-law rule with treble compensation. Her questions to WBI about the other circuits' long-running contrary view are meaningful caution, but do not erase her central objection to petitioners' premise.
- **Kavanaugh** expressly identified the combined force of Miller, Bodcaw, and PennEast, then asked about consumer costs. His questioning is the clearest individual indicator favoring affirmance.
- **Thomas** asked all advocates to address whether sovereign immunity actually explains the difference in fee liability. That is a live analytical concern, not a settled vote against petitioners. His questions about delegation and the value of land nevertheless fit the federal-rule majority more readily than a state-law majority.
- **Alito** tested the conceded constitutional floor through a below-market state compensation rule. This does not independently establish a ceiling; I count it as scrutiny of the proposed framework rather than a dispositive concession.
- **Gorsuch** explored the borrowed Federal Power Act text, historical state-law practice, and the scope of procedural borrowing. He is among the more plausible departures from a fully shared rationale, but also pressed the implications of enhanced state awards.
- **Barrett** distinguished state law operating of its own force from state law borrowed as federal common law. She then pressed the United States on whether valuation still requires judicial development. Those are the strongest openings for the petitioners' gap-filling theory. Her questions are also compatible with writing a carefully bounded federal-default opinion.

These are speaker-turn counts, not interruption counts or votes. The doctrinal content, not an assumed statistical conversion of those counts into odds, drives the adjustment.

I lower most separate-writing probabilities from contentious-case history because the argument suggests substantial agreement. No individual separate writing is more likely than not, so the vote block's point forecast is a Barrett opinion with no other writer. That point forecast should not be mistaken for high confidence that there will be no concurrence at all.

The main substantive residual risk is a coalition concluding that the federal constitutional floor leaves a genuine statutory gap, or resolving the dispute through fee-specific principles while rejecting the Eighth Circuit's general compensation rule. The distinction between affirming a fee result and endorsing a categorical measure-of-compensation rule is also the largest risk to the semantic forecast. I nevertheless predict a rule covering NGA compensation generally, not merely fees, while stopping short of immunity for private companies under other state laws.

The **0.60 stakes score** reflects national landowner and pipeline consequences and a real federal-state allocation question. It is not a cert probability or a claim that this small fee dispute will rewrite the entire law of eminent domain.
