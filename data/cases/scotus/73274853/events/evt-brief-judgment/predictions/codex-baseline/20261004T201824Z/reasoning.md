## Why I move below that anchor

**The statutory-default dispute favors WBI somewhat.** The petitioners have a substantial argument that congressional silence preserves state compensation rules, especially when Congress authorizes private rather than sovereign condemnors. Their merits brief, pp. 7–12 and 37–46, stresses the property-law background, the history of state-law borrowing, the *Kimbell Foods* factors, and the limited sovereign-immunity question actually decided in *PennEast*. Those are meaningful reasons not to make affirmance overwhelming.

Nevertheless, WBI's merits brief, pp. 13–27, supplies a relatively direct textual and historical route to affirmance: a federal eminent-domain delegation, a preexisting federal compensation framework, an express incorporation of state procedure rather than substantive compensation, and contrasting statutes that expressly adopt state compensation rules. Its discussion of *Miller* and the General Bridge Act makes the disagreement more than a policy contest about cheap pipelines. The retrieved *PennEast* passage, slip opinion pp. 11–12, confirms the characterization of the delegation as categorical in the context of authority to condemn. That supports WBI's reading, but does not itself decide this different question.

**The actual remedy provides an additional route to affirmance.** The land value was settled; fees and expenses remained disputed. WBI's merits brief, pp. 43–45, invokes the American Rule, *Bodcaw*, and Congress's targeted provision for condemnation litigation expenses in 42 U.S.C. § 4654. The retrieved *Bodcaw* opinion confirms that ordinary appraisal and litigation costs do not become constitutionally required compensation merely because paying them reduces the owner's net recovery. Petitioners respond, merits brief pp. 47–49, that the issue is the statutory adoption of a state's broader compensation measure, not an enlargement of the Fifth Amendment. That answer is plausible, but it still must establish the state-law adoption; relabeling fees as compensation does not establish it. The fee-specific defense also permits affirmance without complete endorsement of the Eighth Circuit's reasoning.

**The strongest contrary evidence is the sustained state-law approach.** The petition and petitioner merits brief describe contrary decisions in the Third, Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuits, and analogous Federal Power Act cases. The petitioners also argue that existing pipeline construction under those rules undercuts predictions that state compensation standards would obstruct the federal program. I treat these as serious interpretive and reliance considerations, not merely a count of circuit votes. They preserve a substantial reversal possibility despite my preference for WBI's federal-default and fees arguments.

These considerations together move me from the general disturbed-judgment prior to **0.38**, rather than treating a grant resolving a circuit split as an indication that the outlying circuit must lose. The two respondent arguments overlap, so I have not treated them as independent numerical likelihood ratios.
