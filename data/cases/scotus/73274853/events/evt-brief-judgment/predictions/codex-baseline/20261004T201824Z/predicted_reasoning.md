# Forecast of the Court's judgment

The Court will **affirm** the Eighth Circuit. My modal lineup is **6–3**: Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Kagan, Kavanaugh, and Barrett in the majority; Sotomayor, Gorsuch, and Jackson dissenting. The judgment will leave in place the rejection of the landowners' demand for attorney fees and litigation expenses under the state-law compensation theory. This is a merits judgment, not another decision whether to grant certiorari.

## Majority ground

The majority will answer the presented choice-of-law question by holding that the Natural Gas Act's delegation of federal eminent-domain authority carries the federal measure of compensation unless Congress provides otherwise. It will distinguish state law defining the property interest taken from the federal law measuring compensation for the taking. Section 717f(h)'s express reference to state procedure will not be read as adopting state substantive compensation standards.

The Court will use the federal character of the delegated power recognized in *PennEast Pipeline Co. v. New Jersey*, 594 U.S. 482 (2021), together with the compensation framework discussed in *United States v. Miller*, 317 U.S. 369 (1943). It will treat *PennEast* as supporting the statutory interpretation, not as an earlier holding deciding this precise compensation question. On that interpretation, there is no unresolved statutory gap requiring the Court to borrow state rules through *United States v. Kimbell Foods, Inc.*, 440 U.S. 715 (1979).

The exclusion of ordinary litigation expenses from constitutional just compensation, recognized in *United States v. Bodcaw Co.*, 440 U.S. 202, 203–04 (1979), will support the result on the actual fee dispute. The American Rule will provide reinforcement rather than the sole basis for the majority's judgment.

## Breadth and competing rationale

The holding will govern compensation in private Natural Gas Act condemnations generally, not merely this North Dakota fee provision. It will not hold that state law ceases to define property interests, that Congress cannot authorize greater compensation, or that every federal statutory cause of action displaces state law. The settled easement valuation itself will not be reopened.

The dissent will distinguish delegation of the power to condemn from delegation of every sovereign incident of that power. It will read the statutory silence, traditional state control of property, and longstanding lower-court practice as favoring state compensation rules. The principal alternative route to affirmance is a narrower, fee-specific opinion reserving the general valuation question; that is not the breadth forecast committed here. A separate concurrence could prefer that narrower route without changing the judgment.

The snapshot schedules argument for November 9, 2026. I expect an opinion during the ensuing Term, most plausibly in the first half of 2027. A DIG is a small live possibility because the compensation settlement left only fees disputed, but the choice-of-law controversy remains concrete. No provisioned entry identifies a recusal, and an equally divided affirmance is not my forecast. Timing and possible separate-writing roles are context, not additional structured claims.
