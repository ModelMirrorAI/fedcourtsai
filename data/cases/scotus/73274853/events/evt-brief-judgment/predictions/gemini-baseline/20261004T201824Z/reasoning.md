I adjust upward from this base rate for several reasons. First, the Eighth Circuit's decision below created an explicit 4-1 circuit split, standing alone against the Third, Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuits. The Supreme Court typically grants certiorari in such lopsided splits to bring the outlier circuit back into the fold, which strongly implies a reversal. Second, the issue touches on core property rights and federalism—areas where the current conservative supermajority (6-3) has consistently demonstrated a willingness to side against federal preemption of state rules, particularly when state law affords greater protection to private property owners than the Fifth Amendment floor. 

Both sides' merits briefs are on the record. The petitioners have robust amicus support from property rights groups (Institute for Justice, Pacific Legal Foundation, Owners' Counsel of America) and notably the Chamber of Commerce, emphasizing the traditional role of state law in property valuation. The respondent relies heavily on *United States v. Miller*, arguing that delegated federal power should carry a uniform federal compensation standard, but this argument likely falls short of overcoming the Court's reluctance to displace state property law without a clear congressional mandate. 

One source of uncertainty is the precise view of the Solicitor General on the merits. A CVSG brief was filed prior to the cert grant, but it was not provided in the `documents/` directory, so its recommendation and reasoning could not be directly assessed. However, given that cert was granted despite the potential that the SG might have urged denial (e.g., if the SG prioritized uniform federal rules for infrastructure), the Court is highly motivated to resolve the split, likely in favor of the petitioners. Thus, a reversal is heavily favored.
