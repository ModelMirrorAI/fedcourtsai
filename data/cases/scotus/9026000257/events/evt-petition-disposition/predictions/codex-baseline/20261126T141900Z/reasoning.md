I put the chance of any grant at 60%. The strongest reason is the fit between the first question presented and *Stone v. Graham*. *Stone* struck down Kentucky’s requirement to post the Ten Commandments in public-school classrooms. The Fifth Circuit, sitting en banc, divided 9–8 over a Texas law prescribing the text, size and visibility of a classroom display. Its majority treated *Kennedy*’s abandonment of the *Lemon* test as fatal to *Stone*, vacated the preliminary injunction and rendered judgment dismissing the claims. A second panel applied that holding in *Cribbs Ringer*. The families seek review of both judgments in one petition. The Court can decide for itself what remains binding and give schools a rule they can apply. (Questions presented 1–2, petition pp. 2–5, 37–41, petition appendix pp. 1a–3a, 33a–43a.)

The frozen prior-Term grant rate for a private paid petition reaching the baseline band is 5.18%. Three amicus briefs appear on the November 26 snapshot. The frozen rate for the band with at least one brief is 16.31% (2,318 resolved), and the paid statpack’s two-or-more bucket records 22.7% grants including remands (1,459 resolved). Those groups count briefs by disposition and include petitions that advanced farther. The larger adjustment here turns on the lower court’s treatment of a directly applicable Supreme Court holding. The second question offers less reason to intervene. The families describe unavoidable daily exposure, while the districts distinguish the required classroom instruction at issue in *Mahmoud* from a poster teachers have no duty to discuss. (Opposition pp. 13–18, 24–27, reply pp. 3–7, 13–14.)

Two facts make this a close call. First, the opposition identifies no post-*Kennedy* disagreement between circuits on a classroom mandate. The Arkansas district court permanently enjoined a similar law. The Eighth Circuit docket records argument on September 22 and my search found no appellate opinion by the snapshot date. Waiting could give the justices both a second appellate judgment and a more concrete record of implementation. Second, Texas’s incoming attorney general had said he would decline to defend the law. The districts are the defendants and can retain counsel. The en banc court also found the dispute ripe given the specificity of the Texas mandate. Even so, who would defend the law at argument and how much classroom context a facial challenge supplies are genuine reasons to pass. (Opposition pp. 27–34, reply pp. 7–9.)

Louisiana’s conditional petition in *Brumley v. Roake*, No. 25-1360, modestly favors review. Respondents ask the Court to take it if it grants here. The Louisiana case turned on ripeness, while the Texas decision reached the merits. The Court rescheduled *Brumley* from December 4 to December 11, the first conference for *Nathan*. That permits a coordinated decision. Louisiana’s three distributions include a requested response and a rescheduling, so I give the shared date less weight than a series of conferences spent considering the merits.

The paid statpack shows that most petitions finish without a relist (10,311 resolved there) and few draw an invitation to the Solicitor General (166 of 13,840 resolved with known status). I put another distribution at 70% because checking two judgments and deciding the scope of review may take a second conference. The states’ constitutional dispute presents little need for federal executive expertise (1% for an invitation). Conditional on a grant, I give a summary disposition 14%. The petition expressly seeks summary reversal or a remand to apply *Stone*, but the opposition asks the Court to overrule *Stone* expressly if necessary, a choice more suited to argument. Conditional on denial, I give a public dissent or statement 28%. Daily exposure in compulsory classrooms and the departure from *Stone* give a Justice concrete grounds to write.
