## Petition versus the decision below

I read `questions-presented.txt`, the petition's substantive arguments, the matched `decision-below.txt`, `lower-court-docket.txt`, and relevant appendix passages. The lower opinion's caption, appeal No. 25-1068, May 11, 2026 judgment, and affirmed dismissal match the petition. It is the published sixteen-page opinion by Judge Bacharach, with Judges Moritz and Shelby on the panel. The lower-docket summary reports no en banc petition found in the one postjudgment docket entry it examined; that is limited observed coverage, not proof of an exhaustive rehearing history.

The question asks whether one mandatory training communicating derogatory racial stereotypes and directing race-conscious conduct can be sufficiently severe to create a hostile environment without repetition or subsequent misconduct. The petition, printed pages 17–22 and 26–29, argues that the Tenth Circuit displaced severity with a repetition requirement. Its strongest points are the lower court's repeated emphasis on a single training and on the absence of accompanying abuse, despite the employer's authorship and alleged workplace directives. At the pleading stage the allegations are credited, so whether the court demanded too much additional harm is a real legal issue, not merely an evidentiary dispute.

The principal reason for discounting a grant is that the lower court expressly recognizes **severe or pervasive** as alternatives, then evaluates this training and the alleged aftermath. See the decision below, pages 6–15. It credits the alleged glossary but finds no sufficiently abusive effect; it discusses assurances that employees need not change their beliefs, the absence of negative feedback or identified threats, and the conclusory or speculative character of the new allegations about discipline and security. The petition disputes those applications and inferences, but that does not establish that the panel categorically prohibited single-incident claims. The earlier opinion's opening discussion, appendix pages 97a–98a, likewise links its result to the training's alleged effects and speculation as well as its frequency.

The petition's comparators generally involve a directed racial epithet, a threat, or other especially acute harassment. Its own account acknowledges that Chislett involved repeated trainings plus additional workplace encounters. The Tenth Circuit itself opens by citing Chislett as an example of potentially actionable training, rather than rejecting its legal premise. The petition also acknowledges a later Tenth Circuit decision, Sharpe-Miller, expressly allowing a sufficiently severe single episode, and distinguishes it on its facts. That acknowledgment weakens the claim of an entrenched categorical circuit rule even though it does not eliminate the alleged training-specific error. I rely on the petition's description of Sharpe-Miller, not an independently retrieved opinion.

As a primary-source check, I retrieved the Second Circuit's September 25, 2025 Chislett opinion through CourtListener, opinion ID 11144199. The retrieved passages confirm both repeated trainings and workplace spillover, and explain that the manner of conducting training matters without making training inherently unlawful. Chislett's claims were framed under section 1983, adding another reason not to treat it as an exact single-session Title VII counterpart. This check supports factual distinguishability, not a forecast of this petition's merits. Two web attempts to locate Ames supplied no usable result; Ames is therefore considered only as characterized in the provisioned petition and lower opinion, which already applied its equal-treatment principle. No newly retrieved controlling decision supplies a ready GVR trigger.

## Other numbers and uncertainty

`relist-increment = 0.995` forecasts at least one more **distribution entry** beyond the existing one. A new distribution following the requested response is overwhelmingly likely even if the Court then denies immediately; this moment's claim is harness-masked and does not express 99.5% confidence in a substantive relist. The modal path is one further distribution and denial, with one or two additional conference relists a plausible less-likely path.

`cvsg-increment = 0.045` allows for federal employment-enforcement interests, but the issue is a familiar Title VII standard rather than a specialized federal administration question and the United States is not a party. I expect no CVSG.

`summary-disposition-route = 0.20` is **conditional on a grant**. An error-correcting summary reversal or vacatur is possible if the Court reads the opinion as imposing a repetition rule. Nonetheless, resolving the disputed training context is more suited to briefing and argument than to a routine GVR. The 20% is not an unconditional grant-route probability.

`dissent-from-denial = 0.12` is **conditional on denial**. The modal denial is silent; the challenge's race-conscious workplace setting and the potential mismatch between a disjunctive standard and its application create a modest separate-writing possibility. The independent order-writer probabilities are unconditional and remain small. Thomas and Alito receive the largest, followed by Gorsuch; Kavanaugh has a smaller possibility given the petition's cited discussion of severe isolated harassment in his Ayissi-Etoh concurrence. None of this is a claim that a vote to grant produces a writing, or that joining a writing is authoring it.

The **0.66 stakes score** is distinct from the grant number: a decision could materially affect employers' nationwide training practices, while the actual question remains narrower than a general prohibition on diversity training.

The largest uncertainty is whether the response will expose an independent vehicle problem or instead confirm that the dispute really is a legal rule about isolated mandatory training. I have not supplied either possibility as a fact.
