## Why denial still dominates

The alleged split is substantially about application, not an express disagreement over the governing rule. The petition itself acknowledges that the Tenth Circuit recognizes that a sufficiently severe single event can suffice. The current opinion expressly says the conduct can be either severe or pervasive, considers the amended allegations individually and together, and distinguishes offensive training from a materially abusive environment. The cited cases in other circuits largely involve targeted epithets, threats, repeated training, or additional workplace conduct. Those facts are meaningfully different from this record. The opinion also expressly recognizes that diversity training can create a hostile environment, citing Chislett, rather than granting such programs categorical immunity. [Sources: petition.txt, pp. 18–23 and n.8; decision-below.txt, pp. 3, 6–15.]

The employer-training setting is important, but the Court could regard this petition as fact-bound error correction, or await clearer disagreement on materially similar facts. Young's second lawsuit and the earlier appellate rejection add a prior-ruling complication: a grant would need to address the severity of the training itself rather than merely disagree with the handling of five new allegations. The Title VII claim supplies a vehicle independent of the Section 1981 claims, so I do not treat immunity questions as dispositive. The lower-court docket summary reports no located en banc petition or dissent in its limited one-entry review; I do not convert that limited coverage into certainty about every rehearing event. [Sources: petition.txt, pp. 15–16 and 25–26; decision-below.txt, pp. 6–15; lower-court-docket.txt.]

For general legal context, I checked the Court's official Ames opinion, No. 23-1039, decided June 5, 2025, syllabus pp. 1–2, and the EEOC's March 19, 2025 DEI guidance, question 10. Ames rejects an extra evidentiary burden for majority-group plaintiffs; it does not decide the severity of racial training. The EEOC guidance says discriminatory DEI training may support a hostile-environment claim depending on its circumstances; it is guidance, not a binding judicial resolution of this petition. Both predate the May 2026 judgment, and the opinion below already acknowledges Ames and the agencies' position. Neither creates a newly intervening reason to GVR. [Additional sources: Ames v. Ohio Department of Youth Services, 605 U.S. 303 (2025), official Supreme Court syllabus; EEOC, "What You Should Know About DEI-Related Discrimination at Work," question 10; decision-below.txt, p. 3 and concluding n.4.]
