## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear eight firefighters' challenge to a ruling that their employer could deny them religious exemptions from a COVID-19 vaccine mandate.

## What happened

Eight firefighters at Snohomish Regional Fire and Rescue asked for religious exemptions from a 2021 COVID-19 vaccine mandate. The department denied their requests, citing health and safety risks, a contract with a local jail, and potential liability, and placed them on unpaid leave for several months. The firefighters sued under Title VII, the federal law requiring employers to accommodate religious beliefs unless doing so would cause undue hardship. The district court ruled for the department, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, finding undisputed medical evidence showed accommodating the firefighters would have imposed real costs on the department.

## What the Court is being asked

The firefighters asked the Court to decide what an employer must prove to show that accommodating an employee's religion would cause undue hardship: whether it is enough for the employer to show it reasonably believed hardship would result, or whether the employer must show the hardship would actually have occurred. The firefighters argued the Ninth Circuit required only a reasonable belief, splitting from courts that require actual hardship. The department responded that the Ninth Circuit actually required proof of real, substantial costs, so no such conflict exists here.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and after further briefing including supplemental briefs from both sides, the Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The firefighters, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up their case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Ninth Circuit's decision in place, meaning the ruling against the firefighters stands and the department's denial of their accommodation requests was lawful. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that court's judgment as the final word in this case.
