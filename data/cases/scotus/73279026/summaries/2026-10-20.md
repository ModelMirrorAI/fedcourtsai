## In brief

The Court heard arguments over whether Colorado can require Catholic preschools to admit children regardless of sexual orientation or gender identity to receive preschool funding.

## What happened

Colorado's Universal Preschool Program (UPK) offers free preschool to all four-year-olds through public and private providers, including religious ones, but requires participating preschools to give children equal opportunity to enroll regardless of race, religious affiliation, sexual orientation, gender identity, income, disability, or housing status. St. Mary Catholic Parish, another parish, the Archdiocese of Denver, and two parents sued, arguing this requirement violates their religious beliefs, which lead them to decline enrolling children of same-sex parents or gender-nonconforming children. They noted Colorado allows other preschools to prioritize children with disabilities or from low-income families. A federal district court and the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals both ruled for Colorado, holding the requirement was religiously neutral and generally applicable.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide what kind of government rule-making counts as treating religion unfairly under the First Amendment's protection for religious exercise. Specifically: whether a challenger must show that a government program gives completely unrestricted discretion or lets people avoid a rule for exactly the same reasons claimed by a religious objector, before courts will apply the toughest constitutional test; and whether a separate line of cases barring government from excluding people from public benefits because of their religious conduct applies here, or only when a law explicitly singles out religion by name.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to two of the three questions presented, heard oral argument on November 3, 2026, and the case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

St. Mary Catholic Parish and the other schools and parents, as petitioners, are asking the Court to overturn the Tenth Circuit's ruling for Colorado. If the Court reverses, it would hold that Colorado's rule is subject to the strictest judicial scrutiny, making it harder for Colorado to enforce the equal-opportunity requirement against religious objectors and potentially letting these schools participate in UPK while limiting enrollment based on their religious beliefs. If the Court affirms, Colorado's requirement stands, and these schools remain unable to receive UPK funding unless they comply with it. The Court could also vacate and send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
