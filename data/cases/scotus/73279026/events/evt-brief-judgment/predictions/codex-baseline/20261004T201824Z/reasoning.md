## Why 0.83 rather than the roughly 0.70 anchor

The petitioner brief's main strengths are two routes to heightened scrutiny and a limited grant that permits a decision for the schools without formally overruling Smith. Its public-benefits argument attacks the Tenth Circuit's distinction between overt religious exclusion and a condition burdening religious exercise (printed pp. 18–20, 34–35). Its narrower argument identifies allegedly comparable secular enrollment preferences and administrative accommodation within the same funding program (pp. 35–41). I expect the latter route to be the more likely controlling ground because it can produce relief without adopting a general exemption from public-benefits conditions.

Fulton v. Philadelphia, 593 U.S. 522 (2021), retrieved through CourtListener opinion 4696349, supplies a concrete doctrinal analogy, not a frequency estimate. Its discussion of individualized exceptions and the government's specific interest in denying an accommodation supports the possibility of a narrow Free Exercise ruling while leaving Smith intact. The respondent brief identifies the critical distinction: Fulton's contract expressly permitted exceptions, whereas Colorado maintains that its administrators may adjust a matching algorithm but never waive statutory equal opportunity (respondent pp. 44–45). I do not equate those two records or treat discretion over matching as automatically equivalent to discretion to discriminate.

Colorado also has a substantial substantive response. Its brief says religious teaching and religious providers are permitted, and describes the condition as regulating admission rather than religious status or curriculum (pp. 1–3, 20–22). Specialized provision for low-income children and children with disabilities may implement, rather than undermine, the statute's equal-opportunity purpose. That is a serious objection to treating every differentiated admission practice as a comparable secular exemption (pp. 42–43). The trial record and the statutory restrictions on discretion could persuade a majority to affirm or require a narrower remand.

The petitioners propose disclosure and alternative providers as less restrictive means (pp. 51–53). Colorado responds that its interest is freedom from protected-status exclusion from an otherwise available publicly funded school, not simply availability of some seat somewhere; location and access to a preferred school's educational quality matter (respondent pp. 54–57). I therefore do not accept the unsupported shortcut that other available schools necessarily eliminate harm. Nor do I accept the petitioners' assertion that Colorado conceded speculative harm: respondent footnote 17 says the cited motion concerned standing and ripeness, while the trial credited evidence supporting the State's interests.

Nevertheless, my forecast is that six Justices will view the program's accommodation of secular enrollment priorities as requiring more accommodation of religious admissions practices than the Tenth Circuit allowed. This is an interpretive judgment from the briefs and the precedent, not an observed conditional success rate. It raises my disturbed probability to **0.83**, while the contested comparators, adverse trial findings, and real nondiscrimination interests keep it materially below certainty.

An illustrative distribution underlying that number is reversal 0.56, vacatur 0.24, partial affirmance/reversal 0.03, affirmance 0.14, DIG 0.025, and equally divided affirmance 0.005. The three disturbed labels sum to 0.83. The meaningful distinction between reversal and vacatur is whether the Court itself resolves scrutiny on this record or remands for the lower courts to apply the corrected rule.

## Votes, scope, and stakes

The 6–3 lineup is a prediction, not an inference that granting certiorari revealed six merits votes. Roberts, Kavanaugh, and Barrett are the most important uncertainty in the forecast's breadth: a narrow comparator ruling could attract them even if a general public-benefits exception would not. A narrower resolution could also attract a dissent-predicted Justice, so the exact lineup is less secure than the disturbed binary. I assign no writing roles because I have no comparably strong basis for authorship or separate-writing forecasts.

The semantic propositions deliberately choose a ground and a breadth separately. I predict unequal treatment of comparable secular and religious admission practices, with a program-specific accommodation holding rather than a categorical exception from all nondiscrimination conditions. An opinion resting only on a broad Carson benefits rule would make the selected ground wrong even if the schools win.

The **0.86 stakes score** reflects the implications for publicly supported religious education, religious autonomy, and equal access for LGBTQ and other families. It is not a probability of victory or a measure of post-decision publicity. Exclusion of the Smith-overruling question keeps this below the stakes of a wholesale replacement of the governing Free Exercise framework.
