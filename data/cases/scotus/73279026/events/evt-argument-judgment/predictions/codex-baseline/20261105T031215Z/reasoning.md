## Anchor and adjustment

**P(judgment disturbed) = 0.94.** The committed merits section publishes exclusions and is therefore usable. The required ten-grant-Term window is 2015–2024. Available rows within it are 2017–2024: **375 disturbed / 533 parsed judgments = 70.36%**, with **533 parsed / 549 granted** coverage and **40 cert-order exclusions outside that granted cohort**. There are no rendered 2015–2016 rows; I assign those no invented observations. The pool easily clears 30. I exclude OT2025 and do not use the pack-wide rate. This is a parsed, date-observable population; missing judgments combine pendency and capture gaps, and the nearest prior Term can favor quicker decisions. It is a useful anchor, not a claim that 70.36% is this case's answer.

The roughly 24-point upward adjustment rests on two independently plausible routes to heightened scrutiny and, more importantly at this moment, substantive signals from six Justices. My indicative judgment probabilities are reversal 0.70, vacatur 0.21, mixed affirmance/reversal 0.03, ordinary affirmance 0.05 and DIG 0.01. Those are mutually exclusive forecasting allocations, not frequencies estimated from a matched sample. The disturbed outcomes sum to 0.94. Vacatur remains meaningful if the Court settles the standard and sends its application back; the existing trial and competing strict-scrutiny analyses make outright reversal the better exact-label call. No known recusal makes an equally divided affirmance a live alternative.

## Briefing: two routes and a genuine defense

Petitioners' opening brief, pp. 18–20, 28–53, argues both that Sherbert/Thomas/Carson govern exclusion from educational benefits for lawful religious exercise and that Colorado fails Smith's general-applicability requirement anyway. The latter theory targets income/disability-based enrollment restrictions and the catchall matching preference. Their reply, especially pp. 1–5, presses the difference between formally rigid regulations and allegedly flexible administration. Their strongest practical point is the burden on Catholic families' ability to use the same per-child subsidy, not an entitlement to a government-operated religious preschool.

Colorado's merits brief, pp. 20–22 and 51–57, offers substantial answers: religious providers and religious teaching are welcome; the rule regulates exclusion based on protected characteristics rather than religion; disability and poverty provisions advance equal opportunity rather than excepting favored groups from it; algorithmic flexibility is constrained by the statutory mandate. It also preserves an alternative strict-scrutiny defense. Its asserted interest is **equal access to each available publicly funded option**, not merely enough seats somewhere. Location, opening hours and siblings can make alternatives inadequate. It would be wrong to say Colorado conceded that Carson applies or that strict scrutiny necessarily defeats it; that is petitioners' characterization, not Colorado's position.

The United States supports **reversal**, on both lack of general applicability and an independent public-benefits rule (amicus pp. 11–13, 36–37). Harris expressly preferred the public-benefits route at argument. I follow that recommendation on result and, less confidently, on rationale. It matters because it supplies a concrete way to protect religious admissions without granting Question 3, but it is not a controlling judicial view.

Fulton v. Philadelphia, 593 U.S. 522 (2021), retrieved through CourtListener, confirms an important distinction: the Court there avoided overruling Smith, found a discretionary-exception defect, and assessed the interest in denying the particular accommodation. That makes a narrower route credible here. It does not establish that every nondiscrimination condition is invalid or that Colorado's exceptions are comparable. Those remain disputed questions in this record.

## What argument adds

The **content**, not the mere volume, of questioning moves this forecast strongly toward the schools:

- **Roberts:** initially questions the reach of Carson beyond express classifications and asks how a benefits rule is bounded. Later he says the effect resembles Carson, presses whether Sherbert has actually been displaced, and treats Colorado's asserted harms as matters that can be handled within strict scrutiny. This progression is more favorable to reversal than his opening questions alone suggest.
- **Thomas:** asks each supporting advocate whether Carson needs extending, then asks Colorado why Smith should govern when the recent benefits decisions did not invoke it. Sparse questioning but a discernible concern with Colorado's doctrinal premise.
- **Alito and Gorsuch:** repeatedly challenge making protection depend on express religious wording. Gorsuch emphasizes evasion and the practical experience of RFRA; Alito presses religious admissions and the limited demonstrated harm. These are strong directional signals for the schools, not merely difficult hypotheticals posed neutrally to counsel.
- **Kavanaugh:** fairly tests the anomaly of greater scrutiny for funding than prohibition, but develops both routes to strict scrutiny, questions why a workable accommodation was not pursued, challenges other permissible enrollment restrictions and emphasizes the current inability to prefer coreligionists. His respondent-side exchanges are strongly unfavorable to affirmance.
- **Barrett:** tests the original-meaning and administrability issues, then questions the practical difference between an express exclusion and a known exclusionary condition. Her examination of RFRA practice and the absence of a demonstrated preschool problem favors heightened scrutiny. Her closing acceptance of Colorado's explanation about investigating a parental statement is a genuine counter-signal about application and breadth, not a reversal of the entire lean.
- **Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson:** insist the requested exception permits unequal treatment of families, warn that petitioners seek preferential rather than equal treatment, and probe Martinez, race analogies and the distinction between religious instruction and admission. I read these as sustained resistance to the broad benefits theory. They might join a narrow general-applicability correction, so a broader coalition is possible; it is not my modal lineup.

## Votes, writers and remaining uncertainty

I predict **6–3**, with Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh and Barrett in the majority, and Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson dissenting. I expect six to join a common opinion rather than three concurring only in judgment, but that distinction is less secure than their support for disturbing the judgment.

There is no staged sitting-assignment inventory, and I infer none. The tiny probabilities on the anticipated dissenters allow for an unexpectedly narrow coalition.

Sotomayor is my lead dissent-author call, not a claim that each dissenter will write independently. Distribution probabilities remain deliberately dispersed.

The main uncertainty is **ground and remedy**, not the grant or the existence of religious burdens. The Court could prefer the less ambitious Tandon/Fulton path, could adopt an education-specific accommodation rule narrower than the government's all-benefits position, or could remand strict scrutiny instead of deciding it. Roberts's and Barrett's limiting-principle questions keep me from treating broad acceptance of the government's theory as nearly certain. My semantic propositions commit to the school-aid benefits route, not to overruling Smith wholesale or immunizing every religious admissions rule from a compelling-interest defense. The 0.86 significance score reflects that rule's potential reach beyond this preschool program and the rights conflict the parties actually litigated.
