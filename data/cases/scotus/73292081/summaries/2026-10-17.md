## In brief

A Texas death-row inmate asks the Court to revive a habeas claim that his trial lawyers missed a flaw in key drug evidence used to convict him.

## What happened

Richard Vasquez was convicted of capital murder in Texas for killing his girlfriend's four-year-old daughter and sentenced to death. Years later, a toxicologist hired by his lawyers found an inconsistency in a drug test used at trial to show the child had cocaine in her system. Vasquez sought permission to file a new habeas petition raising a claim that his trial lawyers were constitutionally ineffective for not catching this earlier. The Fifth Circuit allowed him to pursue the claim but ultimately ruled against him, holding he had not acted diligently in discovering the problem and had not shown enough evidence of innocence. The district court had dismissed the petition on the diligence ground alone.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in deciding if a prisoner acted diligently enough to raise a new claim in a late-stage habeas petition, courts should measure diligence by what a reasonable lawyer could have discovered or by what the prisoner himself could have discovered, and whether an exception applies when a prisoner's own lawyers are accused of serious misconduct. The petition also raises questions about whether certain evidence-suppression claims count as repeat filings subject to extra limits, and about which Texas official may speak for the state in these proceedings.

## Where it stands

## What each outcome would mean

Vasquez, the inmate, is asking the Court to hear his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions about diligence and related issues, without that grant itself deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision rejecting Vasquez's claims stands, and his conviction and sentence remain in place as currently upheld by the lower courts.
