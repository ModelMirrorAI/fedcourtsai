# Certiorari Arrival Disposition Forecast: *Noah A. Herzon, et al. v. Robert Arias* (No. 26-111)

## 1. Case Overview and Procedural Posture at Arrival Cutoff

At the arrival cutoff of July 24, 2026 (`2026-07-24.json`), Petitioners Noah A. Herzon and five other DEA agents and task-force officers—represented by Solicitor General D. John Sauer and the Department of Justice—have filed a paid petition for a writ of certiorari (docketed July 23, 2026, following an extension in No. 25A1226) seeking review of a published, divided decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the First Circuit (*Arias v. Herzon*, 150 F.4th 27 (1st Cir. Aug. 15, 2025), reh'g and reh'g en banc denied Feb. 24, 2026).

Respondent Robert Arias sued the task-force officers in the District of New Hampshire under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents of Federal Bureau of Narcotics*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), alleging that they used excessive force in violation of the Fourth Amendment when executing an arrest warrant against him in a shopping mall parking lot in Salem, New Hampshire (and that other officers failed to intervene). The district court (680 F. Supp. 3d 61) granted summary judgment to the officers under *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), holding that although the warrant and parking-lot location did not meaningfully distinguish the excessive-force claim from *Bivens*, the post-*Bivens* enactment of the Inspector General Act Amendments of 1988 (IGA) created an alternative administrative remedial structure that placed the claim in a new context at step one and foreclosed a *Bivens* extension at step two.

A divided First Circuit panel (Chief Judge Barron, joined by Judge Thompson) reversed as to the excessive-force claim and remanded (while affirming as to the unchallenged failure-to-intervene claim). The majority held that:
1. Neither the existence of an arrest warrant nor the location of the arrest outside the home (in a shopping center parking lot) meaningfully distinguished the Fourth Amendment excessive-force claim from *Bivens* at step one (joining the Seventh Circuit in *Snowden v. Henning*, 72 F.4th 237 (2023), and the Tenth Circuit in *Logsdon v. U.S. Marshal Service*, 91 F.4th 1352 (2024), while rejecting contrary rulings from the Fourth, Fifth, and Ninth Circuits).
2. The existence of the IGA administrative complaint mechanism did not by itself create a "new context" at step one of the *Bivens* inquiry, because treating a general administrative complaint mechanism as sufficient to create a new context at step one would mean *Bivens* itself has been a dead letter since 1988 despite the Supreme Court's repeated statements declining to overrule *Bivens*. Because the panel found no new context at step one, it declined to reach step two.

Judge Lynch dissented in detail (App. 44a–57a) and dissented from the denial of rehearing (App. 75a), arguing that the majority contravened *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. 120 (2017), *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), and *Goldey v. Fields*, 606 U.S. 942 (2025) (per curiam), and deepened multiple circuit splits on both (a) whether warrant-authorized or out-of-home arrests constitute a new *Bivens* context and (b) whether a post-*Bivens* alternative remedial scheme creates a new context or merges steps one and two into a single inquiry.

Crucially, on June 22, 2026—one month before this petition was filed—the Supreme Court granted certiorari in *Nielsen v. Watanabe*, No. 25-417, to review a Ninth Circuit decision (*Watanabe v. Derr*, 115 F.4th 1034 (9th Cir. 2024)) involving the scope of *Carlson v. Green*, 446 U.S. 14 (1980), whether factual distinctions and alternative remedial schemes create a new context at step one of the *Bivens* framework, and the continuing vitality of *Bivens*/*Carlson* claims. The Solicitor General's petition in *Herzon* asks the Court to grant plenary review on the Fourth Amendment warrant/location question or, "[a]t a minimum, ... hold the petition for a writ of certiorari pending the resolution of *Nielsen v. Watanabe*, cert. granted, No. 25-417 (June 22, 2026)" (Pet. 2–3, 5, 15–16).

## 3. Case-Specific Factors Moving Away from the Anchor

### A. Strong Upward Drivers (Favoring `granted: true` via GVR or Plenary/Summary Grant)
1. **Solicitor General as Petitioner**: The Solicitor General is counsel of record representing federal law enforcement officers after the Department of Justice authorized certiorari. The Supreme Court grants plenary review, summarily reverses, or GVRs the vast majority of SG-filed petitions, particularly where a court of appeals has invalidated federal action or exposed federal officers to personal damages liability.
2. **Explicit Hold Request Pending *Nielsen v. Watanabe* (No. 25-417)**: The Court granted certiorari in *Nielsen v. Watanabe* on June 22, 2026 (to be argued and decided in OT2026). Both *Nielsen* and the First Circuit's decision here turn on the exact same methodological divide in post-*Egbert* *Bivens* doctrine: whether relatively modest factual distinctions render a context "new" at step one, and whether the existence of an alternative remedial scheme (such as the BOP Administrative Remedy Program in *Nielsen* or the DOJ Inspector General Act process here) is only relevant at step two if the context is otherwise deemed new, or whether it independently defeats a *Bivens* claim / collapses steps one and two. When the Solicitor General explicitly asks the Court to hold a petition pending a granted merits case that addresses the same analytical framework, the Court's standard practice is to hold the petition if it does not grant plenary review, and then GVR once the lead case (*Nielsen*) is decided against the plaintiff (which is highly likely given the Court's unbroken 46-year streak rejecting *Bivens* claims, most recently in *Goldey v. Fields*, 606 U.S. 942 (2025), and *Cisco Systems, Inc. v. Doe*, 146 S. Ct. 1882 (2026)).
3. **Acknowledged Circuit Conflict and Vigorous Dissent Below**: Both the First Circuit majority and Judge Lynch's dissent expressly acknowledge that the circuits are split (First, Seventh, and Tenth vs. Fourth, Fifth, and Ninth) on whether a warrant-authorized arrest or an arrest outside the home presents a new *Bivens* context, and Judge Lynch flagged an additional split on whether an alternative remedial structure alone bars *Bivens* relief.
4. **Supreme Court Hostility to *Bivens* Expansions**: Just one Term earlier in *Goldey v. Fields*, 606 U.S. 942 (2025) (per curiam), the Court summarily reversed a Fourth Circuit decision allowing a *Bivens* claim to proceed. The Court has repeatedly intervened to reverse lower-court decisions permitting *Bivens* suits (*Egbert*, *Hernández*, *Abbasi*, *Goldey*).

### B. Downward / Moderating Factors (Why Probability is ~0.72 Rather Than >0.85, and Why `gvr` Exceeds Plenary `granted`)
1. **Prior Denials on the Warrant/Location Issue in Private/Officer Petitions**: As noted in the petition (Pet. 12), the Court previously denied certiorari in *Snowden v. Henning*, 145 S. Ct. 137 (2024) (where an individual hotel-lobby arrestee prevailed in the Seventh Circuit on the warrant/location issue), though *Snowden* did not feature the Solicitor General asking for review alongside a pending *Bivens* merits grant like *Nielsen* and did not squarely present the Inspector General Act argument that split the First Circuit panel here.
2. **Interlocutory Posture & Narrow Framing of the Question Presented**: Although summary judgment denials on *Bivens* / qualified immunity are routinely reviewed when the SG petitions, the SG framed the Question Presented specifically around the warrant and outside-the-home distinctions (while relegating the IGA step-one/step-two issue to the *Nielsen* hold discussion). If the Court prefers to let *Nielsen* settle the overarching step-one/step-two and alternative-remedy methodology first (or potentially overrule or further cabin *Bivens*/*Carlson*), holding *Herzon* for *Nielsen* and then issuing a GVR is much more economical than granting plenary argument in a second *Bivens* case in the same Term.
3. **Possibility of Denial After *Nielsen* or Immediate Denial**: There remains a ~27% chance that the Court denies the petition—either because it views the Fourth Amendment warrant/parking-lot context as distinct enough from *Nielsen*'s Eighth Amendment *Carlson* context not to hold, or if *Nielsen* is resolved on narrow prison-specific grounds (e.g., the PLRA/ARP) that do not trigger a GVR for a Fourth Amendment arrest claim.
