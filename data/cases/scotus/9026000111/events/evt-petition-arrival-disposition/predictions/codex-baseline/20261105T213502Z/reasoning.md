## Evidence for review or a GVR

1. **A concrete legal disagreement.** The petition identifies conflicting approaches to whether a warrant and an arrest outside the home create a new Bivens context (pp. 12–15). The Seventh Circuit's Snowden v. Henning, 72 F.4th 237, 247 (2023), independently retrieved through CourtListener, treats those distinctions as insufficient for the ordinary excessive-force claim there. The adverse authorities and the First Circuit dissent are set out in the petition and bound appendix. I discount the petition's neat three-two-one count: its own account says Logsdon ultimately rejected relief on another ground, so agreement about one analytical step does not mean identical outcomes in three circuits.

2. **A focused vehicle.** The revived excessive-force claim involves federal narcotics enforcement, an arrest warrant, and a public parking lot. The First Circuit rejected the failure-to-intervene claim; that is not the proposed question. The petition says special factors would defeat relief if a new context exists (pp. 8–9), concentrating the dispute on that threshold. The panel was divided and Judge Lynch dissented from denial of panel rehearing (appendix, pp. 44a–57a, 74a–75a). I distinguish that from a dissent from denial of en banc rehearing. The interlocutory remand and remaining factual dispute over force modestly weaken the vehicle, even though the cause-of-action issue is legal.

3. **Recent summary intervention makes grant-side action plausible.** Goldey v. Fields, 606 U.S. 942, 944–945 (2025), independently read through CourtListener, summarily reversed recognition of an Eighth Amendment excessive-force Bivens action. It confirms both the restrictive new-context framework and the availability of summary reversal. It does not itself decide whether this Fourth Amendment arrest claim is new; I do not treat it as an on-point mandate.

4. **An existing lead case lowers the cost of acting.** The petition expressly requests, alternatively, a hold for Nielsen (pp. 15–16). Its account connects both cases to what makes a context new and to the role of alternative remedies. The corpus independently confirms Nielsen's grant. That is the main reason GVR outranks a separate plenary grant. The link is imperfect: Nielsen concerns medical-care claims under the Eighth Amendment, whereas this case concerns an arrest under the Fourth Amendment. A narrow Nielsen decision could leave this judgment untouched or leave the Court wanting a separate vehicle.

The petition also reports a prior cert denial in Snowden. That cautions against equating a split with inevitable review, although the present federal sponsorship, later authorities, and already granted lead case distinguish this posture. A CourtListener citation lookup did not locate Orellana; I treat its characterization as the petition's account, not as independently verified authority. I place no weight on the petition's additional 2026 precedents that I did not independently examine.
