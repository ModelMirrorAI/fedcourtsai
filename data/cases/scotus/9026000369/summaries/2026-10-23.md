## In brief

A father whose federal lawsuit over a divorce arbitration's restraining order was dismissed asks the Court to let his constitutional claims proceed.

## What happened

During John Torsten Loop's divorce, a court ordered the parties into arbitration. The arbitrator, a retired judge working for JAMS, issued an award that included a restraining order barring Loop from contact with his daughter until September 2025. A Washington state court entered that order, and state appellate courts upheld it. Loop then sued the State of Washington, its Attorney General, JAMS, and the arbitrator in federal court, arguing the process violated his constitutional rights as a parent. The district court dismissed the case for lack of jurisdiction, holding the suit was really an improper attempt to appeal the state court's decision. The Ninth Circuit affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things: first, whether a legal rule that blocks federal courts from reviewing state-court judgments also blocks a separate lawsuit claiming the procedures leading up to that judgment were unconstitutional, rather than challenging the judgment itself. Second, whether a state can let a private arbitrator—rather than a judge following required procedures—decide an issue that seriously affects a fit parent's rights regarding his child.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in September 2026, and the state respondents waived their right to respond, as did JAMS and the arbitrator. Several outside groups have since filed amicus briefs supporting Loop. The docket shows no action yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Loop, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the two legal questions, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Loop's lawsuit against Washington, JAMS, and the arbitrator remains dismissed and his constitutional claims will not be heard in federal court.
