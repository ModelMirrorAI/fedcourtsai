## In brief

A father asks the Court to revive his federal lawsuit challenging how a private arbitrator and state courts restricted his contact with his child.

## What happened

John Torsten Loop and his then-wife arbitrated their divorce, and the arbitrator, a retired judge working for JAMS, issued an award including a restraining order barring Loop from contact with his daughter until September 2025. A Washington state court entered that order, and Washington's appellate courts upheld it. Loop separately sued the State of Washington, its Attorney General, JAMS, and the arbitrator in federal court, arguing the process violated his constitutional rights. The district court dismissed the suit for lack of jurisdiction, applying a doctrine that bars federal courts from hearing cases that amount to appeals of state court judgments. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things: whether the jurisdictional doctrine that blocks federal review of state court judgments should have stopped this lawsuit, given that Loop says he was challenging the deficient procedures that led to the restraining order rather than the judgment itself; and whether a state can let a private arbitrator decide something as significant as restricting a parent's contact with a child without the procedural protections the Constitution would otherwise require before the state could impose such a restriction.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and docketed, and the State of Washington and its Attorney General waived their right to file a response. The docket shows no further action since that waiver was filed.

## What each outcome would mean

Loop is asking the Court to hear his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions about jurisdiction and about delegating parental-rights decisions to private arbitrators; a grant alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision affirming dismissal of Loop's lawsuit stands, leaving him without a federal forum for these claims as the lower courts ruled.
