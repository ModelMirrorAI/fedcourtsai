## In brief

A father asks the Court to revive his federal civil-rights lawsuit over how arbitration led to a restraining order limiting his contact with his child.

## What happened

John Torsten Loop and his former wife went through a divorce in Washington state court, which ordered them into arbitration. The arbitrator issued a restraining order restricting Loop's contact with his child, which the state court later confirmed; Washington's appeals courts and supreme court left that outcome in place. Loop then sued the state, the arbitration provider, the arbitrator, and Washington's attorney general in federal court, arguing the process violated his constitutional rights as a parent. The district court dismissed the suit, holding it was barred by a doctrine that keeps federal courts from reviewing state-court judgments. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and rehearing was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions. First, whether a rule that keeps federal courts from acting as an appeals court over state-court judgments also blocks a separate lawsuit claiming the procedures used before and leading up to that judgment were unconstitutional, as opposed to challenging the judgment itself. Second, whether the Constitution allows a state to hand off decisions that substantially affect a fit parent's relationship with a child to a private arbitrator without first providing the procedural protections ordinarily required before the state interferes with that relationship.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed on September 15, 2026, and docketed September 17, 2026. A response from the respondents is due October 19, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Loop, the petitioner, asks the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the two questions, without yet determining who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision affirming dismissal of Loop's lawsuit stands, leaving the restraining order and the lower courts' jurisdictional ruling in place. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower courts were right—only that their decision remains final.
