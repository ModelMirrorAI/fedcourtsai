## What happened

Stephen Johnson was charged in Montana with crimes including theft and bail jumping. Before trial, the state asked to have a key witness, Eugene Maw, testify by two-way video rather than in person, because Maw had moved several hundred miles away, was elderly, and had caregiving responsibilities. Johnson objected that this would violate his constitutional right to confront witnesses against him. The trial court allowed the video testimony, and Johnson was convicted. The Montana Supreme Court upheld the conviction, applying a standard from an older Supreme Court case that allows remote testimony when it serves an important public policy and is otherwise reliable, rather than a stricter, later standard requiring that a witness be genuinely unavailable before live testimony can be replaced.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks the Court to settle which legal test governs when a witness in a criminal case may testify by two-way video instead of appearing in person. Courts around the country apply different rules: some use an older, more flexible test asking whether remote testimony serves an important purpose and remains reliable; others use a stricter, more recent test that permits skipping face-to-face testimony only if the witness is truly unavailable and the defense already had a chance to cross-examine them; and one federal appeals court uses a different standard altogether. Johnson argues the stricter test should apply and that Maw, who was available to testify, should have appeared in person.
