## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a Montana man's challenge to his conviction over a witness's testimony by two-way video at his trial.

## What happened

Stephen Joseph Johnson was convicted in Montana of burglary and other crimes after fleeing a crashed vehicle and hiding in Eugene Maw's garage. Because Maw, an elderly man with caretaking duties, had since moved out of state, the trial court let him testify at trial by two-way video over Johnson's objection. The Montana Supreme Court affirmed, holding that the video testimony satisfied the Confrontation Clause, which guarantees a defendant's right to confront witnesses against him, under the standard set in an older Supreme Court case called Craig rather than a later case called Crawford.

## What the Court is being asked

Johnson asked the Court to decide which legal test governs whether letting a prosecution witness testify by live two-way video, rather than in person, violates a defendant's confrontation right. Johnson argued the newer case's stricter approach, requiring the witness be truly unavailable, should control; Montana argued the older case's more flexible balancing test remains good law for this situation.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court denied it on October 13, 2026, without hearing the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Johnson, who lost in the Montana Supreme Court, asked the Court to take up his case. The denial leaves the Montana Supreme Court's decision in place, so Johnson's conviction stands and the video-testimony question goes undecided by the Court. Had the Court instead granted the petition, it would have heard full briefing and argument on which standard applies, deciding the legal question but not who ultimately wins; that would have continued rather than ended Johnson's case.
