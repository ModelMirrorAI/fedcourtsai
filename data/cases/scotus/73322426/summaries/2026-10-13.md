## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a Montana man's challenge to his burglary conviction, which rested partly on a witness's testimony by two-way video.

## What happened

Stephen Johnson was convicted in Montana of criminal mischief, burglary, and bail jumping after crashing a car and hiding in a stranger's garage. The homeowner, Eugene Maw, testified against him remotely by two-way video because of his age, caretaking duties, and distance from the courthouse. Johnson argued this violated his right to confront witnesses face to face. The Montana Supreme Court disagreed, holding that a 1990 Supreme Court case governing remote testimony, not a later 2004 case about out-of-court statements, supplied the right standard, and upheld Johnson's conviction.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked which of two of its own precedents governs when a prosecution witness may testify at trial by two-way video rather than in person: an older ruling allowing remote testimony when it serves an important public policy and remains reliable, or a newer ruling that focuses on whether the witness was unavailable and the defendant had a prior chance to cross-examine. Johnson says courts are split on which rule applies; Montana says most courts agree and treat the two rulings as addressing different situations.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after the Court requested a response from Montana and after Johnson filed a reply. The Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Johnson asked the Court to take up his case and overturn the Montana Supreme Court's ruling. By denying the petition, the Court leaves that ruling in place: Johnson's conviction stands, and the Montana Supreme Court's use of the older standard for remote witness testimony remains the law in Montana. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with Montana's approach, only that it left the decision undisturbed.
