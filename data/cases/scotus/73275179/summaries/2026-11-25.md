## In brief

Gun owners and advocacy groups asked the Supreme Court to strike down Cook County's ban on AR-15-style rifles as a violation of the Second Amendment.

## What happened

Cutberto Viramontes and others sued Cook County, Illinois, arguing that its ban on AR-15-style and similar semiautomatic rifles violates the Second Amendment. The district court ruled for the county, relying on Seventh Circuit precedent upholding similar bans. The Seventh Circuit affirmed, holding that Viramontes had failed to build an adequate factual record to distinguish his case from that precedent. Cook County responds that the ban targets exceptionally dangerous weapons disproportionate to self-defense, and that every circuit to consider the question except one has upheld similar bans. The Supreme Court later granted review, consolidating the case with a similar challenge to a Connecticut law, and the case was argued; the Court has not yet issued a decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Second Amendment protects a right to possess AR-15-style and similar semiautomatic rifles, or whether governments may ban them as unusually dangerous weapons not suited to ordinary self-defense. The dispute turns on whether these rifles count as weapons "in common use" that cannot be banned, or whether their military origins and capacity for harm place them outside constitutional protection despite their popularity.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition, consolidated the case with a related challenge to Connecticut's law, and heard oral argument on December 2, 2026. One of the original petitioners, Cutberto Viramontes, has since been dismissed from the case under a Supreme Court rule allowing a party's dismissal, and the Court has deferred a request by Cook County to dismiss the case as moot until after argument. The case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Viramontes and the other petitioners are asking the Court to overturn the lower court's ruling, which upheld Cook County's ban. If the Court rules for the petitioners, the ban would be struck down, and Cook County residents could legally possess the affected rifles. If the Court rules for Cook County, the ban stays in place, and similar bans in other states and cities would likely remain enforceable as well. A ruling that sends the case back without resolving the question would leave the matter undecided for further proceedings.
