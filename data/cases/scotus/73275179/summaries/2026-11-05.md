## In brief

The Supreme Court heard a Second Amendment challenge to Cook County's ban on AR-15-style rifles, consolidated with a similar Connecticut case.

## What happened

Cutberto Viramontes and other Illinois gun owners sued Cook County, arguing its ban on "assault weapons," including AR-15-style rifles, violates the Second Amendment. The district court and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Seventh Circuit ruled for the county, relying on circuit precedent that treated such rifles as outside the Second Amendment's protection or as historically subject to being banned. Viramontes asked the Supreme Court to take the case, arguing that AR-15s are commonly owned and therefore protected under the Court's precedents, and that the lower courts are divided on how to analyze bans on weapon types.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether the Constitution protects the right to own AR-15-style and similar semiautomatic rifles, or whether governments may ban them as unusually dangerous weapons outside the scope of that right. The case requires deciding how courts should determine whether a type of firearm is common enough among law-abiding citizens to be protected, and what historical principles justify or forbid banning a class of weapons entirely.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, consolidated the case with a companion case from Connecticut, and heard oral argument on December 2, 2026. The case was fully briefed with merits briefs and numerous amicus filings, and a motion to dismiss one of the petitioners was submitted shortly before argument; the docket does not yet show a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Viramontes and the other gun owners who sued are asking the Court to overturn the Seventh Circuit's ruling that upheld Cook County's ban. If the Court rules for Viramontes, the county's ban on AR-15-style rifles would likely be struck down, allowing residents to own these rifles. If the Court rules for Cook County, the ban would stand, and residents would remain barred from possessing the listed weapons. The Court could also send the case back for further proceedings without resolving which side is right.
