# Rationale

I assign a 0.88 probability that the Court disturbs the judgment below and select `reversed` as the most likely exact judgment. The committed merits baseline for the required ten-Term window is 359 disturbed judgments among 515 parsed judgments, or 69.7%. The contributing OT2017–OT2024 rows cover 515 of 557 grants (92.5%); OT2015 and OT2016 have no published merits rows. The statpack includes the required exclusion guard, with 57 cert-order dispositions excluded from these contributing rows. This is a parsed-slice baseline, so remaining coverage gaps and selection into a merits proceeding limit its precision.

I adjust materially above that baseline because the question granted is broad and outcome-relevant: whether the Second and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee a right to possess AR-15-platform and similar semiautomatic rifles. The petition identifies unusually direct pre-grant signals. Justice Kavanaugh wrote in *Snope* that the common-use argument for AR-15 protection is strong and that the Court should address the issue soon; Justice Thomas had separately called for review after final judgment. The petition also relies on *Heller*, *Bruen*, *Rahimi*, and the Court's description in *Smith & Wesson* of the AR-15 as the country's most popular rifle. The Court granted after a final judgment and consolidated No. 25-566 with this case, making it less likely that it intends simply to endorse the Seventh Circuit's rule.

On the merits, petitioners have the stronger fit with the current Court's text-and-history method. An AR-15 is a bearable semiautomatic firearm, while the Seventh Circuit's threshold “military” classification depends heavily on analogy to a select-fire M16. Respondents' proposed historical analogues—affray rules, proportionality in self-defense, military-weapon restrictions, and gunpowder regulation—do not closely resemble a categorical ban on possession of a widely owned firearm. Those considerations support a six-Justice majority to disturb the judgment.

The main discount is vehicle and record quality. Petitioners deliberately resisted factual development, disclosed no experts, and relied on late or disputed materials; the Seventh Circuit affirmed in part because the record did not distinguish the rifles from military weapons under *Bevis*. Respondents also argue that all post-*Bruen* circuits to reach assault-weapons bans have upheld them. Those defects create a meaningful chance of affirmance or a narrow procedural disposition, although the consolidated grant reduces the likelihood that this case's record alone defeats review. Even on the favored path, the Court could vacate and remand under a corrected legal test instead of reversing outright.

This is a forward, grant-moment forecast. I used the provisioned June 30 grant docket, complete petition, complete brief in opposition, and questions presented. No merits briefs or argument content existed in the provisioned record; the docket showed only the post-grant briefing schedule. I did not use the cert-stage salience band or 22 distributions as evidence about the merits judgment, and I made no CourtListener, corpus-prior, or web lookup.
