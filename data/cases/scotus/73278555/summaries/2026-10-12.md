## In brief

Retirement plan participants ask the Court to decide whether ERISA imprudence claims based on a fund's poor performance must identify a comparable alternative investment to survive dismissal.

## What happened

Winston Anderson and other former Intel employees sued the committees and individuals who managed Intel's retirement plans, claiming they breached their duty under a federal pension law (ERISA) to invest plan assets prudently. The plaintiffs alleged that starting around 2009 the fiduciaries shifted large portions of two retirement funds into hedge funds and private equity, producing lower returns and higher fees than comparable options. The district court dismissed the claims twice, finding the plaintiffs had not identified a "meaningful benchmark" fund with similar goals and risks for comparison. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, agreeing that such a benchmark is generally required when a claim rests on comparing investment performance.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a retirement-plan participant who claims a fund manager acted imprudently because an investment underperformed must point to a comparable investment with similar goals, risks, and potential rewards to support that claim, or whether other kinds of allegations can be enough without such a comparison.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review of the petition on January 16, 2026. The case has been fully briefed and argued, with argument held on October 6, 2026; it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Anderson and the other plan participants, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up and reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling. If the Court affirms, the Ninth Circuit's requirement that plaintiffs identify a comparable benchmark investment stands, and the dismissal of Anderson's claims remains in place. If the Court reverses, it would reject that requirement as the Ninth Circuit applied it, which could revive Anderson's claims for further proceedings. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Ninth Circuit's decision and send the case back without deciding which side is right, leaving the imprudence claims to be reconsidered under whatever standard the Court announces.
