## In brief

Retirement plan participants ask the Court to overturn a Ninth Circuit rule requiring a "meaningful benchmark" comparison to plead that fund managers imprudently mismanaged retirement savings.

## What happened

Former Intel employees Winston Anderson and Christopher Sulyma sued Intel's retirement plan fiduciaries, claiming they breached their duty under a federal pension law (ERISA) by heavily investing retirement plan assets in hedge funds and private equity starting in 2009, producing lower returns than more conventional funds during a long stock market upswing. The fiduciaries respond that they adopted this strategy deliberately to reduce volatility and large losses after the 2008 crash, disclosed this tradeoff to employees, and that the strategy performed as intended. Lower courts dismissed the lawsuit, holding that the plaintiffs failed to identify a sufficiently similar "benchmark" fund to support their claim that the funds underperformed. The Ninth Circuit affirmed that dismissal.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, when retirement plan participants claim a fund manager acted imprudently because their investments underperformed, the law requires them to point to a comparably similar investment option that did better, or whether a court instead should weigh all the allegations in a complaint together to decide if imprudence is plausible.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review in January 2026. Merits briefing and amicus filings are complete, and the case was argued on October 6, 2026; it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Anderson and the other plan participants are asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's rule. If the Court affirms, the "meaningful benchmark" requirement would stand, and the participants' lawsuit would remain dismissed. If the Court reverses or vacates, the case would return to the lower courts for another look at whether the complaint, taken as a whole, plausibly alleges that Intel's fiduciaries acted imprudently, without being bound by that requirement. A vacated judgment would set the Ninth Circuit's ruling aside without deciding the underlying dispute for either side.
