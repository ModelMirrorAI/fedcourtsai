# Anderson v. Intel Corp. Investment Policy Committee (No. 25-498): forecast after argument

**Judgment class: affirmed.** The Court will affirm the Ninth Circuit's judgment upholding the dismissal of the ERISA imprudence claims. I expect no DIG and no equally divided Court, since all nine Justices sat and questioned.

**Vote lineup: 9–0, or close to it.** At argument no Justice took up the petitioners' position. Justice Jackson pressed the petitioners hardest on whether their own asset-allocation theory needs a comparator anyway. Justice Kagan called the Ninth Circuit's decision "pretty reasonable." Justices Sotomayor and Barrett both pressed the petitioners to say what makes an apple an apple. The likeliest dissenter, if there is one, is Justice Jackson or Justice Sotomayor, on the question of how the decision below treated the non-performance allegations. Even that looks unlikely.

**Ground.** The Court answers the question presented yes. When a prudence claim rests on a fund's underperformance, the complaint must compare the fund to a meaningful benchmark. The Court will ground this in the ordinary Twombly/Iqbal plausibility analysis, not in a heightened ERISA-specific rule. Without a comparator that shares the fund's aims, a difference in strategy is an obvious lawful explanation for a gap in returns. "Meaningful" will be defined by similar objectives and risk/reward profile, and the Court will say plainly that the comparator need not be a twin and need not share the challenged fund's asset allocation. Justice Kagan and the Chief Justice signalled that they want the opinion to give this some content, which the Solicitor General's framing supplies. The Court will hold that the Ninth Circuit applied the standard correctly, because the complaint's comparators (equity-heavy funds, the S&P 500, a 60/40 fund) did not share Intel's downside-protection objective.

**Breadth: narrow.** The Court will say expressly that a benchmark is not required for theories that do not rely on comparative performance. Examples are attacks on the objective itself and allocations facially inconsistent with the stated objective (the "50% lottery tickets" and "45% biotech" hypotheticals). Justice Gorsuch pushed to bracket whether underperformance can ever be enough on its own, so I expect the Court to leave that open. It will also decline to revisit the Ninth Circuit's separate rejection of the strategy allegations, which falls outside the question presented.

**Authorship.** Sotomayor has written the Court's recent ERISA pleading decisions, and Kagan showed the most interest in writing guidance, so I give each of them a modest lift over a flat share. The case was argued on the first day of the October sitting, so no assignment pattern exists yet. Separate writing is unlikely. A short concurrence is possible: Gorsuch or Thomas on the narrowness of the question, or Jackson on how the courts below should treat the non-performance allegations.
