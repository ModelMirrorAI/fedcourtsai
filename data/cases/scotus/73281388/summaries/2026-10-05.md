## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a Maine lobsterman's challenge to a state rule requiring GPS trackers on fishing boats.

## What happened

Maine, following an interstate fisheries commission directive, requires federally permitted lobstermen to install GPS trackers on their boats that transmit location data continuously. Frank Thompson, a lobsterman, sued the state's marine resources commissioner, arguing the requirement amounts to an unconstitutional search of his boat, including when he uses it for non-commercial purposes. In the district court, the state conceded the tracking counted as a "search," while Thompson conceded lobstering is a "closely regulated industry," so the court applied a Supreme Court test for evaluating warrantless searches in such industries and ruled the rule constitutional. The First Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

Thompson asks the Court to decide whether continuous GPS tracking of his boat, even during personal use, violates the rule against unreasonable searches, and whether courts assessing such searches in regulated industries must weigh protections against physical intrusion onto property, not just a business owner's reduced expectation of privacy. He argues other appeals courts would have struck down the tracking rule using a property-intrusion approach instead of the test the First Circuit applied.

## Where it stands

After full briefing including amicus briefs and supplemental filings, the Supreme Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Thompson, as the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the First Circuit's decision in place, meaning Maine's tracking requirement remains in effect and Thompson's challenge to it fails. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling standing without deciding the constitutional question itself.
