## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a Maine lobsterman's challenge to a state rule requiring GPS trackers on fishing boats.

## What happened

Maine requires federally permitted lobstermen to install GPS tracking devices on their boats, reporting location data to help regulators manage the lobster fishery. Frank Thompson and other lobstermen sued, arguing the requirement amounts to an unreasonable search under the Fourth Amendment, which protects against unreasonable searches and seizures. The district court dismissed the claim, applying a Supreme Court framework for evaluating searches of "closely regulated industries," and the First Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that ruling. Thompson alone then asked the Supreme Court to take up the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether tracking devices that must stay on lobster boats at all times, including when the boats are used for non-commercial purposes, amount to an unreasonable search, and whether courts evaluating such searches must consider protections against physical intrusion onto property, not just a business owner's reduced privacy expectations in a regulated industry.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after briefing including amicus filings and supplemental briefs from both sides. The Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Thompson asked the Court to review and reverse the First Circuit's decision, which had sided with Maine's commissioner, Carl Wilson, and upheld the tracking requirement. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the First Circuit's ruling in place: Maine's GPS tracking rule continues to apply to Thompson and other lobstermen, and the lower court's reasoning stands. The denial does not mean the Court agreed the rule is constitutional, only that it chose not to review the case further.
