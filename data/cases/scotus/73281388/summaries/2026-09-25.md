## What happened

Frank Thompson, a Maine lobsterman, sued the state's Department of Marine Resources over a rule requiring lobstermen to install GPS trackers on their fishing boats and keep them running at all times, including when boats are used for personal purposes. Thompson argued this violates the Fourth Amendment, which limits unreasonable government searches. The district court dismissed his claim, and the First Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, applying a legal test used for searches of "closely regulated" industries and finding the tracking rule reasonable under that test. Both sides had agreed below that the rule counts as a search and that commercial lobster fishing is closely regulated; Thompson appealed only the reasonableness question. Maine's Commissioner of Marine Resources, Carl Wilson, is the respondent.

## What the Court is being asked

Thompson wants the Court to decide whether the rules courts normally use for evaluating searches of closely regulated businesses were wrongly applied here, and specifically whether reasonableness should instead be judged using stricter rules that apply when the government physically places equipment on someone's property, rather than rules based only on how much privacy a business owner can reasonably expect. He says other appeals courts require that stricter approach and that the First Circuit's ruling conflicts with theirs. Maine responds that Thompson did not raise this argument earlier in the case, that no real conflict among appeals courts exists, and that the case is a poor candidate for the Court to take up.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for September 28, 2026, and both sides have since filed supplemental briefs, but the docket does not yet show any action taken on the petition.
