## What happened

Maine requires lobstermen with federal permits to install a GPS tracking device on their boats, as part of a regional agreement among Atlantic coastal states to manage the lobster fishery. Frank Thompson, a Maine lobsterman, sued, arguing the requirement violates the Fourth Amendment's protection against unreasonable searches, including when he uses his boat for non-lobstering purposes. In the lower courts, Maine conceded the tracking counts as a "search," and Thompson conceded lobstering is a "closely regulated industry," a category that gets reduced constitutional protection. Applying the Supreme Court's existing three-part test for searches of closely regulated industries, both the district court and the First Circuit Court of Appeals ruled the tracking rule is constitutional.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks how courts should decide whether a government tracking requirement on a regulated business is reasonable under the Fourth Amendment. Thompson argues that courts should weigh whether the government is physically intruding on private property, not just whether a business owner has reduced privacy expectations, and that the lower court wrongly limited property-intrusion-based protections to criminal cases. Maine responds that Thompson raised this property-intrusion argument for the first time at the Supreme Court, that the existing regulated-industry test already accounts for the concerns Thompson raises, and that no conflict exists among the appeals courts on this point.

## Where it stands
