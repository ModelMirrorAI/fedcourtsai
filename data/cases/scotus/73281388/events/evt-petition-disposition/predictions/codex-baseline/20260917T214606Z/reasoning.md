## Competing legal accounts

The petition's QPs and argument, especially printed pages 16–24, frame conflicts over the reach of property-based protection and off-hours administrative searches. That framing gives the case potential importance, but a claimed conflict is not an established holding conflict. The opposition, printed pages 16–21, distinguishes home inspections and municipal tire chalking from searches within an admittedly closely regulated fishery. This is a substantial obstacle to certiorari rather than an answer supplied merely by the caption of a precedent.

The opposition also argues forfeiture and a mismatch between recognizing a search and deciding its reasonableness (printed pages 7–15, 22–24). The reply materially reduces the preservation objection: printed pages 11–12 identify earlier arguments about continuous tracking, common-law limits, and issues addressed below. I therefore treat preservation as contested, not a dispositive waiver. Still, the concession that lobstering is closely regulated limits any broader attack on that category. This is review of an affirmed dismissal, not merely an interlocutory refusal of preliminary relief (opposition, printed pages 5–7).

The First Circuit opinion's footnote 18, PDF page 33, distinguishes criminal investigative tracking and rejects a direct analogy; it does not expressly announce that property-based Fourth Amendment protection disappears in civil cases. My inference is that this makes the petition's categorical characterization less persuasive and leaves room for the Court to view the dispute as application of an existing standard.

The September 9 supplement invokes Richards v. Newsom, an August 27, 2026 Ninth Circuit decision. I rely only on the supplement's account, not an independently retrieved Richards opinion. Its account supports recurrence and disagreement over surveillance of mixed-use property. But it also reports that the Ninth Circuit upheld the challenged regime and distinguished Rush; in my assessment, that is not a clean opposite result on the same legal question. It is not an intervening Supreme Court ruling that would ordinarily furnish a straightforward GVR basis.
