# Predicted Court Reasoning — *William Maxwell v. Sandra Dinis, Acting Warden* (No. 25-5930)

## 1. Judgment Class and Vote Lineup

- **Judgment Class:** `vacated` (the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit is vacated, and the case is remanded for further proceedings consistent with the Court's opinion).
- **Vote Lineup:** **9–0** unanimous judgment in favor of vacating and remanding (`Roberts`, `Thomas`, `Alito`, `Sotomayor`, `Kagan`, `Gorsuch`, `Kavanaugh`, `Barrett`, and `Jackson` all voting in the `majority`).
- **Authorship and Separate Writings (Context):** Justice Barrett delivers the opinion of the Court. Justice Thomas files a brief concurring opinion (potentially joined by Justice Alito or Justice Gorsuch) emphasizing that the Court's decision addresses only the threshold question whether 28 U.S.C. § 2241 is an appropriate procedural vehicle for a claim seeking transfer out of physical imprisonment into community-based prerelease custody, and leaves open on remand both whether 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) strips federal courts of jurisdiction to review Bureau of Prisons prerelease-custody determinations and whether petitioner exhausted his administrative remedies.

---

## 2. Question Presented Reached

The Court reaches the single Question Presented—whether a federal prisoner's claim challenging the refusal to transfer him from prison to a halfway house (residential reentry center) or home confinement under the First Step Act of 2018 is cognizable in a petition for a writ of habeas corpus under 28 U.S.C. § 2241—and answers it in the affirmative. Following its settled practice as a "court of review, not of first view," the Court declines to reach in the first instance either (1) the district court's ruling that petitioner failed to exhaust administrative remedies on his First Step Act claims (and petitioner's responsive argument under *Perttu v. Richards*, 605 U.S. 460 (2025)) or (2) the Government's newly raised argument that 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b)'s review bar ("Notwithstanding any other provision of law, a designation of a place of imprisonment under this subsection is not reviewable by any court") precludes judicial review of BOP prerelease placement decisions.

---

## 3. Doctrinal Ground of the Majority (`majority-ground`)

The Court holds that a federal prisoner's claim seeking accelerated transfer out of a prison facility into prerelease custody in a halfway house or home confinement under the First Step Act is cognizable in habeas corpus under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 because it seeks release from physical imprisonment into the community—analogous to parole under *Preiser v. Rodriguez*, 411 U.S. 475 (1973), *Wilkinson v. Dotson*, 544 U.S. 74 (2005), and *Jones v. Hendrix*, 599 U.S. 465, 475 (2023)—notwithstanding the Bureau of Prisons' continuing legal custody under 18 U.S.C. § 3621(a), while leaving alternative defenses including administrative exhaustion and 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b)'s judicial-review bar for the Fifth Circuit on remand.

Specifically, the majority's reasoning turns on three doctrinal pillars:

1. **Release from Physical Imprisonment into the Community Sounds in Core Habeas:**
   Under 28 U.S.C. § 2241(c)(3), the writ of habeas corpus extends to a prisoner "in custody in violation of the Constitution or laws or treaties of the United States." In *Preiser*, 411 U.S. at 484–89, and *Dotson*, 544 U.S. at 82, the Court established that habeas is the proper remedy when a prisoner seeks "release from physical imprisonment" or a "shorter stay in prison" via "release into the community," including release on parole. In *Jones v. Hendrix*, 599 U.S. at 475, the Court similarly identified as paradigmatic § 2241 claims a prisoner's contention "that he is being detained in a place or manner not authorized by the sentence, [or] that he has unlawfully been denied parole or good-time credits." Transfer from a secure prison facility to a halfway house or home confinement under 18 U.S.C. §§ 3624(c), 3624(g), and 3632(d)(4)(C) removes the prisoner from incarceration behind prison walls and places him in the community under conditions Congress itself distinguishes from imprisonment "in a prison" (e.g., 18 U.S.C. § 3624(g)(5), which authorizes BOP to revoke prerelease custody and require the offender to serve the remainder of his term "in prison").

2. **Continuing Legal Custody Does Not Defeat Habeas Cognizability:**
   The Fifth Circuit's bright-line rule in *Melot v. Bergami*, 970 F.3d 596, 599 (5th Cir. 2020), and the decision below—limiting § 2241 to claims that would "automatically entitle [the prisoner] to accelerated release" from all legal custody—conflates termination of legal custody with release from physical imprisonment. Both historically (as with release on bail, where sureties remained the prisoner's legal custodians, see 2 Matthew Hale, *The History of the Pleas of the Crown* 124 (1736)) and under modern federal and state statutes (where parolees remain in the legal custody of the warden or parole board, see *Jones v. Cunningham*, 371 U.S. 236, 238–41 (1963); *Anderson v. Corall*, 263 U.S. 193, 196 (1923)), habeas corpus has always been available to secure release from a prison facility into community supervision even though legal custody continues for the duration of the sentence.

3. **Inapplicability of *Bivens* to Federal Prisoners' Prerelease Transfer Claims:**
   The Fifth Circuit's conclusion that a federal prisoner seeking transfer to a halfway house or home confinement must file a civil rights suit under *Bivens v. Six Unknown Fed. Narcotics Agents*, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), improperly transplants 42 U.S.C. § 1983 state-prisoner cases to federal prisoners. Unlike § 1983, *Bivens* is a strictly limited implied damages remedy for three narrow constitutional contexts (*Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482, 491 (2022)) and provides no cause of action for a federal prisoner seeking prospective enforcement of a federal statute such as the First Step Act.

---

## 4. Breadth of the Majority's Ground (`ground-breadth`)

The Court confines its holding to claims seeking accelerated transfer out of a prison facility into community-based prerelease custody (a halfway house or home confinement) and declines to decide whether every claim seeking a reduction in the level of custody within prison walls is cognizable under 28 U.S.C. § 2241, while also reserving for remand whether 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) precludes judicial review of federal prisoners' prerelease-custody claims and whether petitioner exhausted administrative remedies.

Rather than adopting petitioner's broader invitation to hold that *any* claim seeking a "quantum change in the level of custody" (including intra-prison transfers such as release from solitary confinement to general population, or transfers between security classifications) falls within § 2241, the Court follows the Solicitor General's narrower path: holding that because prerelease custody in a halfway house or home confinement effects a transfer *out of prison* into the community analogous to parole, it is cognizable under § 2241, while leaving intra-prison level-of-custody questions and the Government's threshold defenses (18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) and administrative exhaustion) unaddressed.

---

## 5. Procedural Exit Assessment (DIG / Mootness)

A procedural exit via `dismissed-as-improvidently-granted` (or mootness vacatur/dismissal) is a live minority risk (~14%) but not the modal outcome. The Solicitor General explicitly notes two potential triggers for a DIG: (1) BOP projects that under its own calculation of petitioner's First Step Act time credits, petitioner will likely become eligible for transfer to a halfway house in January 2027, and (2) the Eighth Circuit recently held in *Osorio-Calderon v. Warden*, 179 F.4th 1158 (8th Cir. 2026), that 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b) strips district courts of jurisdiction over federal prisoners' prerelease custody claims. However, because petitioner also requested transfer to *home confinement* (a less restrictive placement than a halfway house) and disputes BOP's calculation of additional First Step Act time credits (which can also apply toward supervised release under 18 U.S.C. § 3632(d)(4)(C)), a January 2027 halfway-house transfer would not moot the entire controversy, and the Court is unlikely to leave the Fifth Circuit's *Melot* rule standing after appointing an amicus and hearing argument.
