# Merits Judgment Prediction: *William Maxwell v. Albert Thomas, III, Warden* (No. 25-5930)

## 1. Case Overview and Procedural Posture

Petitioner William Maxwell, an indigent federal prisoner incarcerated at FCI Beaumont Low, sought transfer to home confinement or a residential reentry center (halfway house) in 2020 under the First Step Act of 2018, the Second Chance Act of 2007 (18 U.S.C. § 3624(c)), 18 U.S.C. § 3621(b), and the CARES Act of 2020. Proceeding *pro se*, Maxwell filed suit in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Texas using a 28 U.S.C. § 2241 habeas corpus form alongside a handwritten "Affidavit and Brief in Support of Habeas Corpus and of Tort Claims." In his filings, Maxwell alleged:
1. That the Bureau of Prisons (BOP) unlawfully denied him individualized consideration for community confinement or home detention by relying on an unverified, erroneous prior state charge ("aggravated assault with a deadly weapon") in his Presentence Investigation Report (PSR) and inmate file, despite his timely objections and lack of any such conviction.
2. That prison officials retaliated against him for exercising his First Amendment right to file administrative grievances by placing him in segregation ("the hole"), threatening him, destroying or losing his property, and interfering with his legal mail and the administrative remedy process.
3. That he was entitled to relief under the Federal Tort Claims Act (FTCA), 28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b), 2671 *et seq.*, as well as equitable and declaratory relief directing BOP to correct his records and fairly consider him for transfer.

The magistrate judge recommended dismissing the action on the ground that challenges to the denial of placement in home confinement or a halfway house do not sound in habeas corpus under 28 U.S.C. § 2241 because success would not automatically entitle the prisoner to accelerated release from custody, and that the district court lacked authority to convert the habeas petition into a *Bivens* action or order a prison transfer. Maxwell filed timely objections pointing out that he had also raised FTCA claims, First Amendment retaliation claims, and administrative exhaustion obstruction issues, and requesting leave to amend or severance if necessary. The district court adopted the magistrate judge's report without addressing the FTCA claims and dismissed the suit with prejudice.

On April 3, 2025, the Fifth Circuit affirmed in an unpublished per curiam opinion (*Maxwell v. Thomas*, 2025 WL 1291952), holding that under circuit precedent (*Melot v. Bergami*, 970 F.3d 596 (5th Cir. 2020)), a challenge to denial of home confinement attacks a condition of confinement rather than the fact or duration of confinement and therefore must be brought as a *Bivens* civil rights claim rather than a § 2241 habeas petition. Unlike in *Melot*—where the Fifth Circuit had liberally construed the *pro se* prisoner's § 2241 petition as a *Bivens* action—neither the district court nor the Fifth Circuit recharacterized Maxwell's petition or addressed his explicit FTCA claims.

After Maxwell filed a *pro se* petition for a writ of certiorari and the Government filed a Brief in Opposition, the Supreme Court Counsel (Paul, Weiss, Rifkind, Wharton & Garrison LLP) entered an appearance and filed a Certiorari Reply Brief framing two principal issues: (1) the entrenched circuit split over whether § 2241 encompasses challenges to the execution/manner of a federal sentence (such as eligibility for community/home confinement) and whether courts must liberally construe *pro se* filings rather than trapping prisoners in a "Bermuda triangle" between § 2241 and *Bivens*; and (2) the lower courts' complete failure to address Maxwell's expressly pleaded FTCA claims. The Supreme Court granted certiorari on June 1, 2026.

## 3. Analysis of the Merits Issues

### A. The "Execution of Sentence" / Habeas-vs.-Civil-Rights Catch-22
The first core issue concerns how federal law channels a federal prisoner's challenge to BOP's refusal to consider him for community confinement or home detention under 18 U.S.C. §§ 3621(b) and 3624(c), and how lower courts should handle *pro se* pleadings that sit at the boundary of habeas corpus and civil actions.

1. **The Statutory and Doctrinal Tension**:
   - Under 28 U.S.C. § 2241(c)(3), habeas corpus extends to a prisoner "in custody in violation of the Constitution or laws or treaties of the United States."
   - Five circuits (the First, Second, Third, Sixth, and Tenth Circuits—e.g., *Woodall v. Federal Bureau of Prisons*, 432 F.3d 235 (3d Cir. 2005); *Jiminian v. Nash*, 245 F.3d 144 (2d Cir. 2001)) allow federal prisoners to use § 2241 to challenge the "execution" or "manner" of their sentence, including categorical or unlawful denials of halfway-house or home-confinement placement under §§ 3621(b) and 3624(c), reasoning that community/home confinement is qualitatively different from institutional imprisonment.
   - Conversely, the Fifth, Seventh, and Ninth Circuits (*Melot v. Bergami*, 970 F.3d 596 (5th Cir. 2020); *Richmond v. Scibana*, 387 F.3d 602 (7th Cir. 2004); *Pinson v. Carvajal*, 69 F.4th 1059 (9th Cir. 2023)) limit habeas strictly to claims that necessarily require accelerated release from all custody (`Preiser v. Rodriguez`, 411 U.S. 475 (1973); `Wilkinson v. Dotson`, 544 U.S. 74 (2005)).
   - However, the Fifth Circuit's approach in *Melot* and *Maxwell* creates a acute doctrinal contradiction: it tells the prisoner that his claim "is more properly brought as a *Bivens* action," yet under *Egbert v. Boule*, 596 U.S. 482 (2022), and *Ziglar v. Abbasi*, 582 U.S. 120 (2017), *Bivens* is a damages remedy against individual officers that is virtually never available in new contexts and does not provide the forward-looking injunctive/declaratory or APA-style review of BOP's record-keeping or statutory compliance that the prisoner seeks. And worse still, whereas *Melot* liberally construed the *pro se* prisoner's § 2241 petition as a civil action, the courts below in *Maxwell* simply dismissed the entire suit *with prejudice* without construing it as a civil complaint or granting leave to amend.

2. **How the Supreme Court Is Likely to Resolve This**:
   - Even Justices who are skeptical of expanding the historic "core of habeas corpus" beyond fact or duration of custody (such as Justices Thomas, Alito, Kavanaugh, and Barrett) are likely to recognize that dismissing a *pro se* prisoner's suit *with prejudice* because he used a § 2241 form—while telling him he should have brought a *Bivens* action—is untenable.
   - Under *Erickson v. Pardus*, 551 U.S. 89, 94 (2007), *Haines v. Kerner*, 404 U.S. 519, 520 (1972), *Castro v. United States*, 540 U.S. 375 (2003), and *Johnson v. City of Shelby*, 574 U.S. 10 (2014), federal courts are required to construe *pro se* pleadings liberally according to their substance rather than their label.

### B. The Ignored FTCA Claims
The second issue provides an even cleaner, near-unanimous ground for vacatur or reversal:
- Maxwell explicitly titled his supporting brief "Affidavit and Brief in Support of Habeas Corpus and of Tort Claims" and repeatedly invoked the Federal Tort Claims Act (28 U.S.C. §§ 1346(b), 2671 *et seq.*), alleging property loss/destruction, threats, and wrongful acts by BOP officers, and attaching his administrative tort claim correspondence.
- The Government's Brief in Opposition (at 10) candidly conceded that Maxwell's filings referenced tort claims and that "the lower courts did not explicitly pass upon those claims," defending the omission only by asserting that the references were "passing" or lacked sufficient factual detail.
- Under Rule 8(a) and *Erickson v. Pardus*, a *pro se* litigant's explicit invocation of a federal statutory cause of action (the FTCA) supported by factual allegations of officer misconduct and administrative presentment cannot be silently ignored and swept into a dismissal with prejudice solely because it was filed alongside a § 2241 form.
- Recent Supreme Court FTCA precedents—such as *Millbrook v. United States*, 569 U.S. 50 (2013) (9-0, per Thomas, J., reversing the dismissal of a *pro se* federal prisoner's FTCA claim), and *Martin v. United States*, 605 U.S. 395 (2025) (9-0, per Gorsuch, J., vacating and remanding an Eleventh Circuit FTCA dismissal)—demonstrate strong cross-ideological willingness to vacate lower court judgments that improperly short-circuit FTCA claims.
