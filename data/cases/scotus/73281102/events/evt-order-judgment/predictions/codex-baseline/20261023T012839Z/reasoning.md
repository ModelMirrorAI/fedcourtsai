## Evidence actually used

I read the provisioned June 2, 2026 snapshot, context, document manifest, questions presented, and relevant portions of the petition, appendix, brief in opposition, and cert reply. The appendix supplies the Fifth Circuit's opinion and the district-court exhaustion posture. The manifest marks the provisioned filings as neither empty-text nor truncated. There are no merits briefs in the directory; this forecast does not rely on merits briefing, oral argument, later docket developments, or another prediction. Although context identifies this as a forward cell, I did not retrieve this case's current docket or disposition.

The original pro se petition advances both cognizability and exhaustion arguments, but the grant order narrows the issue to time credits and prerelease custody. The appendix shows that the Fifth Circuit affirmed on its categorical accelerated-release rule without resolving exhaustion. The district court, by contrast, had rejected the relevant First Step Act claim for failure to exhaust. Those are different grounds, not a finding that Maxwell has already won his credit claim.

The government's opposition, especially pages 6–10, is unusually favorable to the legal premise of reversal: it recognizes the difficulty reconciling an absolute accelerated-release requirement with Jones, while opposing review on exhaustion and intracircuit-vehicle grounds. This is not a concession of Maxwell's ultimate entitlement. The counseled reply, especially pages 6–9, separates the threshold habeas question from the exhaustion issue that the Fifth Circuit did not decide. I give that separation substantial weight now that the limited grant has occurred. I do not adopt the original petition's broader characterization of agreement across nine circuits; the reply describes a narrower, developed disagreement.

For general precedent only, CourtListener MCP identified Jones v. Hendrix, 599 U.S. 465 (June 22, 2023), and a within-opinion search verified its page-475 discussion of detention challenges distinct from attacks on sentence validity. That passage supports a route for sentence-execution and custody-level claims but did not itself decide this First Step Act question. I treat the extension to statutory prerelease credits as a prediction, not an existing holding. General web lookups returned no usable material, and an additional citation lookup for Preiser returned an unrelated result that I discarded.

Coverage is **516 parsed / 539 granted = 95.73%**; 56 excluded rows sit outside those 539 grants. Term 2025 is excluded entirely. These are counts in the committed pack read from this checkout, not a fresh live-corpus query; no corpus-wide current freshness claim is made. Coverage and the possible censoring of recent grants limit the anchor.

I move upward from that roughly 70% anchor because the challenged rule is categorical, the respondent's own cert-stage brief acknowledges its doctrinal problem, and the limited grant supplies a clean way to correct that rule while preserving exhaustion for remand. I stop at 86%, rather than near certainty, because the judgment can still be affirmed on the district court's alternative exhaustion ground, the Court may distinguish Jones's discussion, or the vehicle may ultimately produce a DIG.

My full judgment allocation is: reversed 0.50, vacated 0.34, affirmed-in-part-reversed-in-part 0.02, affirmed 0.11, DIG 0.03, equally divided affirmance 0.00. The three disturbed categories sum to 0.86. Reversal is thus only the modal exact label, not an 86% exact-label claim; vacatur is a close alternative. No recusal is established by the dated proceedings used here.

The unanimous lineup is less secure than the disturbed binary. It rests on the possibility of a narrow sentence-execution holding acceptable both to Justices favoring ordinary habeas access and to those protective of restrictions on collateral attacks on convictions. Thomas's authorship of Jones's majority, identified in the precedent retrieval, makes the relevant distinction less plausibly a simple ideological split. I assign no individual writing roles. A narrower coalition or concurrence in the judgment would not surprise me.

The stakes score of 0.56 reflects practical consequences for federal prisoners' ability to obtain statutory prerelease-custody review and for the habeas/civil-rights boundary, while recognizing that this is not a decision about the validity of sentences or a general constitutional right to home confinement.
