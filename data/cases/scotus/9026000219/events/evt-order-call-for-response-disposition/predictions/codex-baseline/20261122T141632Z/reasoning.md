The best reason to take the case is that the same military lawyer advised investigators seeking access to Johnson's phones and the inexperienced commander who approved the search. Chief Judge Ohlson explained why even the agent's oral account failed to justify searching seven and a half months of messages and media. The search uncovered evidence used for a separate conviction. Johnson also identifies the same advisory arrangement in *Perkins* and *Lattin*. The practice can recur across the services, where the CAAF has the final appellate word below the Supreme Court. (Petition pp. 5–8, 11–13 and appendix pp. 22a–27a.)

The question Johnson asks is broader than the one the military court decided. He conceded the commander's competence under the military rule, and the commander had received some instruction on probable cause. The four-judge majority assumed probable cause was lacking and upheld the finding that the agents reasonably relied on the authorization, including information presented orally. The dissent disputed the justification for this particular search. The cited civilian cases do not address the same dual-adviser arrangement. *Shadwick* already makes independence and capacity to assess probable cause the tests for an issuing officer. The stronger open question is what the prosecutor's involvement did to that independence. Even a favorable answer on good faith would leave the trial judge's separate finding that exclusion would yield too little deterrence to justify its costs. Those difficulties keep my estimate close to the band rate despite the call and the dissent. (Questions presented, petition pp. 8–12 and appendix pp. 5a, 10a–11a, 16a–27a. [Shadwick v. City of Tampa, 407 U.S. 345, 350–52 (1972)](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/108582/shadwick-v-city-of-tampa/))
