## In brief

Daytona Beach asks the Court to review a ruling that its panhandling ordinance violates the First Amendment.

## What happened

Daytona Beach passed an ordinance restricting panhandling, including bans on "aggressive" panhandling, panhandling in certain locations, and panhandling after dark. Four men who panhandle in the city sued, claiming the rules violated their free-speech rights. The district court agreed, declaring most challenged provisions unconstitutional, permanently blocking their enforcement, and awarding damages under a stipulation. On appeal, the Eleventh Circuit held that some, but not all, provisions were challenged by plaintiffs with standing to do so, found that those provisions were content-based and failed the demanding "strict scrutiny" test, but narrowed the lower court's broad, citywide injunction to apply only to the individual plaintiffs. The City then sought rehearing, which was denied, and now petitions the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a rule regulating in-person requests for money based on whether the request seeks an immediate donation counts as neutral toward the content of speech under the Court's existing speech-regulation precedents, how closely a definition of "solicitation" must match the one the Court has already used for such a rule to count as neutral, and whether protecting traffic safety can count as a government interest strong enough to justify restricting speech under the most demanding level of judicial review.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed on November 16, 2026, and a response is due December 21, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Daytona Beach, which lost most of the case in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full briefing and argument and decide the First Amendment questions itself, without yet deciding which side wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Eleventh Circuit's decision stands: the ordinance's challenged provisions remain unenforceable against the plaintiffs who had standing, and the damages award to the plaintiffs stands, without the Supreme Court weighing in on the underlying legal questions.
