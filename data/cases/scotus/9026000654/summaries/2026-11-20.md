## In brief

Daytona Beach asks the Court to review a ruling that its panhandling ordinance violates the First Amendment.

## What happened

Daytona Beach passed an ordinance restricting panhandling citywide, including a ban on seeking immediate, in-person donations in certain locations and at certain times. Four men who panhandle for their living, including Dennis Scott and Chad Driggers, sued, saying the ordinance violated their free-speech rights. A federal district court agreed, struck down several provisions, and awarded damages. The Eleventh Circuit largely agreed that the challenged provisions regulate speech based on content and fail the demanding review that applies to such laws, though it found some plaintiffs lacked standing to challenge some provisions and sent part of the case back, and it also found the injunction too broad. The city then asked the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The city asks the Court to decide whether a panhandling rule that distinguishes based on when a donation is requested - immediately versus at some future time - counts as neutral toward the content of speech, or whether it instead triggers the strictest level of judicial scrutiny. It also asks how closely a city's definition of "solicitation" must match the one the Court has previously used, and whether preventing traffic accidents can count as a government interest strong enough to justify restricting speech under that strictest standard.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and docketed on November 19, 2026, with the respondents' response due December 21, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on whether to take the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Daytona Beach, which lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse that ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the First Amendment questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If the Court denies review, the Eleventh Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place its conclusion that the challenged parts of the ordinance are unconstitutional and its instructions for further proceedings on standing and the scope of relief.
