The city has a question other municipalities face. Its ordinance treats a request for an immediate donation the same whether the speaker seeks money for a meal, a church or a campaign. The city argues that the physical exchange creates the hazard, so timing can be a neutral criterion under *City of Austin*. The Eleventh Circuit acknowledged that a city may regulate solicitation evenhandedly. Its objection was that a person who can ask for a donation next week in a given place can violate the ordinance by asking for it today. The timing of the requested help conveys part of the message. A ruling on that distinction could matter well beyond Daytona Beach. The published opinion matches the petition's appendix in caption, consolidated appeals and judgment. [Petition, pp. 12–16](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/26/26-654/432881/20261116161722524_26-_Petition.pdf) · [Opinion below, pp. 32–41](../../../../../record/documents/decision-below.txt)

The weakness is the claimed conflict. The city itself describes this as apparently the first federal appeals decision after *Austin* addressing a face-to-face panhandling ordinance. The Fifth Circuit upheld a law governing placement of donation boxes regardless of the message on the box. It had no occasion to distinguish requests for payment now from requests for payment later. The Seventh Circuit did face that distinction in *Norton*. On rehearing after *Reed*, it held that singling out oral requests for an immediate donation was content based. Although *Norton* predates *Austin*, the closest factual comparison supports the judgment below. This pulls the forecast below the 5.17% anchor. I leave some room for the Court to clarify its own discussion of solicitation before a direct conflict develops.
