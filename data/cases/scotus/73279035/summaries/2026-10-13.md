## In brief

The Court declined to hear former Ruby Tuesday managers' bid to recover lost retirement benefits from Regions Bank under a federal benefits law.

## What happened

Jerry Aldridge and other former Ruby Tuesday managers participated in company retirement plans for highly compensated employees, funded through a trust that Regions Bank administered. After Ruby Tuesday was sold and later went bankrupt, the managers say Regions failed to protect their benefits as required, costing them millions. They sued Regions under a federal retirement-benefits law, seeking money to replace lost benefits, and also brought state-law claims. The district court ruled against them on all claims, and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that the type of relief they sought was not available under the federal law and that the law barred their state-law claims.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions about a federal law governing employee retirement plans. First, whether a plan beneficiary may recover a particular form of compensation, called surcharge, from the bank managing their plan under a provision allowing "equitable" relief, or whether that compensation instead counts as an ordinary damages award, which the law does not permit under that provision. Second, if that compensation is unavailable, whether the federal law still blocks beneficiaries from pursuing related claims under state contract law, potentially leaving them without any remedy at all.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court invited the Solicitor General to weigh in on the government's views, which it did. The Court then denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The managers, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up their case and reverse the Sixth Circuit's ruling in Regions Bank's favor. The denial leaves that ruling in place: the managers cannot pursue their claim for compensation under the federal benefits law, nor their state-law claims, and they recover nothing further in this case. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Sixth Circuit's reasoning, including on the recovery question, which the government's brief said the lower court got wrong; it simply leaves that decision standing.
