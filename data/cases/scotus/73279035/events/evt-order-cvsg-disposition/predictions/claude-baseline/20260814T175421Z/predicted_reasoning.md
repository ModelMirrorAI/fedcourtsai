# Predicted reasoning — Aldridge v. Regions Bank (No. 25-590), CVSG-moment cert forecast

**State forecast from.** The docket shows **two distributions** (for the
1/9/2026 and 4/2/2026 conferences) and a **CVSG issued April 6, 2026**, four
days after the second conference. The respondent initially waived, the Court
requested a response, and the case reached the SG invitation with a full set of
filings: petition, BIO, reply, and one academic amicus (Samuel L. Bray)
supporting review.

**Relists.** The petition will be distributed **at least once more** with near
certainty (~0.97): a CVSG case is redistributed for conference once the
Solicitor General files, and this docket shows no sign of settlement or
withdrawal. Expect **one to three further distributions** — one when the SG
brief arrives, plausibly one or two more while the Court digests the SG's
recommendation, on the modern pattern of considering a CVSG'd petition over a
couple of conferences before acting.

**Timing.** No deadline binds the SG. For an April invitation the typical
filing windows are **December 2026** (to make the January conferences) or
**April–May 2027** (to make the end-of-Term cleanup). I expect the brief in
December 2026 or early 2027, redistribution within two to three weeks of
filing, and a disposition order between **January and June 2027** (OT2026).
Disposition before the SG files is very unlikely.

**Further CVSG.** None — the invitation already on the docket is the only one
this petition will see (the increment claim is vacuous here and masked by the
harness; the 0.02 is nominal).

**Disposition.** More likely denied than granted, but this is close to a
coin flip: P(any grant) 0.45. If the SG recommends grant — the Department of
Labor has historically defended surcharge's availability under § 502(a)(3),
which cuts toward a merits-sympathetic SG brief — a grant follows with high
probability. If the SG says the top-hat posture makes this a poor vehicle for
the surcharge split (respondent's central point: every circuit permitting
surcharge did so against an ERISA *fiduciary*, and top-hat plans are exempt
from ERISA's fiduciary duties), the Court most likely denies and keeps waiting
for a cleaner vehicle, as it did after denying Rose v. PSA Airlines (23-734)
in April 2024 on interlocutory-vehicle grounds.

**Which question, if granted.** The grant would center on **QP1** — whether a
§ 1132(a)(3) beneficiary may seek surcharge, the acknowledged 6–2 split the
Sixth Circuit deepened below. QP2 (whether state-law claims on a separate,
non-plan-required trust contract are preempted when surcharge is unavailable)
is written as conditional on QP1 and supplies the no-remedy-at-all stakes; I
expect the Court to take **both questions as written** if it grants, though a
grant limited to QP1 is the main alternative. A reformulated QP folding the
two into a single remedies-gap question is possible but less likely than
taking the petition's framing.

**Summary disposition.** Unlikely (~2–3% of the grant mass): no intervening
decision of this Court bears on the split, so a GVR has nothing to ride on,
and the question is too contested across circuits for a summary reversal.

**If denied.** A **statement or dissent respecting denial is plausible but
less likely than not** (~30%). The likeliest writers are Justices with
expressed interest in the law-equity boundary in ERISA cases — Gorsuch or
Thomas — flagging that the Amara/Mertens tension needs resolution in a case
where the fiduciary-duty posture is clean.
