## In brief

An Air Force officer asks the Court to review whether military appeals courts may examine a post-conviction notice barring him from owning firearms.

## What happened

Parker Myslow, an Air Force officer, pleaded guilty at a court-martial to drug-related offenses and was sentenced to dismissal from service. His judgment was accompanied by a document called a "First Indorsement," which stated that his convictions triggered a federal law barring him from possessing firearms. Myslow argued to the Air Force Court of Criminal Appeals that this firearms restriction was unconstitutional, but that court, following a precedent from the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces called Johnson, ruled it had no authority to review the indorsement. The Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces denied further review, and Myslow petitioned the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether military appeals courts have the power, under a law letting them fix errors that occur in a court-martial's processing after judgment is entered, to review and correct a firearms restriction noted in a document issued alongside the judgment. The government responds that the indorsement is not a separate, later step but part of the judgment itself, so no error-correction authority applies.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed and was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026. The docket shows no action taken on it since that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Myslow, the officer who lost below, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether military appeals courts can review this kind of firearms restriction, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the ruling against Myslow stands, and the military appeals courts' position—that they lack authority to review the indorsement—remains in effect for his case and others raising the same issue.
