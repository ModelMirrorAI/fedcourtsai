## III. Case Background & Decision Below

Petitioner The Nielsen Company (US), LLC collects and sells radio audience listenership data in over 270 local markets and compiles that data into a nationwide report ("Nationwide"). It is undisputed for purposes of the preliminary injunction that Nielsen holds a 100% monopoly in the national radio ratings data market, while facing a single low-cost rival, Eastlan Ratings, in certain local radio ratings markets (App. 6a–7a).

Historically, Nielsen sold Nationwide and local market data separately. In September 2024, Nielsen adopted its "Network Policy," under which any broadcaster operating both a national network and local stations that declined to subscribe to Nielsen's local data in a given market would have that local market's data excised from its Nationwide report—rendering the Nationwide report unusable "Swiss cheese" (App. 7a–8a).

When Respondent Cumulus (which operates 395 local stations and the Westwood One national syndication network) sought to renew its expiring contract in May 2025 while dropping Nielsen's local data in several markets in favor of Eastlan, Nielsen repeatedly refused to quote a standalone price for Nationwide, offering either a full bundle of all 80 local markets plus Nationwide or the unusable "Swiss cheese" Nationwide report (App. 9a). In August 2025, Cumulus sent a cease-and-desist letter alleging unlawful tying under Section 2 of the Sherman Act. One month later, on September 15, 2025, Nielsen purported to "exempt" Cumulus from the Network Policy and quoted a standalone price for Nationwide that was ten times what Cumulus paid under its existing contract and at least 150% higher than any other network paid for standalone Nationwide (App. 10a, 32a–33a). At that standalone price, purchasing Nationwide from Nielsen and local data from Eastlan would have cost Cumulus $1.2 million more than buying Nielsen's entire tied bundle (App. 32a).

Judge Jeannette A. Vargas (S.D.N.Y.) held a three-day evidentiary hearing and granted a preliminary injunction enjoining Nielsen from (i) enforcing its Network Policy, and (ii) charging a "commercially unreasonable rate for its Nationwide Report as a complete, standalone product," with a safe harbor providing that a rate equal to or lower than the highest annual 2026 rate Nielsen charges any broadcaster for Nationwide is presumptively reasonable (App. 14a, 50a–51a).

On July 13, 2026, a unanimous Second Circuit panel (Judge Nathan, joined by Judge Pérez and Judge Gary S. Katzmann sitting by designation) affirmed:
1. **Bankruptcy Automatic Stay (11 U.S.C. § 362(a)(1)):** Although Cumulus filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in March 2026 and Nielsen had asserted counterclaims in the district court, the Second Circuit joined the majority of circuits in holding that actions are disaggregated by claim and § 362(a)(1) does not stay an appeal arising solely from the debtor's own affirmative claims (App. 16a–19a). (Nielsen does not challenge this bankruptcy holding in its cert petition.)
2. **Constructive Tying & Coercion:** Relying on *United States v. Loew's, Inc.*, 371 U.S. 38 (1962), and *American Manufacturers Mutual Insurance Co. v. American Broadcasting-Paramount Theatres, Inc.* (*American Manufacturers I*), 388 F.2d 272 (2d Cir. 1967), the panel held that a seller cannot evade an express tying prohibition by substituting a non-cost-justified, exorbitant standalone price that leaves the buyer with no economically rational choice but to take the tied bundle (App. 25a–36a). Crucially, the panel emphasized that Nielsen did not challenge the district court's ruling that the express Network Policy was an unlawful tie, and affirmed the district court's factual finding that Nielsen's September 2025 standalone quote was a continuation of the Network Policy rather than a genuine standalone price or mere opening offer (App. 23a, 29a–36a).
3. **Anticompetitive Effects & Pretext:** The panel declined to reach whether monopolist tying is *per se* unlawful under Section 2 (App. 36a–37a n.7) because it affirmed the district court's factual findings of actual anticompetitive foreclosure of Eastlan in local markets and pretextual justifications (supported by internal emails from Nielsen's Managing Director stating the policy was meant to "command subscriptions in local markets") (App. 37a–44a).
4. **Irreparable Harm & Rule 65(d) Specificity:** While rejecting two of the district court's theories of irreparable harm, the panel affirmed based on imminent loss of customers, goodwill, and market share, and held that the injunction's benchmark safe harbor satisfied Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 65(d) (App. 44a–54a).

Rehearing and rehearing en banc were denied without noted dissent on August 18, 2026 (App. 105a).

## IV. Evaluation of Certiorari Criteria (Rule 10)

### 1. The Alleged Circuit Split and Doctrinal Tension
Nielsen and its amici argue that the Second Circuit's decision conflicts with *Linkline*, 555 U.S. 438, and *Brooke Group*, 509 U.S. 209, and creates or deepens a circuit split with the Ninth Circuit (*Cascade Health Solutions v. PeaceHealth*, 515 F.3d 883 (9th Cir. 2008)), the Eighth Circuit (*Southeast Missouri Hospital v. C.R. Bard, Inc.*, 642 F.3d 608 (8th Cir. 2011)), and the Tenth Circuit (*Suture Express, Inc. v. Owens & Minor Distribution, Inc.*, 851 F.3d 1029 (10th Cir. 2017)) by condemning a bundled discount without requiring a showing of below-cost pricing under the discount-attribution test. Nielsen also equates the Second Circuit's ruling with the Third Circuit's much-criticized en banc decision in *LePage's Inc. v. 3M*, 324 F.3d 141 (3d Cir. 2003), and argues that the preliminary injunction forces the district court into price regulation contrary to *Verizon Communications Inc. v. Law Offices of Curtis V. Trinko, LLP*, 540 U.S. 398 (2004).

This doctrinal framing has real surface appeal—which explains why four business/antitrust amici filed briefs and why a chambers called for a response. However, upon closer examination (and once Cumulus files its brief in opposition), the asserted split is substantially overstated:
- **Express Tying Evasion vs. Pure Bundled Discounting:** Unlike *PeaceHealth*, *Linkline*, or *Brooke Group*, this case did not begin as a challenge to a discount offered on a bundle of separately available products. For over a year, Nielsen enforced a formal, express contractual tie (the Network Policy) under which it flatly refused to sell a complete Nationwide report unless the broadcaster bought Nielsen's local data in every market where it owned stations. Nielsen **did not even appeal** the district court's holding that its express Network Policy was an unlawful tie (App. 23a, 31a).
- **Remedial / Evasion Context under *Loew's*:** Nielsen only quoted a "standalone" price for Nationwide after Cumulus sent a cease-and-desist letter threatening suit, and set that price at 10x Cumulus's existing contract rate and 150% above the highest price paid by any other network, while offering no evidence quantifying or correlating its costs (App. 32a–33a, 41a). The Second Circuit relied directly on *United States v. Loew's, Inc.*, 371 U.S. 38, 52–54 (1962), where the Supreme Court specifically approved enjoining non-cost-justified price differentials between standalone and packaged products after a defendant rescinds an express block-booking policy on the eve of litigation.
- **No Holding That Above-Cost Bundled Discounts Generally Violate § 2:** The Second Circuit explicitly acknowledged *Linkline*'s rule that "antitrust law does not prohibit lawfully obtained monopolies from charging monopoly prices" (App. 34a), and did not reject *PeaceHealth*'s discount-attribution test for ordinary bundled discounts. Even in *PeaceHealth* and the Areeda & Hovenkamp treatise cited in the petition (Pet. 22), courts and commentators recognize that a sham standalone price that exceeds the bundle price or serves as a subterfuge for an express tie is treated as a de facto tie rather than a standard bundled discount.

### 2. Severe Vehicle and Posture Flaws
Even if several Justices are sympathetic to tightening Section 2 standards around "constructive tying" and price-linked injunctions, this case is an exceptionally poor vehicle for three independent reasons:
1. **Interlocutory Preliminary Injunction:** The case comes to the Court from an interlocutory appeal of a preliminary injunction based on an expedited three-day hearing. Both the district court and the Second Circuit emphasized that the factual record is preliminary—for instance, noting that Nielsen "made no effort to quantify these costs or correlate them with its price for standalone Nationwide" at the preliminary injunction stage, though "Nielsen may later 'quantify [its] costs or correlate them with its price for standalone Nationwide'" at summary judgment or trial (App. 33a, 41a). The Supreme Court strongly disfavors granting certiorari to review fact-bound antitrust questions in an interlocutory preliminary-injunction posture before full discovery and a final judgment on the merits.
2. **Unchallenged Express Tie Taints the Standalone Offer:** Because Nielsen forfeited any appellate challenge to the district court's holding that the Network Policy itself is an unlawful express tie (App. 23a), the Court cannot cleanly reach the abstract question whether a monopolist with *no* express tying policy may charge whatever standalone and bundled prices it wishes so long as the bundle is above cost. In this case, the district court made a factual finding—reviewed for clear error—that the September 2025 standalone offer was a pretextual continuation of the unlawful express Network Policy triggered by Cumulus's threat of litigation.
3. **Pending Chapter 11 Bankruptcy and District Court Stay:** Cumulus filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in March 2026, and the district court has stayed the underlying merits proceedings (App. 14a–15a). Furthermore, the Second Circuit expressly invited Nielsen to move the district court to modify or vacate the preliminary injunction if Cumulus's intervening bankruptcy alters the irreparable harm calculus (App. 47a). Granting certiorari in a private commercial dispute where the plaintiff-respondent is in Chapter 11 reorganization and the underlying action is stayed carries substantial risk of mootness, settlement, or shifting factual circumstances before merits resolution.
