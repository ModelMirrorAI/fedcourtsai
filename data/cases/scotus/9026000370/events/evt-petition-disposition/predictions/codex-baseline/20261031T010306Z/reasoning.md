## Baseline and adjustments

The originating-Second-Circuit cut has n=2,097 resolved and approximately 4.9% grants plus GVRs. Neither replaces the selected private-petition anchor. In the paid scored segment, terminal relist-0 has n=10,204 resolved and about 1.8% grant-family outcomes, relist-1 n=2,532 and 13.5%, relist-2 n=495 and 41.2%, and relist-3+ n=482 and 37.1%. The 2+ amicus bucket has n=1,446 resolved and about 22.7% grant-family outcomes, versus n=11,109 and about 3.8% with zero. These overlapping, terminal-state marginal cuts cannot be multiplied or treated as independent evidence.

I move above the 5.09% anchor because this petition presents a concrete, consequential legal disagreement rather than routine fact correction. The petition's pp. 11–18 identify the Sixth Circuit's Collins Inkjet rule and the Ninth Circuit's discount-attribution approach against the Second Circuit's constructive-tying analysis. The two amicus entries support treating the question as consequential beyond the immediate parties, although their texts were not provisioned and I infer no particular argument from them.

Independent checking of Collins Inkjet Corp. v. Eastman Kodak Co., 781 F.3d 264 (6th Cir. 2015), through CourtListener confirms its below-cost-equivalence requirement for a tie enforced solely through differential pricing. That supports the petition's strongest cert contention. It also cautions against accepting the petition's broad framing: Collins itself affirmed a preliminary injunction, and economic equivalence under a discount-attribution test is not the same as asking whether each invoice price exceeds its own cost. A grant is plausible, but the alleged split requires careful matching of the conduct and tests.

The appendix materially tempers the petition. At Pet. App. 25a–29a, the panel grounds constructive tying in Loew's and its own American Manufacturers precedent. At 30a–34a, it treats the standalone offer as continuation of an express tying policy, emphasizes largely undisputed negotiation facts, and distinguishes lawful monopoly pricing from exclusionary conduct. At 31a–32a it notes that Nielsen did not challenge the finding that the original Network Policy was unlawful express tying. That context gives a respondent a substantial answer to the characterization of this case as ordinary benign bundle discounting. The appellate analysis does not squarely work through Collins's cost-attribution test; the exact preservation and fit of the petition's cost-based formulation remain uncertain on the materials examined.

Finally, this is review of a preliminary injunction, with merits proceedings and a separate enforcement appeal still pending according to the petition's related-proceedings section and pp. 26–27. A legal issue can warrant interlocutory review, but the Court can await a final record or a cleaner vehicle. No response request, actual relist, or invitation to the government yet supplies a judicial-interest signal. Those limitations keep the upward adjustment modest rather than adopting the amicus bucket's roughly 23% marginal rate. The resulting 14% is a judgmental update, not a fitted empirical estimate.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The 26% redistribution forecast includes a later distribution following a requested response, not merely a true post-conference relist. The existing waiver and first conference date make outright denial the modal path, while the legal conflict supplies a meaningful chance of further scrutiny. I assign a 6% CVSG probability because federal antitrust-policy interests make consultation possible, but this private, interlocutory dispute does not itself establish that consultation is likely. The published terminal CVSG cut is not an invitation hazard.

Conditional on a grant, I give only 7% to disposition in the cert order. No identified intervening decision supports a routine GVR, and resolving this doctrinal conflict against the panel's older authorities would more naturally require briefing and argument. Conditional on denial, I give 6% to a noted dissent or statement. The order-writer probabilities are unconditional and very small: an unwritten order is overwhelmingly likely. Their slight tilt toward Thomas and Gorsuch is a low-confidence judgment about possible interest in the asserted legal conflict, not a vote prediction or a claim about an observed case-specific signal.

The significance score, 0.58, measures the potential reach of a pricing/tying rule across industries, not grant likelihood. The petition's pp. 23–27 argues for that broader reach; I discount its advocacy and the interlocutory, bilateral setting.
