## In brief

Nielsen asks the Court to review a ruling that it may have illegally tied its data products together by pricing a standalone option too high.

## What happened

Nielsen sells radio audience data, including a national report that is the only one of its kind. Cumulus, a broadcaster, wanted to buy that national report along with data in only some local markets, planning to buy other local data from a competitor, Eastlan. Nielsen's policy required customers like Cumulus to buy its local data everywhere they operated to get a full national report. After Cumulus objected that this was an illegal tying arrangement under antitrust law, Nielsen offered the national report alone, but at a much higher price. Cumulus sued, and a district court issued a preliminary injunction against Nielsen; the Second Circuit affirmed, finding Cumulus likely to succeed on its claim that the high standalone price itself functioned as an illegal tie.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a seller that offers its products both separately and bundled, at prices nobody claims are below its own costs, can still be held liable for illegally forcing customers to buy a bundle, simply because a court decides the standalone price is too high compared to the bundled discount.

## Where it stands

Nielsen's petition was distributed for a November 13, 2026 conference. The Court then asked Cumulus to respond, with that response now due December 15, 2026 after a partial extension. The Court has not yet acted on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Nielsen, which lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Second Circuit's ruling. If the Court grants review and later rules for Nielsen, the finding that its pricing was an illegal tie would be overturned, and the preliminary injunction limiting its pricing could fall. If it rules for Cumulus, the injunction and the Second Circuit's legal reasoning would stand. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's decision favoring Cumulus remains in effect, and the case continues in the lower courts.
