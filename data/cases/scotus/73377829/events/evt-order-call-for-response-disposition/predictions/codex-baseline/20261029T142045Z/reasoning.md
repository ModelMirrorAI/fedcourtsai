## Why move to 8%?

**Upward considerations.** The response request is an affirmative departure from disposing of the petition on the waiver. It supports some possibility that a Justice wants to examine the issue with adversarial briefing. The first question also raises an institution-wide issue: whether an internal judiciary employment process supplies constitutionally adequate safeguards for discrimination complaints. The petition's account of an earlier partial reversal and a subsequent six-day trial means this is not simply an undeveloped pleading. Its institutional significance is real even if review is unlikely.

**Limits and downward considerations.** The petition chiefly presents importance and asserted error, rather than demonstrating a developed conflict among appellate holdings on the same preserved question. Its second question asks the Court to derive enforceable Title VII-equivalent protections from the Congressional Accountability Act and Judicial Conference policy, although the petition itself acknowledges Congress declined to extend the statutory scheme to the judiciary (petition pp. 31-37). A policy commitment and a statutory reporting requirement do not by themselves establish the particular enforceable remedy requested; that is a contested premise of the petition, not an established holding I assume.

The appended appellate opinion supplies a particularly important vehicle obstacle. It declined to reach the argument that Title VII's exclusion was unconstitutional because Strickland first raised it on appeal from the final judgment and supplied no exceptional reason to excuse that failure (appendix pp. 44a-45a). This is not a finding that every due-process issue was forfeited. It does, however, make the petition's broad comparison between judiciary employees and Title VII-covered employees a less clean vehicle for a nationwide constitutional holding.

The adjudicated due-process questions were also intertwined with trial findings about available remedies, whether she reasonably viewed the unit executive as the final decisionmaker, and why she did not pursue Chapter X procedures. The appellate court described Chapter X as facially adequate under the earlier decision and distinguished it from the disciplinary procedure (appendix pp. 10a-19a). Thus, the most sweeping formulation of an entirely unavailable independent remedy does not straightforwardly match the lower court's reasoning. The case concerns a 2013 plan, and the petition notes subsequent model and circuit revisions (petition p. 10 n.1), further complicating the case's fit as the vehicle for current system-wide reform. The affirmance was unanimous by a panel drawn from outside the Fourth Circuit (appendix pp. 2a-3a); I infer neither an intra-panel split nor a Supreme Court recusal from the lower-court recusals.

Balancing those points, I place review modestly above the 5.23% primary anchor, at 8%, rather than treating the response request as evidence that four votes for review already exist. The adjustment is judgment, not a fitted response-request model. The main uncertainty is how much independent interest the constitutional adequacy issue attracts once the government responds, despite the preservation and factual obstacles.
