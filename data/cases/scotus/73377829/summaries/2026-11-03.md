## In brief

A former federal public defender asks the Court to revive her claim that the judiciary's internal process for handling her sexual harassment complaint violated her constitutional rights.

## What happened

Caryn Strickland worked for the Federal Public Defender's Office in the Western District of North Carolina. She alleged her supervisor sexually harassed her and that the judiciary's response, carried out under an internal Employment Dispute Resolution (EDR) Plan rather than under Title VII (the law barring workplace discrimination for most other employees), violated her due process and equal protection rights. After a six-day trial, the district court ruled against her, and the Fourth Circuit affirmed. Strickland asked for rehearing, which was denied for lack of a quorum after Fourth Circuit judges recused themselves.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution allows the judicial branch to resolve its own employees' harassment and discrimination claims through an internal process, instead of extending them the protections Congress gave other workers under Title VII. The Court is also asked whether a law directing the judiciary to study applying Title VII-type protections to its own employees means the judiciary's internal process must match Title VII's substantive protections.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief opposing review from the federal respondents and a reply from Strickland. The case was distributed for the Court's November 13, 2026 conference, where the justices will decide whether to take it.

## What each outcome would mean

Strickland is asking the Court to take up her case and reverse the Fourth Circuit's ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the constitutional and statutory questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision rejecting Strickland's claims stands, and the judiciary's internal EDR process continues to govern such disputes rather than Title VII.
