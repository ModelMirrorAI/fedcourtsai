## In brief

A former federal public defender asks the Court to hear her challenge to the judiciary's internal process for handling her sexual harassment complaint against a supervisor.

## What happened

Caryn Strickland worked as a federal public defender in the Western District of North Carolina. She says her supervisor sexually harassed her, and that when she raised the problem, the judiciary's internal Employment Dispute Resolution (EDR) process—used because judiciary employees are not covered by Title VII, the law that protects most workers from workplace discrimination—failed to protect her. After a bench trial, a district court ruled against her, though it found many problems with how the process was carried out. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals, sitting with judges from outside the circuit because all Fourth Circuit judges recused themselves, affirmed that ruling. The full Fourth Circuit later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether requiring judiciary employees to resolve harassment and discrimination claims through the judiciary's own internal process—rather than through the protections available to other workers—violates constitutional guarantees of fair process and equal treatment. The Court is also asked whether a circuit's internal process that falls short of those broader protections breaks a federal law and policy meant to give judiciary employees comparable rights.

## Where it stands

The federal respondents waived their right to respond, but the Court later asked for a response, which has now been delayed several times by agreed extensions, most recently to October 13, 2026. The petition has not yet been distributed for a conference since that response was requested.

## What each outcome would mean

Strickland, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up her case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the questions itself, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies the petition, the Fourth Circuit's ruling against Strickland stays in place, though a denial would not mean the Court agrees with that outcome. The case remains at the stage of the Court deciding whether to hear it at all.
