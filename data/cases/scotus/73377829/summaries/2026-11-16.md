## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a former federal public defender's bid to revive constitutional and statutory claims over how the judiciary handled her sexual harassment complaint.

## What happened

Caryn Strickland, a former attorney with the Federal Public Defender's Office in the Western District of North Carolina, sued judiciary officials after reporting that her supervisor sexually harassed her. She claimed the judiciary's internal Employment Dispute Resolution Plan violated her due-process and equal-protection rights, and that excluding judiciary employees from Title VII's workplace protections was unconstitutional. After a six-day bench trial, the district court ruled against her, and the Fourth Circuit affirmed, finding she had voluntarily withdrawn from the internal process before completing it and had not shown she was unfairly coerced to do so.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asked whether the Constitution requires the judiciary to give its employees the same kind of independent decisionmaker, procedural protections, and remedies that Title VII gives most other workers, rather than relying on its own internal dispute process. It also asked whether a law directing the judiciary to study applying Title VII to its employees means the judiciary's internal plan must match Title VII's protections.

## Where it stands

The Court considered the petition at conferences in June and November 2026. On November 16, 2026, it denied the petition and granted a motion by amici to file a brief.

## What each outcome would mean

Strickland, who lost in the lower courts, asked the Court to take up her case. By denying review, the Court leaves the Fourth Circuit's ruling in place, meaning her claims remain rejected and the judiciary's internal dispute process, as applied in her case, stands. A grant would instead have meant the Court would hear full arguments and decide the constitutional and statutory questions itself, without determining in advance which side would win.
