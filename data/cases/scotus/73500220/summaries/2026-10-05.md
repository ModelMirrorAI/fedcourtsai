## In brief

The Patels asked the Court to revive their claim that their guilty-plea lawyer's silence about related civil fraud liability violated their right to counsel; the Court denied review.

## What happened

Nita and Kirtish Patel pleaded guilty to health care fraud. The next day, the government intervened in a separate civil fraud lawsuit against them, and their plea admissions were later used to find them liable for millions of dollars in that case. The Patels argued in habeas corpus proceedings that their lawyer knew about the pending civil suit but never warned them that pleading guilty could lock in that civil liability, and that this failure violated their right to counsel. The district court and the Third Circuit Court of Appeals both rejected the claim, holding that this right only requires lawyers to advise clients about the direct consequences of a plea, not collateral ones like separate civil liability.

## What the Court is being asked

The Patels asked the Court to decide whether the right to counsel requires a defense lawyer to advise a client about a consequence of a guilty plea that is not part of the criminal sentence itself, when that consequence is both closely tied to the conviction and severe. They pointed to a split among federal appeals courts and state courts on how broadly an earlier Supreme Court ruling about deportation advice extends to other serious side effects of a guilty plea.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond to the petition. The case was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, and the petition was denied on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Patels asked the Court to take up their case and overturn the Third Circuit's ruling against them. Granting review would have let the Court hear full arguments and decide whether their lawyer's silence about the civil fraud consequences violated their right to counsel. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Third Circuit's decision in place, meaning the Patels' convictions and the related civil liability judgment against them stand, and the lower court's rule limiting counsel's advice duty to direct consequences remains the law in that circuit.
