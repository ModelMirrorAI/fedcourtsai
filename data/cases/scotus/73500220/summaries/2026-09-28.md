## In brief

Nita and Kirtish Patel ask the Court to decide whether their lawyer's silence about looming civil fraud liability made their guilty pleas unconstitutional.

## What happened

Nita and Kirtish Patel pleaded guilty to healthcare fraud in 2015. The next day, the government intervened in a separate lawsuit accusing the couple's companies of defrauding Medicare, and later won a judgment against them for more than $7.7 million, based partly on facts they had already admitted in their guilty pleas. The Patels later argued in habeas petitions that their criminal defense lawyer never told them the guilty pleas would expose them to this civil liability, and that had they known, they would not have pleaded guilty. The district court denied relief, and the Third Circuit affirmed, holding that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of effective counsel does not require lawyers to advise clients about this kind of civil consequence.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the right to a competent lawyer in a criminal case includes a right to be told about a serious legal consequence outside the criminal case itself—here, civil fraud liability—when that consequence is closely tied to the guilty plea and financially severe. The Patels argue lower courts disagree on how far this protection reaches beyond a prior case involving deportation, and ask the Court to clarify the rule.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference after the government waived its right to respond. The docket shows no action since that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

The Patels, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal question itself, without yet determining who wins. If the Court denies review, the Third Circuit's ruling against the Patels stays in place, meaning their lawyer's failure to warn them about civil liability would not count as a Sixth Amendment violation, and the multimillion-dollar judgment against them would stand. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with the Third Circuit, only that its decision remains final.
