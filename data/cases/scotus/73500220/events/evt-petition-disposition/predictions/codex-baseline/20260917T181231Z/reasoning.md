The constitutional question and the precedential appellate decision give the petition more substance than a routine factual challenge. The petition documents divergent approaches to extending Padilla beyond deportation, including civil commitment, registration, parole, and denaturalization (petition pp. 12–24). But that is not a demonstrated square conflict over FCA civil liability. Farhane's denaturalization analysis remains tied to deportation; affirmative misadvice cases also differ from a failure-to-warn claim. I treat the petition's split characterization as advocacy, not as an independently established same-issue split.

Three vehicle problems outweigh those positive considerations:

- **An independent retroactivity holding.** Appendix A, pp. 14–16, holds that extending counsel's duty to FCA liability would announce a new rule unavailable on collateral review. The single QP does not separately frame that issue, and the petition does not squarely develop a response to this alternative ground. Correcting a categorical collateral-consequence rule would not alone secure relief. I verified the underlying new-rule distinction in *Chaidez v. United States*, 568 U.S. 342, 347–354, 358 (2013), through CourtListener. The obstacle is the proposed extension to FCA liability, not a claim that these post-2010 pleas predated Padilla.
- **An additional FCA-specific ground.** Appendix A, p. 13 n.3, says the petitioners lose even under their broader reading because this civil liability is not comparable to deportation. Thus, the Court could disagree with the appellate court's broad language yet leave its disposition undisturbed.
- **Advice and prejudice remain contested.** The petition alleges counsel knew of the pending civil proceeding and failed to explain the estoppel consequences (pp. 8–11, 31–34). The appellate account reports warnings that the plea did not foreclose civil proceedings and evidence concerning global settlement discussions (App. 6–7). The record does not establish that better advice would have produced a different plea or a rationally preferable trial. The appellate court did not reach the requested evidentiary-hearing issue (App. 16 n.5). These are vehicle uncertainties, not findings that prejudice was independently adjudicated against the petitioners on appeal.

Together with the response waiver and absence of affirmative Court attention beyond initial distribution, those problems move my estimate from 5.12% to **1.5%**. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted model. Most residual grant probability reflects the possibility that the Court values clarification of the broader constitutional boundary despite this vehicle.
