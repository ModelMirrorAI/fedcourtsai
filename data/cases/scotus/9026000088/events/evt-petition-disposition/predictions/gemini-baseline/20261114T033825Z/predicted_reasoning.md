Beyond that guaranteed post-response distribution (`distribution_count = 2`), there is roughly a one-in-three chance (~35%) of at least one true post-opposition conference relist (`distribution_count >= 3`)—either for chambers to scrutinize the state-law statutory tailoring record (similar to *Smith v. ## 2. Call for the Views of the Solicitor General (`cvsg-increment`: 0.003)

The Court will **not** call for the views of the Solicitor General. This petition presents a pure First Amendment and Fourteenth Amendment due process challenge to a state agency's enforcement of state ballot-measure disclosure statutes (Alaska Stat. § 15.13). No federal statute, treaty, preemption question, or federal agency program is implicated, and the Court has resolved analogous post-*AFPF v. Bonta* state and municipal campaign-finance disclosure petitions (*Gaspee Project*, *No on E*, *Smith v. Stillie*, *Rio Grande Foundation*) without inviting the Solicitor General's views.

## 3. Certiorari Disposition (`disposition`: 0.11 — Denied)

After receiving the briefs in opposition and redistributing the petition in early 2027, the Court will **deny certiorari** (~88% denied, ~1% dismissed, ~11% granted).

Although at least one chambers called for a response and sixteen amicus briefs urge review, the briefs in opposition will expose formidable doctrinal and vehicle obstacles to assembling four votes for certiorari:

1. **Lack of four votes to revisit *Citizens United* Part IV on election-related disclosure:** The Court has repeatedly declined since *Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta*, 594 U.S. 595 (2021), to grant review to extend *AFPF* against state and local ballot-measure donor-reporting and on-communication top-donor disclaimer laws—most recently denying certiorari on October 5, 2026 in *Rio Grande Foundation v. Oliver* (No. 25-1248) after likewise requesting a response over a state waiver, and previously denying review in *Smith v. Stillie* (No. 23-1316, challenging Alaska's current AS 15.13 disclosure regime) and *No on E v. Chiu* (No. 23-926).
2. **Binding state-law earmarking construction neutralizing the donor-unmasking claim:** Under the Alaska Supreme Court's authoritative construction of the 2020 statutes at issue (AS 15.13.040(e)(5) and AS 15.13.090(a)(2)(C), (c)), reportable "contributors"—including the "three largest contributors, if any" on a communication disclaimer—are limited to donors who contributed *for the purpose of influencing an election or ballot proposition*, and no contributor statement is required where an entity has no such election-purpose contributors. Because a 501(c)(3) organization with no election-earmarked donors is not compelled under that binding construction to unmask its general-support donors at all, Questions Presented 1 and 3 do not cleanly present the sweeping donor-privacy injury the petition describes.
3. **Fact-bound record and superseded statutory version:** Contrary to the framing that petitioner never discussed the November 2020 election, petitioner's July 24, 2020 Alaska press release opened with *"As Alaskans take to the polls in November, history should provide a warning for what Ranked Choice Voting would lead to,"* and its July 31 embedded video displayed *"SAY NO TO RANKED CHOICE VOTING"* while Ballot Measure 2 was certified for the November ballot. In addition, the 2020 version of AS 15.13 enforced here (with all civil penalties waived) was superseded in February 2021 when Ballot Measure 2 took effect.

## 4. Conditional Scope of Review and Summary-Disposition Route (`summary-disposition-route`: 0.05)

- **Conditional scope if granted:** If the Court were to grant certiorari, it would likely **narrow or reformulate Review to Question Presented 2** (whether *WRTL II*'s objective functional-equivalent standard rather than a contextual *Furgatch*-derived standard governs the boundary between issue speech and regulated ballot-measure advocacy) or pair QP 2 with QP 3 (compelled on-communication disclaimers), rather than taking all three sprawling questions as drafted. If granted on the merits, a majority would likely vacate or reverse narrowly on the ground that applying contextual timing and external coalition activity to classify non-explicit policy commentary as "express communications" fails exacting scrutiny / First Amendment vagueness limits without reaching wholesale invalidation of ballot-measure disclosure regimes.
- **Plenary review vs. summary disposition:** Conditional on a grant, plenary merits briefing and oral argument is overwhelmingly the likelier route (~95%), with only a small conditional probability (`0.05`) of a cert-order GVR or per curiam summary reversal. No pending Supreme Court merits case presents an overlapping question for a hold-and-GVR, and lower federal appellate courts have uniformly upheld comparable state ballot-measure disclosure and disclaimer laws rather than defying settled precedent in a manner inviting summary reversal.

## 5. Separate Writings on Denial (`dissent-from-denial`: 0.08)

Most likely (~92% conditional on denial), certiorari will be denied **without noted dissent or statement**, matching the Court's unannotated denials in *Rio Grande Foundation v. Oliver*, *Smith v. Stillie*, *No on E v. Chiu*, and *Gaspee Project v. Mederos*. There is, however, a modest conditional probability (`0.08`) that one or more conservative Justices—most plausibly **Justice Thomas** (who has long argued that campaign-finance disclosure and disclaimer mandates trigger strict scrutiny), **Justice Gorsuch**, or **Justice Alito**, or **Justice Kavanaugh** in a brief vehicle-focused statement respecting denial—writes separately to flag First Amendment concerns when state campaign-finance agencies regulate policy think tanks' issue commentary near ballot-measure elections.
