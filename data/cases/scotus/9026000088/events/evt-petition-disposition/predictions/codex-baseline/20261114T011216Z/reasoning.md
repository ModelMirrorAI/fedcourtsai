## Why above the anchor, but below even odds

The questions concern whether nonprofit speech about an issue, without discussing the election itself, can trigger campaign registration and donor disclosure; whether Alaska's advocacy test is vague; and whether donor names can be compelled on the communication itself. The petition describes education about ranked-choice voting, including reposting a national coalition's video, as the trigger. Those questions have consequences beyond Alaska and connect associational privacy with the boundary between issue advocacy and election advocacy. Sixteen briefs and the Court's decision to demand a response after waivers distinguish this petition substantially from a routine paid petition. [Sources: questions-presented.txt; petition.txt, pp. 1–9; snapshot, response request and amicus entries.]

There is a substantial legal argument, not an automatic entitlement to review. Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, 594 U.S. 595, 607–11 (2021), requires narrow tailoring under exacting scrutiny even without a least-restrictive-means requirement. That makes the claimed overbreadth and compelled donor labels important. But Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 368–69 (2010), rejected restricting the federal disclosure requirements there to express advocacy and its functional equivalent. Applying a spending-restriction precedent such as Wisconsin Right to Life to disclosure is consequently a contested doctrinal step. My inference is that resolving that boundary could attract review, but it also gives respondents a substantial answer. [Sources: petition.txt, pp. 9–21, 32–37; CourtListener primary-opinion excerpts identified in retrieval.md.]

The asserted splits need discounting. The petition's small-contribution authorities involve differing thresholds, amounts, and burdens; they do not by themselves establish opposite outcomes on identical facts. Its earmarking discussion acknowledges the Alaska court's view that the statute already limits relevant contributions, then disputes the adequacy and clarity of that limitation. Some cited federal decisions interpret federal statutes or regulations rather than hold every broader law unconstitutional. For on-communication donor disclosure, the petition expressly describes the First and Ninth Circuits as reaching the same general result as Alaska. That is a request to revisit the governing approach, not an established contrary appellate holding on that exact question. These weaknesses keep me below the probability suggested by treating every asserted conflict as square. [Source: petition.txt, pp. 22–37, especially pp. 27–31 and 32–33.]

The incorporated state opinion confirms a final affirmance on statutory and federal constitutional grounds and treats the publications as election-related communications. The surviving excerpt also reports that the agency waived the proposed civil penalty but ordered compliance; I do not treat a waived fine as eliminating the dispute. The petition asserts preservation and no mootness or jurisdictional obstacle, but that is an advocate's account rather than a point tested against an opposition. [Sources: petition.txt, Appendix A, pp. 1a–5a, 13a–15a; petition pp. 37–38.]

## Other probabilities and limitations

**Another distribution: 99%.** This is the literal increment beyond the snapshot's one distribution, not a 99% forecast of a substantive post-conference relist. The outstanding requested response should ordinarily be followed by redistribution. Additional substantive relists after that are appreciably less certain.

**New CVSG: 2%.** There is no existing CVSG. The federal constitutional issue does not on this record require specialized federal administrative input. **Summary route given a grant: 12%.** No provisioned companion or intervening decision supplies an obvious mechanical remand; resolving these contested standards more naturally calls for plenary briefing. `same-question.json` lists no above-threshold match, which is not proof that no relevant case exists.

**Writing given denial: 14%.** The issue can support a donor-privacy or political-speech statement, but an unexplained denial remains the central prediction. The order-writer numbers are unconditional, low-probability forecasts, not votes or numbers conditional on denial. Their sum is 0.125; this is compatible with roughly 0.102 unconditional probability of a denial accompanied by a writing, with modest additional writing risk on other routes and some possibility of multiple authors. Thomas receives the largest individual probability as a judgment about doctrinal fit, not information about private conference votes.

The **0.72 significance score** measures the possible reach of a decision for nonprofit policy speech, donor privacy, and ballot-measure regulation, not grant likelihood.
