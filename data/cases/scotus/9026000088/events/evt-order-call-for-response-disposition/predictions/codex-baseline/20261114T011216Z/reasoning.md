## Why this petition could attract review

The petition challenges the boundary between discussion of public policy and election advocacy, a vagueness standard, and requirements to print donor identities on communications. These are recurring First Amendment questions that can reach many nonprofit speakers, not merely the administration of ranked-choice voting. APF presents both facial and as-applied challenges and says it preserved them throughout an agency proceeding and two levels of judicial review. The final state-court judgment provides a possible vehicle for a federal constitutional ruling without awaiting further ordinary appellate review. These are petition representations, not findings about the unavailable opposition. Source: petition, questions presented, pp. 1–9 and 37–38.

There is a substantial legal argument against demanding identification of general donors as the price of policy speech. Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta, 594 U.S. 595, 607–615 (2021), requires narrow tailoring of compelled donor disclosure even without a least-restrictive-means requirement. The official opinion was consulted independently. That makes the tailoring question serious, though Bonta's charitable-registration setting does not itself resolve election-related disclosure. The twelve amicus briefs reinforce the question's practical importance rather than proving a constitutional defect. Sources: Bonta official opinion, part II; petition pp. 19–21, 32–37; snapshot amicus entries.

## Why denial remains more likely

First, the petition's premise that APF only discussed policy is contestable on the actual materials. The included state opinion reproduces the July press release's express reference to Alaskans going to the polls in November, and the petition itself acknowledges that the embedded video told its audience to say no to ranked-choice voting. The absence of the ballot measure's formal name does not eliminate an argument that these were electoral appeals. The state court's opening analysis treats the express-communication test as a narrowing construction of a broader ballot-expenditure statute. Those details make this a less frictionless vehicle than the questions presented suggest. Sources: petition pp. 17–18; included opinion App.8a, 20a–24a.

Second, Citizens United v. FEC, 558 U.S. 310, 366–371 (2010), upheld disclosure and disclaimer requirements and declined to limit disclosure to express advocacy or its functional equivalent. Its opinion was consulted independently. APF argues that the electioneering-communication setting distinguishes that holding, and invokes Wisconsin Right to Life's protections in a different setting. That is an arguable doctrinal distinction, not a demonstrated direct contradiction requiring summary correction. Source: Citizens United, part IV; petition pp. 9–19.

Third, the alleged conflicts are uneven. The small-expenditure decisions cited by APF involve different thresholds, burdens, and factual records. Most importantly, independent checking confirms that Van Hollen v. FEC, 811 F.3d 486 (D.C. Cir. 2016), upheld an agency earmarking rule under statutory and administrative-law review; permission to choose a narrower rule is not a constitutional holding requiring every state to choose it. The petition also acknowledges that the Alaska court construed state law to include a political-purpose limitation, and then disputes that limitation's sufficiency and implementation. Those features weaken the clean-split characterization. Sources: petition pp. 22–32; Van Hollen opinion, introduction and part II, retrieved through CourtListener.

Fourth, the on-communication question has substantial disagreement but not an established favorable circuit holding on the same rule in the materials read. The independently retrieved amended No on E v. Chiu opinion, 85 F.4th 493 (9th Cir. 2023), applied exacting scrutiny and rejected a preliminary-injunction challenge to a different donor disclaimer regime, with separate disagreement. That supports both recurrence and the existence of a developed defense of these laws. It does not establish that Alaska's rule is constitutional, or that four Justices will take this vehicle. Sources: No on E amended opinion, discussion of exacting scrutiny and narrow tailoring; petition pp. 32–37.

Taken together, a potentially attractive speech case with unusually broad amicus support and an affirmative response request warrants **24%**, but the mixed vehicle and less-than-square conflicts leave denial at approximately three chances in four. The number is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted response-request model.
