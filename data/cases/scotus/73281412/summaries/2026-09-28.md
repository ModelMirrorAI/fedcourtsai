## In brief

A former pretrial detainee asks the Court to revive his lawsuit over conditions in a jail's suicide-prevention cell and its mental health care, after the Fifth Circuit rejected his claims.

## What happened

Ronnie Alexander was held in the Henderson County, Texas jail in March 2021. After telling guards he was suicidal, he was placed for five days in a bare "violent cell" designed for suicide prevention, with no toilet, shower, bedding, or clothing. He later sued the county, several officers, and mental health providers, claiming the cell's conditions amounted to unconstitutional punishment and that the jail's mental health care was inadequate. The district court dismissed his claims. A divided Fifth Circuit panel affirmed, with one judge dissenting. Alexander's request for rehearing was denied, and he petitioned the Supreme Court for review.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises three questions. First, whether jail mental health providers' constitutional duty to give medical care to detainees is limited to protecting them from violence and suicide, as Alexander reads the appeals court's ruling, or extends further, as he argues the Court's precedent requires. Second, whether jail conditions imposed in the name of suicide prevention can still count as unlawful punishment when they are excessive and deprive a detainee of basic necessities, or whether a looser standard applies, as Alexander says the appeals court used. Third, whether a court reviewing a request to dismiss a complaint may treat any inference about an official's intent as too speculative to consider, or must weigh the detainee's allegations as a whole in his favor.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for two conferences, most recently one dated after briefing was complete; respondents filed briefs opposing review, and Alexander filed a reply. The docket does not show any ruling yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Alexander, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide these questions, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision affirming dismissal of Alexander's claims stands, and the case ends in favor of the county, the officers, and the mental health providers. The respondents' briefs argue the questions rest on a mistaken reading of the appeals court's opinion and that the case is a poor vehicle for review.
