## Why 24%, rather than the approximately 5% anchor

The unusually strong positive evidence is the combination of a substantive constitutional question, seven recorded amicus entries, including the states' filing, and a Court-requested response after waiver. The latter is affirmative attention already visible in the common snapshot. It is not a grant signal from four Justices and is not a CVSG. I give it weight without treating it as dispositive.

The petition's first question challenges differential treatment of religious degrees within and across Virginia's civilian and National Guard tuition programs; the second asks the Court to overrule Locke v. Davey, 540 U.S. 712 (2004). The petition describes overlapping religious coursework, different classifications of similar programs, and intrusive administration. It advances both a narrower discrimination theory and a broader attack on Locke, citing the post-Locke religious-benefits cases. Those features make it more substantial than routine error correction. See petition pp. 6–13, 18–34 and the questions-presented extract; these are the petitioners' contentions, not independently established findings about every program detail.

I checked the asserted Tenth Circuit authority, Colorado Christian University v. Weaver, 534 F.3d 1245, 1255–56 (10th Cir. 2008), through CourtListener. The retrieved passage distinguishes Locke where a funding scheme discriminates among religions and requires intrusive religious judgments. This supports a genuine doctrinal argument. It does not independently establish a square circuit split here: Colorado Christian addressed exclusions of institutions, while this petition concerns degrees, and this appeal has no merits judgment yet. The Fourth Circuit's Hall ruling is described by the petition, not separately verified in this run.

The major downward adjustment is procedural. This is expressly certiorari before judgment under Rule 11, not an ordinary petition following a final appellate decision. The petition itself invokes the imperative-public-importance standard and says the appeal is fully briefed, the injunction pending appeal was denied, and expedition was deferred. See petition pp. 16–17, 37–38. An asserted likely merits victory does not by itself establish why the Court must intervene before the Fourth Circuit decides. The related Hall petition, described as following a Fourth Circuit decision, also offers an alternative vehicle for the common Locke question. The broader factual coverage here is an advantage for the petitioners' argument but not necessarily for judicial administration.

The included district-court opinion reinforces the vehicle concern. It allowed Stevens's National Guard claims to survive dismissal because factual development remained appropriate, but denied preliminary relief in light of Locke; it also discussed partial mootness of semester-specific requests without declaring the whole controversy moot. See petition appendix pp. 25a–27a. I therefore do not characterize this petition as wholly moot or all claims as dismissed. Mixed procedural postures and an incomplete record nevertheless reduce the appeal of immediate plenary review.

The resulting 24% is a judgmental forecast, not a fitted estimate: materially above the baseline because of the response request, organized support, and substantial legal issue, yet well below even odds because the Court can decline this vehicle without rejecting its underlying concerns. For coherence, an illustrative probability allocation is 9.6% plenary grant, 14.4% summary grant route, 75% denial, and 1% other non-grant termination. This yields 24% any grant and a 60% summary share conditional on a grant.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The 95% distribution-increment estimate rests primarily on the response deadline falling after the currently scheduled conference. It forecasts a recorded redistribution, not necessarily a substantive relist. Three percent for a CVSG reflects the absence of a clear need for federal technical input; it leaves room for an interest connected to military tuition assistance. The 60% conditional summary-route estimate is particularly sensitive to companion-case handling and includes a potential later GVR, rather than predicting a summary merits reversal now. Eighteen percent for a writing conditional on denial reflects tension between doctrinal salience and an uncomplicated procedural reason not to reach the merits. These three latter figures are judgmental and have no case-specific empirical calibration in the consulted pack.

A stakes score of 0.78 reflects the potential reach across religious higher education, public tuition assistance, and the survival or narrowing of a Supreme Court precedent. It is not the grant probability. This petition's procedural weakness does not eliminate that significance if the questions are decided.
