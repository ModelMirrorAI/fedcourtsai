## In brief

Three Virginia students ask the Court to hear, before a lower appeals court rules, their challenge to state tuition grant programs that fund some religious college majors but not others.

## What happened

Cameron Johnson, Luke Thomas, and Trace Stevens are Virginia students who were denied state tuition grants because their chosen religious majors were classified as ineligible, even though Virginia funds other, similar religious majors. They sued Virginia officials, arguing the funding distinctions violate the First Amendment's protections for religious exercise. A federal district court dismissed two students' claims and denied the third a preliminary injunction, relying on the Supreme Court's 2004 decision in Locke v. Davey, which it read as barring any such challenge. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals denied a request to block the programs while the appeal continues, and has not yet ruled on the merits. The students then asked the Supreme Court to take the case before the Fourth Circuit decides it.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's religion clauses allow a state to fund some religious college majors while excluding other, similar religious majors from the same grant program. The students also ask the Court to reconsider and discard its own past ruling that they say lower courts have wrongly stretched to block all such challenges.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 16, 2026 conference; the docket does not record a decision from that conference. The Court separately asked Virginia's officials to respond by November 4, 2026, and the officials have asked for more time to do so, which the students oppose.

## What each outcome would mean

The students, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case early. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case in full and decide the legal question itself, without first deciding who is right. If it denies the petition, the lower courts' rulings so far—which rejected two students' claims and declined to block the programs for the third—remain in place, and the case continues in those courts. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the state's programs are lawful, only that it is not taking up the question now.
