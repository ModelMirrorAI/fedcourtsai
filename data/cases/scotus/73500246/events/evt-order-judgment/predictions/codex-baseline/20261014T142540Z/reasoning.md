I raise P(disturbed) to **0.84** principally because of the statutory distinction developed in Judge Richardson's dissent. The statute separately describes support for terrorist activity and support to a terrorist organization. Requiring the latter to advance actual terrorist activity risks collapsing that distinction. Regular cooking for the organization's leaders also looks like a concrete contribution to maintaining the organization, rather than merely remote association. These are reasons to reject the legal test below independently of general views about immigration. Source: *Ozurumba v. Bondi*, 153 F.4th 396 (4th Cir. 2025), slip opinion pp. 23–28, particularly p. 26; official question-presented sheet for No. 25-1343.

The majority below nevertheless supplies a substantial opposing case: the modifier material must do work; the enumerated examples arguably identify support relevant and significant to terrorist activity; and compelled meal preparation with the group's own supplies may differ from giving money, weapons, or operational training. It also treats the criminal material-support precedent as consistent with an activity connection. I do not assume that the criminal statute and this immigration provision are interchangeable. The Fourth Circuit left the duress argument and the Convention Against Torture challenge unresolved, creating potential alternative grounds or remand work rather than guaranteeing immediate removal after a government victory. Sources: majority slip opinion pp. 12–19 and dissent pp. 26–28.
