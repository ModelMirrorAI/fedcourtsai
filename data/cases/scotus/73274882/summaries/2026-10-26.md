## In brief

California gun owners ask the Court to strike down the state's ban on possessing ammunition magazines holding more than 10 rounds.

## What happened

Virginia Duncan and other California residents, along with a gun-rights group, sued in 2017 over a California law banning possession of firearm magazines that hold more than 10 rounds. After years of litigation, including an earlier trip to the Supreme Court that sent the case back for reconsideration, a full Ninth Circuit panel ruled for California, holding that such magazines are not "arms" protected by the Second Amendment and, even if they were, that the ban fits the nation's history of regulating dangerous weapons. The panel also rejected a claim that the law takes property without compensation. Four judges dissented.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a ban on possessing magazines that hold more than 10 rounds violates the right to keep and bear arms, and separately whether requiring people to give up, modify, or sell magazines they already lawfully owned, without payment from the state, violates the constitutional protection against government takings of property.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference many times since November 2025 without any recorded grant or denial. The parties have filed supplemental letters and briefs updating the Court on related appeals court rulings, including a conflicting decision from another federal appeals court, and the docket shows no action on the petition as of the most recent entries.

## What each outcome would mean

Duncan and the other challengers are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit. If the Court grants review and later rules for Duncan, California's magazine ban could be struck down, at least in part. If it rules for Bonta, the ban would stand. If the Court denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's decision upholding the ban remains in effect, and California could enforce it, including its requirement that owners dispossess themselves of existing magazines; a denial does not mean the Court agrees with that ruling, only that it lets it stand.
