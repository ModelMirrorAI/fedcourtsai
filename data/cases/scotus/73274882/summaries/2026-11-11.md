## In brief

California gun owners ask the Court to strike down the state's ban on possessing ammunition magazines holding more than 10 rounds.

## What happened

Virginia Duncan and other California residents, along with a gun-rights group, sued in 2017 to block a state law requiring owners to give up, modify, or sell magazines that hold more than 10 rounds. After years of litigation, including an earlier trip to the Supreme Court that sent the case back for reconsideration, a federal district court ruled the ban unconstitutional. The full Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals disagreed, reversing and upholding the law, finding that such magazines are not protected "arms" under the Second Amendment and that, even if they were, the ban fits the nation's history of regulating dangerous weapons. The petitioners also argue the law amounts to an uncompensated taking of their property.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state may ban possessing ammunition magazines that hold more than 10 rounds without violating the right to keep and bear arms, and whether requiring owners to give up lawfully acquired magazines without payment violates the rule against the government taking private property without compensation.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for many conferences since November 2025 without the Court recording a grant or denial, most recently for a June 2026 conference; the parties have continued to file letters about related appeals court rulings, including a letter from California in August 2026 noting that the case overlaps with other cases the Court has already agreed to hear.

## What each outcome would mean

Duncan and the other challengers are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which upheld California's law. If the Court grants review and later rules for Duncan, California's magazine ban would be struck down, at least in part. If the Court instead affirms, the ban would remain in effect, and owners of larger-capacity magazines would need to modify, sell, surrender, or remove them from the state. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision upholding the ban stands, leaving California free to enforce it. A vacate-and-remand would set aside the Ninth Circuit's ruling without deciding the issue, sending the case back for further proceedings.
