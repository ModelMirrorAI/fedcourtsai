# Predicted Court Action: *Stanley Kappell Watson v. Shenekka Bradsher, et al.* (No. 25-651)

## Disposition and Procedural Trajectory

The Court will **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari without recorded dissent (`predicted_disposition: "denied"`, `probability: 0.04`).

### 1. Post-CFR Distribution and Relist Trajectory (`relist-increment: 0.96`)
As of the February 3, 2026 snapshot, the petition had been distributed once (on February 2, 2026, for the February 20, 2026 conference), and the Court requested a response on the same day, due March 4, 2026. Because the call for a response (CFR) removes the petition from active consideration at the initial conference pending receipt of the brief in opposition (or expiration of the response window, including any extensions), the petition will be redistributed for a subsequent conference once briefing is complete. Accordingly, the distribution count is virtually certain to rise above its snapshot value of 1, but the petition is unlikely to experience multiple post-response relists once re-distributed for conference.

### 2. Solicitor General Invitation (`cvsg-increment: 0.005`)
The Court will not call for the views of the Solicitor General (CVSG). While the case arises under the Bankruptcy Code (11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(6)), the dispute concerns the application of *Kawaauhau v. Geiger*, 523 U.S. 57 (1998), to a private state-court tort judgment for false imprisonment and the waiver/law-of-the-case bar applied by the lower courts. It does not implicate federal regulatory administration, United States trustee operations, or sovereign interests that would prompt a CVSG.

### 3. Summary Disposition vs. Plenary Review Conditional on Grant (`summary-disposition-route: 0.25`)
In the unlikely event certiorari is granted, plenary review (75% conditional probability) is more likely than a summary disposition (25% conditional probability). If the Court were to reach the merits despite the procedural obstacles below, it would do so to resolve the longstanding circuit tension over whether "substantial certainty" under *Geiger* is evaluated under a subjective or objective standard and how implied malice under § 523(a)(6) interacts with subjective mistake of fact.

### 4. Dissent from Denial and Order Writings (`dissent-from-denial: 0.015`)
The denial order will almost certainly issue as a standard one-line order without any separate statement or dissent. Although the petition invokes *Kawaauhau v. Geiger* and raises a recurring doctrinal question in bankruptcy law, the profound vehicle defects—most notably that the Eleventh Circuit did not even reach the § 523(a)(6) false-imprisonment dischargeability merits in *Watson II* because petitioner failed to preserve or appeal the issue on remand—make a separate writing respecting denial highly improbable (~1.5% aggregate probability, concentrated slightly in Justices Thomas and Gorsuch given their occasional interest in statutory text and bankruptcy questions).

### 5. Conditional Merits Assessment (If Granted)
If the Court were to grant certiorari, it would likely address only the first Question Presented (whether a debtor's genuine but mistaken belief that a victim stole his property precludes a finding of "willful and malicious injury" for false imprisonment under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(6)). However, if granted, the Court would face a high risk of either affirming on the narrow ground that intentionally procuring a person's unlawful physical confinement is an intentional injury regardless of motive, or dismissing the writ as improvidently granted (DIG) once full briefing highlights that the Eleventh Circuit's decision under review (*In re Watson*, 147 F.4th 1330 (11th Cir. 2025)) rested on law-of-the-case and waiver from an unappealed 2022 interlocutory remand order rather than a fresh merits holding on § 523(a)(6).
