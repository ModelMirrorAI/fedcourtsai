The main restraint is vehicle fit. The petition's argument at pages 6-8 invokes a real distinction between subjective and objective substantial certainty. But Appendix A, pages 10a-16a, expressly distinguishes intending an act from intending injury and rests on Watson's undisputed intent to cause confinement. It also treats justification through a separate malice inquiry, supported by findings concerning pressure on officers and persistence after their investigation. An honest belief that property was stolen is not self-evidently a conclusive privilege to demand the confinement that followed. Thus the asserted certainty-of-injury split need not determine this case.

General precedent retrieval reinforces that caution. Kawaauhau v. Geiger, 523 U.S. 57, 61-64 (1998), excludes negligently or recklessly inflicted injuries; it does not itself resolve every mistake-of-justification question involving a deliberately intended consequence. In re Su, 290 F.3d 1140, 1142-47 (9th Cir. 2002), applies a subjective willfulness standard and preserves a distinct malice inquiry. Its driving-injury setting does not establish that the Ninth Circuit necessarily would discharge this false-imprisonment debt. These authorities support taking the disagreement seriously without accepting the petition's claim of an inevitable opposite outcome elsewhere. I did not independently verify every other circuit authority the petition cites.
