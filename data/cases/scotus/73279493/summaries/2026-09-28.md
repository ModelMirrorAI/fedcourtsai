## In brief

Stanley Watson asks the Court to hear his case over whether his false-imprisonment debt can be discharged in bankruptcy despite his belief the confinement was lawful.

## What happened

Stanley Watson, a former Georgia county commissioner, accused Sheneeka Bradsher and Zarinah Ali of stealing his wallet at a bar and pressed police to arrest them; his wallet later turned up in his car. A jury awarded the women damages for slander, battery, and false imprisonment. After Watson filed for bankruptcy, the women sued to have that debt excluded from discharge. Bankruptcy and district courts ruled the slander debt dischargeable, because Watson genuinely believed his accusation, but the false-imprisonment debt nondischargeable. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, and Watson now petitions for review.

## What the Court is being asked

The bankruptcy law does not erase debts for "willful and malicious injury." The Court is asked whether "injury" in that law means any harm a person intentionally causes, or only harm from intentionally violating someone's legal rights. Relatedly, it is asked whether a debtor who honestly, if mistakenly, believed he was acting lawfully when he had someone confined can still be found to have caused that kind of injury, or whether courts may find fault simply by judging his conduct as excessive after the fact.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's February 20, 2026 conference. Instead of acting then, the Court asked the respondents to file a response, due March 4, 2026, so the case remains pending before the Court decides whether to hear it.

## What each outcome would mean

Watson, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide what "injury" means under this bankruptcy provision, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies review, the Eleventh Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Watson's debt to Bradsher and Ali for false imprisonment nondischargeable.
