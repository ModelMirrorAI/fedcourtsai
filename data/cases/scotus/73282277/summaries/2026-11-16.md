## In brief

Hamed Kian asks the Court to overturn Williams v. Florida and hold that the Sixth Amendment requires 12-person juries, after Florida convicted him using a six-person jury.

## What happened

Hamed Kian, a Florida chiropractor, was charged with five felony counts of practicing chiropractic medicine after his license was suspended. Under Florida law, non-capital felonies are tried before six-person juries rather than twelve. A six-person jury convicted Kian on all counts, and he was sentenced to prison followed by probation. On appeal, Kian argued that a six-person jury violated his Sixth and Fourteenth Amendment right to a jury trial, citing the Supreme Court's 1970 decision in Williams v. Florida, which upheld Florida's six-person juries, but arguing that a more recent decision, Ramos v. Louisiana, undermined that precedent. Florida's Fourth District Court of Appeal affirmed his conviction without a written opinion.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the right to a jury trial in serious criminal cases requires exactly twelve jurors, or whether a state may use a smaller jury, as Florida and several other states do. This requires deciding whether an earlier ruling allowing six-person juries remains good law, or whether a more recent decision about jury unanimity effectively undercut it such that the larger jury-size requirement should now be adopted.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; it was set for argument on January 13, 2027, and the case awaits decision following oral argument preparations and the Court's request for the record from the Florida appellate court.

## What each outcome would mean

Kian, the petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the Florida appellate court's decision upholding his conviction. If the Court affirms, Florida's use of six-person juries stands, and Kian's conviction remains in place. If the Court reverses, it would hold that the Sixth Amendment requires twelve-person juries in serious criminal cases, which would call into question Kian's conviction and similar convictions in Florida and several other states that use smaller juries, potentially requiring retrials. The United States, supporting Florida as amicus, argues reversal would also disrupt reliance interests in multiple states.
