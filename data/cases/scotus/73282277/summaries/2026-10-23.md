## In brief

Hamed Kian asks the Court to overturn its 1970 ruling that six-person juries satisfy the Constitution and to require 12-person juries in serious criminal cases.

## What happened

Florida charged Kian with five felony counts of practicing chiropractic medicine after his license was suspended. Under Florida law, a six-person jury tried non-capital crimes, and that jury convicted him on all counts. He appealed, arguing for the first time that the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments require a 12-person jury, relying on the Supreme Court's 2020 decision requiring unanimous verdicts. Florida's Fourth District Court of Appeal affirmed his conviction without a written opinion, citing binding Supreme Court precedent allowing six-person juries. Kian then asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's guarantee of a jury trial in serious criminal cases requires exactly 12 jurors, or whether a state may try such cases before a smaller jury, as Florida and several other states do.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, and the case has been fully briefed, including merits briefs from both sides and numerous outside groups, and an amicus brief from the United States supporting Florida. The Solicitor General has also asked to take part in oral argument. The case is awaiting argument and a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Kian, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to reverse that decision. If the Court affirms, Florida's six-person-jury system stands and Kian's conviction remains in place. If the Court reverses or vacates, Kian's conviction could be set aside, and states that currently allow juries smaller than 12 in serious criminal cases would need to change that practice, potentially affecting other pending cases. A reversal would overturn the Court's 1970 precedent allowing six-person juries; a vacatur would send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
