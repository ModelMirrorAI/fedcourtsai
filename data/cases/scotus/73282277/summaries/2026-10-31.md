## In brief

Hamed Kian asks the Court to overturn a 1970 ruling and hold that the Constitution requires a 12-person jury, not six, for serious criminal trials.

## What happened

Florida charged Kian, a chiropractor whose license had been suspended, with five felony counts of practicing chiropractic medicine without a valid license. Under Florida law, a six-person jury—rather than twelve—hears non-capital criminal cases. A six-person jury convicted Kian on all counts, and he was sentenced to over a year in prison followed by probation. Florida's Fourth District Court of Appeal affirmed his conviction without a written opinion, citing binding Supreme Court precedent allowing six-person juries. Kian argued that more recent Supreme Court decisions had undermined that precedent and that the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments require twelve jurors in serious cases.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether the Constitution's guarantee of trial by jury in serious criminal cases requires exactly twelve jurors, meaning states could no longer use smaller juries such as Florida's six-person panels for non-capital felonies.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed, including briefs from Kian, Florida, the federal government (supporting Florida), and numerous outside groups, plus oral argument participation requested by the Solicitor General. The case awaits a decision following oral argument.

## What each outcome would mean

Kian, who was convicted below, is asking the Court to overturn his conviction and the precedent allowing six-person juries. If the Court rules for Kian, it would hold that the Constitution requires twelve-person juries in serious criminal cases, which could affect Kian's conviction and potentially other pending cases in Florida and several other states that use smaller juries. If the Court rules for Florida, the use of six-person juries for non-capital felonies would remain constitutional, and Kian's conviction would stand.
