# Predicted Court Reasoning: *Hamed Kian v. Florida* (No. 25-6623)

## I. Judgment Class and Vote Lineup

- **Judgment**: **Reversed** (reversing the judgment of the Fourth District Court of Appeal of Florida and remanding for further proceedings not inconsistent with the opinion).
- **Vote Split**: **6–3**.
  - **Majority (6)**: Justices Gorsuch (author), Thomas, Sotomayor, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson.
  - **Dissent (3)**: Justice Alito (author), joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justice Kagan.

## II. Question Reached and Procedural Exit Analysis

The Court will reach the single question presented on the merits: whether the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments guarantee a right to trial by a 12-person jury when a defendant is charged with a serious felony. Neither a dismissal as improvidently granted (DIG) nor an affirmance by an equally divided Court is likely:
- **Jurisdiction and Preservation**: As Petitioner's merits brief details (Pet. Br. 8 n.1), the Florida Fourth District Court of Appeal's per curiam affirmance without written opinion (`421 So. 3d 439`) is unreviewable by the Florida Supreme Court under Fla. Const. art. V, § 3(b) (*Jenkins v. State*, 385 So. 2d 1356, 1359 (Fla. 1980)), rendering the Fourth DCA the highest state court in which a decision could be had under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a). Under settled Florida law, a constitutional challenge to a six-person jury may be raised on direct appeal without a contemporaneous trial objection, and Respondent did not assert any preservation bar in its Brief in Opposition or in its merits brief.
- **No Vehicle Defect**: Petitioner was convicted of five third-degree felony counts of practicing chiropractic medicine with a suspended license—each punishable by up to five years' imprisonment—by a six-person jury empaneled pursuant to Fla. Const. art. I, § 22 and Fla. Stat. § 913.10. The record cleanly and squarely presents the validity of *Williams v. Florida*, 399 U.S. 78 (1970).

## III. Doctrinal Ground (`majority-ground`)

The majority will overrule *Williams v. Florida*, 399 U.S. 78 (1970), holding that the Sixth Amendment's guarantee of "an impartial jury" in criminal prosecutions, as incorporated against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment, preserves the common-law requirement of a 12-person petit jury according to the provision's original public meaning at the Founding:

1. **Common-Law and Founding-Era Meaning**: Drawing on the same historical sources that controlled in *Ramos v. Louisiana*, 590 U.S. 83 (2020), and *Erlinger v. United States*, 602 U.S. 821, 831 (2024), the majority will trace the 12-member criminal trial jury from 14th-century English common law through Coke, Hale, Bacon, Duncombe, and Blackstone's canonical statement that the truth of every accusation must "be confirmed by the unanimous suffrage of twelve of his equals and neighbors" (4 Blackstone, *Commentaries* 343). It will pair those authorities with Founding-era dictionaries, ratification debates, early state court decisions invalidating statutes that authorized trials by fewer than 12 jurors (*Holmes v. Walton*; *Zylstra*; *Whitehurst*; *Work*; *Cancemi*), Story's and Cooley's treatises, and this Court's pre-1970 decisions (*Thompson v. Utah*, 170 U.S. 343 (1898); *Maxwell v. Dow*, 176 U.S. 581 (1900); *Rassmussen v. United States*, 197 U.S. 516 (1905); *Patton v. United States*, 281 U.S. 276 (1930)).
2. **Repudiation of *Williams*'s Functionalism and Drafting-History Inference**: Following *Ramos*, 590 U.S. at 97–102, the majority will hold that *Williams* committed two fundamental errors: first, treating the First Senate's unexplained conference deletion of Madison's "accustomed requisites" phrasing as an invitation to strip away settled attributes of the common-law jury; and second, substituting an ahistorical functionalist inquiry into whether six jurors perform "the function" of a jury well enough. Because *Ramos* held that the Sixth Amendment's jury-trial guarantee is fixed by original public meaning rather than judicial cost-benefit balancing—and is incorporated identically against the States under *Malloy v. Hogan* and *Duncan v. Louisiana*—*Williams*'s doctrinal foundation has been thoroughly eroded.
3. ***Stare Decisis* Analysis**: Applying the *Ramos* factors, the majority will conclude that *Williams*'s reasoning was egregiously wrong from the start, conflicted with prior precedent from *Thompson* to *Patton*, failed even on its own functionalist terms as recognized in *Ballew v. Georgia*, 435 U.S. 223, 232–39 (1978), and lies out of step with post-*Ramos* Sixth Amendment jurisprudence. On reliance, the majority will respond (as in *Ramos*) that only six states authorize fewer than 12 jurors in felony cases, that final convictions on collateral review remain undisturbed under *Edwards v. Vannoy*, 593 U.S. 255 (2021), and that state fiscal and administrative convenience in conducting felony trials cannot override an individual constitutional guarantee.

## IV. Breadth of the Holding (`ground-breadth`)

The majority will state a categorical rule that the Sixth and Fourteenth Amendments require a 12-person jury to be empaneled for any serious criminal (felony) prosecution, while explicitly cabining the decision's reach in three ways:
1. **Serious Offenses vs. Petty Offenses**: Consistent with *Baldwin v. New York*, 399 U.S. 66 (1970), and the petty-offense doctrine, the 12-person jury requirement attaches only where the Sixth Amendment jury right itself applies—serious criminal prosecutions—leaving summary or smaller-jury procedures for petty offenses untouched.
2. **Good-Cause Excusal During Deliberations (Fed. R. Crim. P. 23(b)(3))**: The majority (reinforced by a concurrence) will note that this case involves a state statute mandating a six-person jury from the outset of a felony trial, and does not resolve whether a court that properly empanels a 12-person jury may accept an 11-juror verdict when a juror is excused for good cause after deliberations have begun under Rule 23(b)(3) or analogous state rules.
3. **Civil Juries Under the Seventh Amendment**: Because the Seventh Amendment contains distinct text ("preserved" in suits at common law) and is not incorporated against the States, the Court will leave *Colgrove v. Battin*, 413 U.S. 149 (1973), and Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 48(a) outside the scope of the holding.

## V. Separate Writings

- **Justice Kavanaugh (concurring)**: Expected to write separately to apply his three-part *stare decisis* framework from *Ramos*, 590 U.S. at 115–33, emphasize the real-world deliberative and demographic harms of six-person felony juries documented in *Ballew*, note that *Edwards v. Vannoy*, 593 U.S. 255 (2021), limits the decision's retroactive impact to cases still on direct review, and underscore that neither Rule 23(b)(3) nor civil juries are invalidated.
- **Justice Thomas (concurring in part and in the judgment, or concurring)**: Expected to reiterate his view that the Sixth Amendment right to a 12-person jury applies against the States through the Fourteenth Amendment's Privileges or Immunities Clause and that *Williams* is "demonstrably erroneous" under *Gamble v. United States*, 587 U.S. 678, 710 (2019).
- **Justice Alito (dissenting, joined by Roberts, C.J., and Kagan, J.)**: Expected to dissent on *stare decisis* and historical grounds, arguing that *Williams*—unlike the 4-1-4 *Apodaca* decision—was a 7-1 majority opinion; that Article III and the Sixth Amendment omit a numeric floor while colonial and state practices showed flexibility; and that overruling *Williams* after 56 years inflicts severe reliance costs on Florida (~5,200 pending direct appeals and six-person courtroom jury boxes) and the other states that have structured their criminal and misdemeanor courts around *Williams*.
