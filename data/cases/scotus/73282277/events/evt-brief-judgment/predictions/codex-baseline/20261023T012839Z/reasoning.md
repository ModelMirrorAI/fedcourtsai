## Why modestly above the anchor

The petitioner presents a direct conflict between a numerical rule upheld in Williams and the historical understanding of the jury employed in Ramos. The merits brief's introduction and Argument I identify twelve members as a defining historical feature, rather than a modern procedural preference; Argument II asks the Court to displace Williams either through Ramos or through ordinary overruling analysis. That is a particularly intelligible originalist route to a pro-defendant outcome. I expect the Court to take the more institutionally explicit route of formally overruling the relevant holding after a stare decisis analysis.

Gorsuch's prior criticism of Williams, reproduced and discussed in the provisioned briefs, is stronger evidence about his inclination than about the other eight votes. The petitioner's reliance on Kavanaugh's Ramos framework supplies a plausible route for another vote. The historical argument can also appeal to Barrett and Thomas, while the jury's protection against government power and its representative function offer a route for Sotomayor and Jackson. Those are forecasts of judicial choices, not independently observed commitments in this case.

The specific facts do not make the jury entitlement marginal: the briefs agree that Kian faced five felony counts carrying potential imprisonment well above six months and was convicted by six jurors. The question is the tribunal's lawful constitution, not whether the underlying allegations are sympathetic. I did not use Florida's allegations concerning conduct underlying the licensing suspension as proof about the strength of the jury-size claim.

## Why not a much higher number

Florida's merits brief, especially its summary and Argument II, makes substantial arguments the petitioner cannot answer merely by invoking Ramos. Williams had an actual majority, its numerical floor is workable, and its defenders can distinguish the meaning of unanimity from the choice of panel size. The State also disputes that founding-era practice constitutionalized every common-law jury attribute. Its account of the reach into misdemeanor trials, courtroom arrangements, civil juries, and eleven-person federal verdicts makes reliance materially more serious than an abstract objection to retrials. The petitioner's historical and empirical submissions are advocacy, not findings I can assume the Court will accept. I give Florida's response meaningful weight.

The most important vehicle risk comes from **preservation and waiver**. The BIO's statement, at printed page 2, says the defense accepted the jury without objection and that Kian personally accepted the selected panel. The appendix, at its printed pages 5–6, argues instead that a facial constitutional challenge could be raised initially on appeal, that the error was fundamental and structural, and that no valid personal waiver occurred. Those are competing positions, not a settled record of preservation. The lower court's unexplained affirmance does not establish which ground it adopted. Although the merits briefs predominantly join issue on Williams, I retain probability for a procedural affirmance or DIG, and for a vacatur-and-remand rather than my selected reversal label. My forecast does not assume that a constitutional holding automatically eliminates ordinary procedural defenses.

The **0.26 undisturbed complement includes both affirmance and a possible DIG**; I do not condition the 0.74 on a merits opinion issuing. The exact six-vote coalition is less secure than the binary call. In particular, Barrett may find the reliance defense persuasive, Kagan may conclude that following Ramos now favors extension rather than retention, and Thomas may join more of the opinion than my concurrence-in-judgment forecast assumes. I choose Roberts, Alito, and Kagan as the dissent because their institutional defense of precedent supplies a coherent position here, but the vote list is an elicited forecast, not a report of case-specific voting evidence.

## Stakes and retrieval limits

The **0.82 significance score** reflects the scope of the criminal-jury right and the practical stakes for states using smaller panels, pending defendants, and trial administration. It is not an estimate of success, does not rest on postdecision publicity, and does not imply that all existing convictions would reopen.

Attempts to obtain general preexisting legal context did not add evidence: web searches returned no usable content; a CourtListener search returned no results, and the next search was throttled with HTTP 429. I stopped live retrieval, used the committed base-rate tooling and provisioned briefs, and made no direct REST request. The unavailable independent opinions limit verification of the parties' characterizations of precedent. All attempted external queries are recorded in `retrieval.md`.
