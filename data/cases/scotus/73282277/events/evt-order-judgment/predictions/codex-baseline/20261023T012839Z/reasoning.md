## Why P(disturbed) = 0.72

Kian received five convictions for practicing chiropractic medicine with a suspended license after a six-person jury trial. The petition's statement and the appendix report concurrent prison terms followed by probation; the seriousness of these felony prosecutions is not the difficult part of the question. The Fourth District affirmed without a reasoned opinion. The legal obstacle is Williams v. Florida, 399 U.S. 78 (1970), which the petition expressly asks the Court to overrule.

The strongest merits argument is historical and doctrinal, rather than empirical. The petition, pp. 11-16, contrasts the traditional understanding of twelve jurors with Williams's functional approach and invokes Ramos v. Louisiana, 590 U.S. 83 (2020), for interpreting the jury guarantee through its historical meaning. That creates a plausible cross-ideological coalition: originalist Justices and Justices protective of criminal jury rights need not share broader politics to reject six-person felony juries. The petition's discussion of Justice Gorsuch's earlier criticism of Williams supplies a concrete pre-grant indication of that position. The grant establishes that this question is being heard; it does not establish five votes to overrule precedent.

Florida's opposition, pp. 6-13 and 18-19, supplies substantial counterweight. It argues that Williams itself examined history, that unanimity and jury size are distinguishable, and that Ramos overruled an unusually fractured precedent rather than a settled majority rule. Longstanding state reliance is real as an argument even though the brief's numerical estimates are advocacy, not independently verified counts. I do not adopt its assertion that overruling a Sixth Amendment criminal-jury decision necessarily jeopardizes federal civil verdicts: the Seventh Amendment question can be reserved. The conflicting empirical studies and state conviction-rate comparisons are not decisive evidence of the constitutional minimum; cross-state raw rates do not isolate the causal effect of jury size.

The principal case-specific discount concerns preservation and remedy. Florida states that counsel made no trial objection and Kian personally accepted the selected panel (BIO pp. 1-2). The appendix, pp. 5a-6a, argues that the facial constitutional claim and structural/fundamental error were reviewable without a trial objection, and that Kian never knowingly waived a twelve-person-jury right. Those are contested legal positions; the summary affirmance does not tell us which ground the state court adopted. Acceptance of the selected jurors is not obviously an informed waiver of a different constitutional jury size, but a preservation ruling remains a meaningful route to no relief.

Florida separately argues harmlessness based on the strength of its trial proof (BIO pp. 19-22). I expect the Court to resist treating the votes of six jurors who never sat as a foregone conclusion, but the precise structural-error and forfeiture questions are less secure than the constitutional argument. A rule favoring Kian could produce vacatur and a remand for further procedural consideration rather than the outright reversal I select.

My 0.72 is only modestly above the 0.6977 committed anchor: a substantial chance of overruling Williams is offset by stare decisis, possible forfeiture, and a procedural exit. My subjective allocation is reversed 0.43, vacated 0.27, affirmed-in-part-reversed-in-part 0.02, affirmed 0.20, dismissed-as-improvidently-granted 0.075, and affirmed-by-an-equally-divided-court 0.005. These sum to one and put exactly 0.72 on disturbed outcomes. Reversal is the most likely individual label, not a 72% exact-label prediction. No recusal is established; the tiny equally-divided allowance is contingency rather than a forecast of a known absence.
