## In brief

Property owners asked the Court to order a federal judge to keep hearing their flooding lawsuit against Texas instead of sending it to state court; the Court denied the request.

## What happened

Richard Devillier and other landowners sued Texas, claiming a highway barrier flooded their property during storms, violating the Takings Clause. After years of litigation, the Supreme Court in 2024 avoided deciding whether the Takings Clause itself lets property owners sue, because Texas said its own state law let them pursue the same claims, and sent the case back for further proceedings. On remand, a federal magistrate judge recommended, and the district judge ordered, that the case be sent back to the Texas state courts it had originally been removed from, concluding federal courts lacked jurisdiction. The property owners then asked the Supreme Court directly to intervene.

## What the Court is being asked

The property owners asked the Court to use its power to enforce its own prior orders, arguing the trial judge misread the Court's 2024 decision by sending their case to state court instead of letting them continue pursuing their claims in federal court. They argued only the Supreme Court itself could fix this, since normal appeals of such a transfer are not allowed.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and was denied on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Devillier and the other property owners asked the Court to step in. Because the Court denied the request, the district court's order sending their case to Texas state courts stands, meaning their claims will proceed there rather than in federal court. Had the Court instead granted the request, it would have directed the district judge to keep the case in federal court. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the district court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling in place.
