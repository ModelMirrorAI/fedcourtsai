## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a retiree's challenge to how his pension plan calculated his court-ordered benefit share after his divorce.

## What happened

David Gasper's 2013 divorce produced a court order dividing his pension, which the plan approved. When Gasper sought retirement benefits in 2019, the plan administrator required him to choose among benefit options that, he says, changed the payment structure the divorce order had set, reducing his share and shifting costs. Gasper appealed within the plan and received three denial letters, which he says gave shifting and inadequate reasons. He then sued in federal district court, which ruled against him, and the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that ruling in December 2025.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a court may uphold a benefit-plan administrator's denial of benefits by relying on reasons or plan provisions the administrator itself never gave during the internal appeal process, rather than only the reasons it actually stated. The Court is also asked whether a plan administrator may replace a retiree's already-finalized, court-ordered pension division with a different benefit form that the plan documents do not authorize.

## Where it stands

Gasper filed his petition in March 2026. The respondents waived their right to respond, and after being distributed for conferences in June and September 2026, the Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Gasper, the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case and overturn the Fourth Circuit's ruling for EIDP (DuPont) and the other plan-related respondents. Because the Court denied the petition, it will not hear the case, and the Fourth Circuit's decision upholding the benefit reduction and the lower courts' handling of his claims remains in place. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower courts' reasoning; it simply leaves that outcome standing, with no further review by the Supreme Court.
