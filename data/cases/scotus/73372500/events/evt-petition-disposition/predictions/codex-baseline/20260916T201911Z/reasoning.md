## Why below that anchor

The petition's strongest point is a concrete asserted disagreement over substantive due process in executive land-use administration. It contrasts the Second Circuit's treatment of unauthorized action and fundamental procedural irregularity with more demanding conscience-shocking approaches and the Eleventh Circuit's restriction on executive deprivations of state-created rights. That is more substantial than a bare request to correct a local zoning error. However, these are the petitioner's descriptions of the circuit authorities, not holdings I independently verified in each underlying opinion. Even the petition acknowledges qualifications in the Second Circuit approach (printed pages 11–12), so I do not assume identical facts necessarily produce opposite results.

The vehicle sharply reduces the probability of plenary review. The decision presented for review is an unpublished order declining original jurisdiction, not an opinion selecting a federal constitutional standard. The petition itself recounts objections based on duplicative proceedings, the availability of lower-court remedies, discretionary rather than ministerial official duties, and the limits of prohibition (pages 8–9). Those create substantial independent-state-ground and suitability concerns without requiring any conclusion that the federal theory loses on its merits.

Preservation is also uncertain: the petition acknowledges that the federal issue was not framed below in the refined terms used at the cert stage (page 21). It argues that references to arbitrary and irrational zoning action and Nectow adequately presented it. Without the full state-court filings or a reasoned decision, I cannot resolve that dispute in petitioner's favor. Nor is a neighborhood association challenging permits issued to another entity obviously interchangeable with the permit-holder disputes featured in the asserted split. The protected interest and alleged deprivation would require further examination. Finally, the county disputes the premise that the zoning board's ruling legally barred continued permitting (pages 8 and 18). A state-law predicate remains contested.

The absence of a requested response in the visible record, following waiver, offers no affirmative sign that the Court has engaged with those issues. I therefore lower the roughly 5.12% prior-band anchor to 2.5%, while preserving meaningful nonzero mass for narrow summary relief.

## Alternative route and ancillary claims
