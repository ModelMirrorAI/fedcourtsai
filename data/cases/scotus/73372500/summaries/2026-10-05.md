## In brief

A nonprofit group's petition challenging South Carolina's refusal to hear its case over disputed solar-facility zoning permits was denied by the Court.

## What happened

A York County zoning board ruled that a company's proposed solar-panel manufacturing use was not allowed under local zoning rules. The petitioner, Citizens Alliance for Government Integrity, says county officials then let the company get building permits anyway, without any rezoning or other lawful approval. Citizens Alliance asked the South Carolina Supreme Court directly for an order blocking further permits. That court declined, in an unexplained one-page order, to take up the matter under its original jurisdiction. Citizens Alliance then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that decision, after receiving an extension of time to file.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, and under what circumstances, local officials who issue land-use permits after a zoning board has ruled the proposed use prohibited can be found to have acted in a way that is arbitrary enough to violate constitutional protections against irrational government action. The petition says federal appeals courts disagree sharply on how to treat such permitting: some treat it as potentially unconstitutional, others require far more extreme misconduct, and still others bar this kind of claim entirely.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the county and officials waived their right to respond. After a supplemental brief from Citizens Alliance was filed, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Citizens Alliance asked the Court to take up the case. With the petition denied, the South Carolina Supreme Court's order declining to hear the original dispute stands, meaning Citizens Alliance's request for an order blocking the county's permits was not revived through this avenue. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the South Carolina court's decision or resolved the underlying disagreement among federal appeals courts; it simply leaves that lower court's order in place.
