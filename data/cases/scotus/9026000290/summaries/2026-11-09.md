## In brief

The Court declined to hear a San Francisco property owner's bid to revive her lawsuit challenging the city's code-enforcement searches of her property.

## What happened

Mihai Emberton, representing herself, sued the City and County of San Francisco over code-enforcement actions involving a fence and other structures on her property, arguing that inspections and violation notices amounted to unconstitutional searches and seizures. A California trial court sustained the city's demurrer (a motion arguing the complaint fails to state a valid claim) against her complaint. The California Court of Appeal denied her petition challenging that result, noting it also appeared to exceed court word-count rules. The California Supreme Court denied review. Emberton then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks the Court to clarify when a government inspection of property counts as a search requiring constitutional safeguards. It asks whether that question turns on whether the property was visible to the inspector without trespassing, or instead on whether the inspection deprived the owner of the right to keep others out, regardless of visibility or where the inspection happened.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026. On November 9, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Emberton, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up her case after losing in the California courts. The denial means the Court will not review the case, so the California Court of Appeal's rejection of her challenge, and the underlying trial court ruling favoring the city, remain in place. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the lower courts' reasoning, only that it chose not to hear the case further.
