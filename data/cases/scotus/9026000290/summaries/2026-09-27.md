## In brief

A San Francisco property owner asks the Court to hear her claim that city code-enforcement inspections of her property amounted to unconstitutional searches.

## What happened

Mihai Emberton owns property in San Francisco that was the subject of city code-enforcement actions after a neighbor's complaint. She sued the city, arguing that inspections of her property by building, planning, and public works officials violated her constitutional rights against unreasonable searches, along with related due-process claims. A trial court sustained the city's demurrer (a motion arguing the complaint fails to state a valid claim) without leave to amend, ending her case. The California Court of Appeal denied her petition challenging that outcome, and the California Supreme Court denied review. Emberton, representing herself, now petitions the U.S. Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to clarify how courts should decide whether a government inspection counts as a search requiring constitutional protection: whether that determination turns on whether the area inspected was visible to the public and on what kind of property or information was examined, or whether a search occurs regardless of those factors whenever someone's right to keep others out is invaded.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and docketed, with the response due October 5, 2026. The docket shows no further action since filing.

## What each outcome would mean

Emberton, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up her case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the search question itself, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the lower courts' rulings against Emberton stand, and her case ends with the city's earlier win in place. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower courts were correct; it simply leaves their decisions undisturbed.
