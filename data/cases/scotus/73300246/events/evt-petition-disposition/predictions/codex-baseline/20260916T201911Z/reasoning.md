## Why below the anchor

**A real federal theory, not a nonexistent issue.** The petition argues that state-law labels cannot erase constitutionally protected access and that a taking arose only when replacement access ceased, not during the original highway construction. Its reliance on longstanding permitted access and a state-approved billboard permit makes the claimed practical deprivation more substantial than a naked assertion of convenience (petition pp. 3–4, 7–12). Tyler v. Hennepin County, 598 U.S. 631, 638 (2023), supports looking beyond state law to traditional property interests; I verified that limited proposition through CourtListener. It prevents treating the opposition's characterization of the controversy as exclusively state law as conclusive. Tyler does not itself establish a compensable easement or complete loss of access here.

**The alleged conflict is not demonstrably clean.** The petition invokes decisions involving denial of all feasible access or rights of public-road abutters (petition pp. 8–10). Reno distinguishes those situations from permissive use across state-owned land where the parcel still abuts Huffaker Place (opposition pp. 7–12). These differences could explain the results without a conflict over the same federal rule. I have not independently verified every cited access case and therefore treat the asserted conflict as advocacy rather than an established split.

**The underlying opinion creates concrete vehicle obstacles.** The Nevada affirmance rejects an easement by necessity for lack of the required common ownership, rejects prescription against state property, and finds the allegations insufficient for an implied easement (appendix A, pp. 7a–9a). Its inverse-condemnation discussion separately rejects the access claim for lack of an interest and the alleged excess 1987 taking for insufficient pleading and limitations (pp. 9a–10a). I do not simply adopt Reno's broader assertion that every takings theory is time-barred: the recent-sale theory and the historic-condemnation theory are distinct. Even with that distinction, the opinion does not supply a straightforward holding that government may eliminate an established access right without compensation.

**Preservation further weakens the statutory entitlement route.** The affirmance's footnote at pp. 10a–11a says the NRS 408.533 argument was not raised below and was waived. The petition relies on that provision as a source of entitlement, while Reno emphasizes its discretionary wording (petition p. 7; opposition pp. 13–14). The express waiver is an additional obstacle to using this case to settle the federal significance of those statutory protections; I do not assume that a rehearing request cured it.

**Factual disputes matter to selection.** The petitioner describes a severed northwest portion separated by Dry Creek; Reno says access remains through the parcel and the cul-de-sac. Reno also disputes the petition's ownership history and distinguishes 2020 authorization from the 2021 conveyance (opposition pp. 1–3, 6–7). I do not resolve those disputes on advocacy alone. Their relevance is that the question's premise—elimination of the only feasible access—is contested, making this a less attractive vehicle for a national per se rule.

Together these concerns reduce the approximately 5.12% anchor to 2.5%. The nonzero probability recognizes the potential significance of an access-based takings claim and the possibility that the Court regards the state-law framing as evasion of a federal guarantee. There is no quantified model behind this adjustment; it is a judgment about vehicle quality from the supplied pre-decision record.
