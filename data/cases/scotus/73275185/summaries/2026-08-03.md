## In brief

Alabama asked the Court to review a ruling that its 2023 congressional map diluted Black voters' strength, and the Court vacated that ruling and sent the case back for reconsideration.

## What happened

After earlier litigation found Alabama's 2021 congressional map likely violated the Voting Rights Act by splitting a Black Belt region of the state, Alabama enacted a new 2023 map. Voters challenging the map, led by Marcus Caster, argued it still diluted Black voters' influence by keeping a different region, the Gulf Coast, undivided instead of creating a second district where Black voters could elect their preferred candidates. A federal district court agreed, permanently blocking the 2023 map and separately finding Alabama had intentionally discriminated. Alabama appealed and asked the Supreme Court to take the case directly, before the appeals court ruled.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether voting-rights law required Alabama to draw district lines based on race to create a second district where Black voters form a majority, whether doing so would itself violate the Constitution, and whether the challengers could sue to enforce this part of the voting-rights law at all. Alabama also argued the lower court wrongly treated its refusal to use race as proof of discriminatory intent.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted Alabama's petition, set aside the district court's judgment, and sent the case back to the court of appeals with instructions to return it to the district court for further review in light of the Court's recent decision in a related Louisiana redistricting case. Three justices dissented. An emergency request Alabama had filed for a stay was denied as no longer necessary once the Court acted.

## What each outcome would mean

Alabama, as the party that petitioned, sought to use its 2023 map rather than one with two Black-majority districts. The Court's action means neither side's position was finally decided: the district court's ruling against Alabama is wiped away, and the case returns for a fresh look under the newer legal standard from the Louisiana case, leaving open whether the 2023 map will ultimately be upheld or rejected. The dissenting justices would have left the lower court's ruling against Alabama in place.
