## In brief

The Court vacated a ruling that Alabama's congressional map diluted Black voters' votes and sent the case back for reconsideration in light of a newer decision.

## What happened

Black voters sued Alabama, arguing its 2023 congressional map illegally diluted their voting strength by keeping only one of seven districts where they could elect their preferred candidates. After a trial, a federal district court permanently blocked the map, finding it violated the Voting Rights Act and was enacted with discriminatory intent. Alabama appealed to the Eleventh Circuit and simultaneously asked the Supreme Court to take the case immediately, before the appeals court ruled, arguing the map was lawful and that the relevant voting-rights standard itself was unconstitutional.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether Alabama's map illegally weakened Black voters' influence under the law barring racial discrimination in voting, and separately whether that law, as the lower court applied it, is constitutional at all.

## Where it stands

The Court granted Alabama's petition, set aside the district court's judgment, and sent the case back to the Eleventh Circuit with instructions to return it to the district court for further review in light of a recently decided related case, Louisiana v. Callais. Three justices dissented. A related emergency stay application was denied as no longer necessary.

## What each outcome would mean

Alabama, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up the case and overturn the ruling against its map. The Court's action wipes out the district court's decision that the map was unlawful, without deciding for either side whether the map actually violated the law. The case returns to the lower courts, which must now reconsider the dispute taking the Callais decision into account, leaving open whether Alabama's map will ultimately be upheld or struck down.
