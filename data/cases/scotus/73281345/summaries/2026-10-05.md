## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear Holly Elkins's challenge to her cyberstalking conviction, which argued that using a phone alone cannot justify federal jurisdiction.

## What happened

Holly Elkins was convicted of conspiring to stalk and cyberstalking her fiancé's ex-girlfriend, Alyssa Burkett, who was later murdered by Elkins's fiancé. Elkins argued the federal cyberstalking law could not constitutionally apply to her because her only connection to interstate commerce was intrastate phone use. The district court and the Fifth Circuit rejected that argument, treating phones as automatically within Congress's power to regulate, though the appeals court separately threw out a related firearm conviction on other grounds. Elkins asked the Supreme Court to review the Commerce Clause question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether Congress may rely on someone's use of a telephone, by itself, to support a federal cyberstalking charge, or whether prosecutors must also show that the phone use actually affected commerce between states. Elkins pointed to a Tenth Circuit decision treating vehicles differently, arguing some courts require proof of an actual commercial connection while the Fifth Circuit treats phone use as automatically sufficient.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, received full briefing including a response from the government and a reply from Elkins, and the Supreme Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Elkins, the petitioner, asked the Court to take up her case and overturn the Fifth Circuit's ruling. The denial means the Court will not review the question, leaving the Fifth Circuit's decision in place: Elkins's convictions for conspiracy and cyberstalking stand, upheld on the view that intrastate phone use alone satisfies the Commerce Clause. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with that approach, only that it chose not to decide the issue now.
