## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear Holly Elkins's challenge to her cyberstalking conviction, which argued Congress lacked power to criminalize her phone use under the Commerce Clause.

## What happened

Holly Elkins was convicted of conspiring to cyberstalk and of cyberstalking resulting in the death of Alyssa Burkett, the former girlfriend of Elkins's fiancé, who was later murdered by the fiancé. Elkins argued that the federal cyberstalking law could not constitutionally apply to her because her phone use was only local, not interstate. The trial court and the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals rejected this argument, following circuit precedent that phone use, even within one state, counts as use of an "instrumentality" of interstate commerce that Congress may regulate. The appeals court also threw out a related firearm conviction on other grounds but left her cyberstalking and conspiracy convictions, and her life sentence, in place.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether Congress may rely on someone's use of a phone, by itself, as enough of a connection to interstate commerce to justify federal criminal jurisdiction, or whether prosecutors must also show that the specific conduct actually affected interstate commerce.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond but was later asked by the Court to respond, and did so; after further briefing, the petition was considered at a conference and the Court denied it.

## What each outcome would mean

Elkins, the petitioner, asked the Court to take up her case. Because the Court denied the petition, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands: Elkins's convictions and life sentence for cyberstalking and conspiracy remain in place, and the circuit's rule that phone use alone can support federal jurisdiction over cyberstalking continues to apply there. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fifth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed without deciding the underlying constitutional question.
