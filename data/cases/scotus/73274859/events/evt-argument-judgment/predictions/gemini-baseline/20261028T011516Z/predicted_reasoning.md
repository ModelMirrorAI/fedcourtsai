# Predicted Reasoning — *Suncor Energy (U.S.A.) Inc. v. County Commissioners of Boulder County* (No. 25-170)

## 1. Judgment Class and Vote Lineup

- **Predicted Judgment:** `reversed` (P(disturbed) = **0.83**). The Court will reverse the judgment of the Colorado Supreme Court (with a secondary possibility of vacatur and remand if the Court leaves open whether any narrow, purely in-state or source-state claim could be repleaded).
- **Predicted Vote Lineup:** **6–2** (with Justice Alito recused).
  - **Majority (6):** Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett.
  - **Dissent (2):** Justices Sotomayor and Jackson.
- **Procedural Exit Assessment:** Neither a dismissal of the writ as improvidently granted (DIG) nor an affirmance by an equally divided 4–4 Court is likely. Although Justice Alito recused prior to argument, six participating Justices (Roberts, Thomas, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett) signaled on the merits that a single affected state cannot apply its own tort law to impose damages liability for injuries caused by global greenhouse-gas emissions, and at least five (and likely all six) will find jurisdiction satisfied under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a) and Article III.

## 2. Threshold Question: Statutory and Article III Jurisdiction

The Court will reach both the threshold jurisdictional question added in the grant order and the merits question presented by the petition, holding that it possesses both Article III and statutory jurisdiction:

1. **Article III Standing:** Under *ASARCO Inc. v. Kadish*, 490 U.S. 605 (1989), and *Atlantic Richfield Co. v. Christian*, 590 U.S. 1 (2020), Petitioners have a concrete personal stake because the Colorado Supreme Court entered a binding judgment in an original proceeding that conclusively rejected their federal preemption defense, stripping them of their asserted federal immunity from state regulation and forcing them to undergo burdensome merits litigation in state court.
2. **Finality Under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a):** The Court will hold that the Colorado Supreme Court's decision discharging its order to show cause under Colorado Appellate Rule 21 is a "final judgment" reviewable under § 1257(a) on two reinforcing grounds:
   - First, under *Atlantic Richfield*, a proceeding under C.A.R. 21 invokes the Colorado Supreme Court's original jurisdiction under Article VI, Section 3 of the Colorado Constitution rather than ordinary appellate review, making the ruling culminating that discrete proceeding final for purposes of § 1257(a).
   - Second, and independently, the decision satisfies the fourth category of *Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469 (1975): the Colorado Supreme Court definitively decided the federal preemption issue; reversal on that federal ground will terminate the action as pleaded (since Respondents asserted only Colorado-law tort claims seeking climate-change damages and dropped their standalone statutory consumer-protection count); Petitioners might otherwise prevail on nonfederal grounds after years of discovery and trial, insulating the federal issue from review; and allowing dozens of coordinated municipal lawsuits to proceed threatens a serious erosion of federal energy and environmental policy.

## 3. Merits Ground (`majority-ground`) and Breadth (`ground-breadth`)

### Doctrinal Basis (`majority-ground`)
The Court will hold that the structure of the Constitution and the Clean Air Act, under *American Electric Power Co. v. Connecticut*, 564 U.S. 410 (2011) (*AEP*), and *International Paper Co. v. Ouellette*, 479 U.S. 481 (1987), preclude applying affected-state (Colorado) common law to impose liability for injuries allegedly caused by interstate and global greenhouse-gas emissions, after first holding that the Colorado Supreme Court's Rule 21 original-proceeding ruling is reviewable under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a) pursuant to *Atlantic Richfield* and *Cox*.

Specifically, the majority will bridge the structural federalism and statutory preemption arguments along the lines developed at oral argument by Justices Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Gorsuch and Chief Justice Roberts:
- In *Milwaukee I*, *Milwaukee II*, and *AEP*, the Court established that under the constitutional scheme of equal state sovereignty, interstate and ambient air pollution cannot be governed by the law of a particular affected state ("borrowing the law of a particular state would be inappropriate," *AEP*, 564 U.S. at 422).
- When Congress displaced federal common law by enacting the Clean Air Act, that displacement did not resurrect affected-state common-law authority that the Constitution's structure rendered incompetent in the first place. Instead, as *Ouellette* held in the parallel Clean Water Act context, the federal regulatory scheme preempts any effort by an affected state to apply its own tort law to out-of-state pollution sources, preserving at most traditional source-state law for localized pollution.
- Because greenhouse gases mix uniformly in the global atmosphere—making climate change a "50-state" and international phenomenon rather than a bilateral border dispute—and because Respondents pleaded their claims exclusively under Colorado law (as the affected state) to recover damages for the global production, promotion, and sale of fossil fuels, their claims are preempted.
- Furthermore, framing the claims as targeting upstream fossil-fuel production, promotion, and alleged deception about climate risks does not save them: every asserted injury in Respondents' nuisance, trespass, unjust-enrichment, and conspiracy counts is causally mediated through global greenhouse-gas emissions, and imposing billions of dollars in damages on upstream fossil-fuel sales regulates a stage of the causal chain that Congress in the Clean Air Act chose not to subject to fifty conflicting state tort regimes.

### Breadth of the Holding (`ground-breadth`)
The Court will adopt a categorical rule barring affected-state common-law tort claims that seek damages for injuries mediated through interstate and global greenhouse-gas emissions—regardless of whether plaintiffs frame those claims as targeting upstream fossil-fuel production, promotion, and deceptive marketing rather than direct point-source emissions—while leaving undisturbed *Ouellette*'s preservation of traditional source-state claims for localized pollution. By anchoring the holding jointly in *AEP*/*Ouellette* and the Clean Air Act's structure rather than relying solely on untethered extraterritoriality or foreign-affairs field preemption, the Court will avoid disturbing general state product-liability law under *National Pork Producers Council v. Ross*, 598 U.S. 356 (2023), while definitively foreclosing the nationwide wave of state-law climate-change tort suits.

## 4. Opinion Authorship and Separate Writings

- **Majority Opinion Author:** **Chief Justice Roberts** (0.220) or **Justice Kavanaugh** (0.210) is the likeliest author of the opinion of the Court, followed by **Justice Barrett** (0.165), **Justice Gorsuch** (0.135), and **Justice Kagan** (0.115). Chief Justice Roberts authored *Atlantic Richfield* and frequently retains marquee federalism and environmental decisions in the October sitting; Justice Kavanaugh laid out the comprehensive *AEP*/*Ouellette*/CAA synthesis at oral argument that unites the six-Justice majority.
- **Separate Writings:**
  - **Justice Jackson** (0.55) and **Justice Sotomayor** (0.52) are very likely to write in dissent—either jointly or in twin dissents dividing the threshold § 1257(a) finality objection (championed by Justice Jackson at argument) and the merits preemption/state-remedy defense (emphasized by Justice Sotomayor).
  - **Justice Thomas** (0.38) and **Justice Gorsuch** (0.34) have elevated probabilities of concurring separately to address the precise relationship between statutory preemption under the Clean Air Act, constitutional structure, and § 1257(a) finality.
  - **Justice Kagan** (0.22) or **Justice Barrett** (0.18) may write a brief concurrence if the majority opinion reaches beyond the *AEP*/*Ouellette*/Clean Air Act synthesis into broader constitutional extraterritoriality or foreign-affairs preclusion.
