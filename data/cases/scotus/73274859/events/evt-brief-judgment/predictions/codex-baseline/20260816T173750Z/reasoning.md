# Rationale

I assign a 0.57 probability that the judgment below is disturbed and predict a reversal. This is a forward-mode merits prediction from the respondent-brief moment. I used the as-stored August 16, 2026 snapshot, the petition and questions presented, the provisioned composite opposition/respondent brief, both parties' principal merits briefs, and the United States' merits amicus brief obtained from Supreme Court docket links already present in the snapshot. The provisioned composite was marked truncated and stopped during the respondent's merits argument; retrieving the complete respondent brief supplied the omitted Clean Air Act section.

The committed merits anchor points toward disturbance but is not decisive. The case was granted in OT2025, so the prescribed ten-Term window is 2015-2024. The statpack renders no rows for 2015 or 2016 because those Terms have no parsed judgments. Its rendered rows for 2017-2024 contain 359 disturbed judgments among 515 parsed judgments, a pooled rate of 69.7%, comfortably above the 30-judgment floor. The required numeric exclusion column is present: those rows report 57 exclusions, and the pack reports 67 overall, reflecting the guard that removes judgments entered on or before their own grants rather than treating cert-order dispositions as merits judgments. The rendered rows report 515 parsed among 557 grants, but that is not full-window coverage because the omitted Terms have no published grant denominators. The nearest Terms are also the most exposed to pendency and quick-disposition selection.

I adjust below 69.7% because the Court itself added statutory and Article III jurisdiction to the grant, and respondents have substantial arguments that the Colorado Rule 21 ruling was an interlocutory refusal to dismiss rather than a reviewable final judgment. On the merits, *Hencely*'s rejection of preemption in the absence of constitutional text or a federal statute, *AEP*'s direction to examine statutory preemption after displacement, and *Ouellette*'s preservation of source-state law all weaken petitioners' categorical structural theory. The question presented also does not identify which State's law applies, and claim-specific issues concerning deception, causation, and in-state conduct remain.

I adjust back upward because the Court granted despite those objections, the United States supports jurisdiction and preclusion on the merits, and the Clean Air Act supplies a narrower statutory route for a conservative majority. The causal theory alleged in the petition ties the requested damages to worldwide production, interstate and foreign emissions, global climate alteration, and local harms. A majority can therefore reverse without embracing a rule that all multistate tort effects are constitutionally beyond state law.

The 6-3 lineup follows the current Court's broad ideological divide on federal limits affecting nationwide climate litigation. The largest vote uncertainty is whether Justices Gorsuch or Barrett prefer the respondents' narrower federal-common-law account or a procedural disposition. The current docket does not record a recusal at the grant, so I predict Justice Alito participates despite his recusal from an earlier related petition. I omit writing-role predictions because the present record does not make authorship materially forecastable.

The 0.94 big-case score reflects the nationwide stakes rather than the outcome probability: the briefs describe dozens of climate-damages suits, potentially enormous liability, and a ruling that could materially define the boundary between state tort authority and national emissions policy.
