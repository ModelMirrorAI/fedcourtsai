## In brief

Energy companies ask the Supreme Court to decide whether federal law bars Boulder's state-law lawsuit seeking damages for harms linked to climate change.

## What happened

Boulder County and the City of Boulder sued Suncor and Exxon Mobil in Colorado state court, claiming the companies' production, marketing, and sale of fossil fuels contributed to climate change and caused local harms like wildfires and droughts. Boulder seeks damages, not an order to stop emissions. The energy companies argued federal law bars these claims entirely. Colorado's trial court and, in a divided ruling, the Colorado Supreme Court disagreed, allowing the suit to proceed. Two justices dissented, arguing federal law should block the claims and urging the U.S. Supreme Court to weigh in.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court must decide whether the Constitution's structure, or the federal Clean Air Act, prevents a state from using its own tort law to award damages for harms linked to greenhouse gas emissions that occur across state and international lines. A related question is whether the Court even has authority to review this case at this stage, since the Colorado Supreme Court's ruling did not end the lawsuit but sent it back for further proceedings.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review and directed the parties to also brief whether it has jurisdiction to hear the case now. The case was argued on October 5, 2026, with the United States participating as amicus curiae in support of the energy companies, and it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Suncor and Exxon Mobil are asking the Court to reverse the Colorado Supreme Court's decision, which allowed Boulder's lawsuit to continue. If the Court reverses, Boulder's claims would be barred, ending this lawsuit and likely affecting dozens of similar suits by other state and local governments nationwide. If the Court affirms, Boulder's case would continue in Colorado courts, though further defenses the energy companies have raised would still need to be resolved. If the Court instead finds it lacks jurisdiction to decide the issue now, the case would return to Colorado courts without a ruling on the merits.
