## In brief

Energy companies ask the Supreme Court to decide whether federal law bars local governments from suing fossil-fuel producers under state tort law for damages tied to global climate change.

## What happened

Boulder County and the City of Boulder sued Suncor and Exxon Mobil in Colorado state court, claiming the companies' production, marketing, and sale of fossil fuels contributed to climate change and caused local harms like wildfires, flooding, and drought, while allegedly concealing known risks. They sought damages, not an injunction on emissions. The trial court let the claims proceed, rejecting arguments that federal law barred them. The Colorado Supreme Court agreed, holding that the Clean Air Act displaced any federal common law that might have blocked the suit and that the Act itself did not preempt the state claims. Two justices dissented, warning of a patchwork of local climate rulings nationwide.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's structure and federal clean-air law prevent a state's courts from awarding damages for harms traced to greenhouse gas emissions that come from many states and countries, or whether states retain power to apply their own tort law to such claims. The Court also asked the parties to address whether it has authority to hear this case at all, given questions about whether the decision below counts as a final judgment and whether the companies suffered the kind of injury needed to bring the appeal.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the companies' petition and directed briefing on both the preemption question and the Court's own jurisdiction. The case has been fully briefed and argued, with argument held on October 5, 2026, and it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Suncor and Exxon Mobil, as petitioners, are asking the Court to shut down Boulder's lawsuit. If the Court rules that federal law bars the claims, Boulder's suit would end and similar suits elsewhere would face the same barrier. If the Court rules that federal law does not bar the claims, Boulder's case would continue in Colorado courts, leaving the companies to litigate liability and damages there. If the Court finds it lacks jurisdiction to decide the preemption question, it would dismiss the case, leaving the Colorado Supreme Court's ruling allowing the suit to proceed in place without resolving the underlying legal question nationally.
