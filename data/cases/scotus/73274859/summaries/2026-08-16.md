## In brief

Energy companies Suncor and ExxonMobil asked the Supreme Court to hold that federal law blocks Boulder, Colorado's lawsuit seeking climate-change damages from them.

## What happened

Boulder County and the City of Boulder sued Suncor and ExxonMobil in Colorado state court, claiming the companies' production, marketing, and alleged deception about fossil fuels contributed to climate change and caused local harms like wildfires and drought. They seek damages under state tort theories including nuisance and trespass. A trial court refused to dismiss the suit, rejecting the companies' argument that federal law barred the claims. The Colorado Supreme Court affirmed, over two justices' dissent, holding that the Clean Air Act displaced earlier federal common law but did not preempt Colorado's claims. The companies then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that ruling before the case goes further.

## What the Court is being asked

The companies argue that disputes over the effects of multistate and international air pollution are the kind of issue the Constitution's structure reserves to federal law, so no state's tort law can supply the rule of decision, and that the Clean Air Act independently blocks these claims. Boulder responds that nothing in the Constitution or the Clean Air Act stops a state from using its own tort law to address in-state harm from out-of-state conduct, particularly deceptive marketing. The Court also directed the parties to brief whether it has authority to hear the case at all, given that the Colorado Supreme Court's decision did not end the lawsuit.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition and ordered briefing on both the preemption question and whether it has jurisdiction. The case has been fully briefed on the merits and was set for oral argument on October 5, 2026; the record has been requested from the Colorado Supreme Court and the case was circulated as of the snapshot date.

## What each outcome would mean

Suncor and ExxonMobil, as petitioners, asked the Court to step in. If the Court finds it lacks jurisdiction or that the case was not ready for review, it would dismiss, leaving the Colorado Supreme Court's decision in place and allowing Boulder's lawsuit to continue in state court. If the Court reaches the merits and rules that federal law preempts Boulder's claims, the suit would likely be dismissed, ending Boulder's attempt to recover damages this way. If the Court rules that federal law does not preempt the claims, Boulder's lawsuit would continue toward trial on its state-law theories. The Court could also send the case back for further proceedings without resolving the preemption question for either side.
