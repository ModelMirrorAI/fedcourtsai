## In brief

Energy companies Suncor and ExxonMobil asked the Supreme Court to block Boulder County's climate-change lawsuit against them, arguing federal law bars such state-law claims.

## What happened

Boulder County and the City of Boulder sued Suncor and ExxonMobil in Colorado state court, seeking damages for local harms they say were caused by the companies' role in global climate change, through both deceptive marketing and fossil fuel production and sales. The companies argued federal law—rooted in the Constitution's structure, the Clean Air Act, and foreign-affairs principles—bars these state-law claims. Colorado's trial court and, on interlocutory review, the Colorado Supreme Court rejected that argument and allowed the suit to proceed. The companies then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that interlocutory ruling, noting a split among state and federal appeals courts on whether such claims can proceed under state law.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court must decide two things: first, whether it even has authority to review this case at this stage, given that the Colorado Supreme Court's ruling did not end the lawsuit; and second, whether federal law—including the Constitution's structure, the Clean Air Act, and the federal government's control over foreign affairs—prevents local governments from using state tort law to seek damages from energy companies for harms tied to greenhouse gas emissions that cross state and international lines.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition and directed the parties to also brief and argue whether it has jurisdiction to hear the case. Merits briefing and amicus filings were completed, and the case was argued on October 5, 2026; it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Suncor and ExxonMobil, as petitioners, are asking the Court to shut down Boulder's lawsuit. If the Court rules for the companies on the merits, Boulder's claims would be barred, ending this suit and likely affecting dozens of similar climate lawsuits nationwide. If the Court rules for Boulder, the companies' preemption defense would fail, and the case would return to Colorado courts for further proceedings on the merits. If the Court instead finds it lacks jurisdiction to decide the preemption question at this stage, it would dismiss without resolving the dispute, leaving the Colorado Supreme Court's decision allowing the suit to proceed intact for now, with the underlying case continuing in Colorado courts.
