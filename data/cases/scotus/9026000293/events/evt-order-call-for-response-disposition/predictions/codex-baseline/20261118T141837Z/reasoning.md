The question presented and the petition's twelve-page argument seek a GVR in light of Exxon Mobil Corp. v. Corporacion CIMEX, S.A. (Cuba), decided June 23, 2026. Petitioners argue that the Second Circuit improperly made executive recognition dispositive of foreign-state status under the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA), whereas CIMEX requires judicial statutory interpretation. They also support the companion petition's challenge to the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) rule fixing instrumentality status at the asset-blocking date. The importance of the funds, a divided panel, the asserted inter-circuit disagreements, experienced counsel, and the actual CVSG support a substantial review probability. The alleged circuit conflicts remain petitioners' characterization rather than an independently established square split.

The petition's own appendix supplies the strongest reason to shade the anchor down. The panel separately analyzes Afghanistan's recognition and DAB's statutory instrumentality status, expressly reserving the latter determination to courts (App. 28a–34a). More directly, it already holds that TRIA overrides both jurisdictional and execution immunity under the FSIA and supplies independent jurisdiction, analyzing the statute's notwithstanding clause and relevant precedent (App. 35a–42a). It rejects the judgment creditors' recovery because DAB was not a Taliban instrumentality when the assets were blocked (App. 43a–50a). This complicates the petition's assertion that the panel never considered whether TRIA displaces immunity. CIMEX's statute-specific abrogation holding does not itself answer the recognition question or the separate TRIA timing question. Changing the FSIA classification might still open another attachment route, as Sullivan's dissent explains (App. 62a); I therefore do not treat the timing holding as making relief categorically impossible.

I checked CIMEX through CourtListener rather than relying solely on the petition's description. The June 23 opinion's syllabus and opening reasoning confirm a holding about the Helms-Burton Act's abrogation of immunity for Cuban instrumentalities. Its four stated considerations concern that statute's text and structure. Searches of the complete opinion returned no occurrence of `recognition` or `Terrorism Risk`; the passage transferring primary responsibility to courts appears in its discussion of the history of immunity and the Helms-Burton Act's presidential suspension power. That does not resolve every possible analogy, but it weakens the claim that this is a mechanical application of an intervening holding. I did not independently verify the petition's precise Supreme Court Reporter pinpoint citations.
