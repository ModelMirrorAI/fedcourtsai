## In brief

Families of 9/11 and embassy-bombing victims ask the Court to send their case back for reconsideration of whether the Taliban can claim sovereign immunity from asset-execution claims.

## What happened

Thousands of family members and estates of terrorism victims hold judgments against the Taliban and have tried to collect from frozen Afghan central bank assets held at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The Second Circuit ruled against them, holding that the bank and the Taliban are protected by sovereign immunity because the Executive Branch still formally recognizes Afghanistan as a state, and separately that the bank did not count as a Taliban "instrumentality" for a terrorism-judgment statute because of when its assets were frozen. Rehearing en banc was denied over dissents. The petitioners then asked the Supreme Court to review the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The petitioners argue that a more recent Supreme Court decision, issued after the lower court ruled, clarified that courts—not the Executive Branch—must decide through ordinary legal analysis whether an entity counts as a foreign state entitled to immunity. They ask the Court to set aside the lower court's ruling and send the case back so the appeals court can apply that clarified approach to decide whether the Taliban and the bank are immune.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in August 2026, and an amicus brief was filed in October 2026. The case was distributed for conference in early November 2026 and then distributed again for a second conference in mid-November 2026; the docket shows no decision yet on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The Ashton petitioners, who lost in the Second Circuit, are asking the Court to step in. If the Court grants the petition, it could vacate the lower court's ruling and send the case back for reconsideration under the newer legal standard, deciding nothing yet about who ultimately wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Second Circuit's ruling favoring the Taliban and the bank on immunity stands, leaving the petitioners unable to collect on their judgments through this case.
