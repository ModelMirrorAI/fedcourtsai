## In brief

9/11 victims' family members ask the Court to send their case back for reconsideration under a new ruling on how courts decide foreign sovereign immunity.

## What happened

Kathleen Ashton and thousands of other 9/11 victims' family members hold judgments against the Taliban and sought to collect from blocked assets of Afghanistan's central bank, held at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York. The Second Circuit ruled against them, holding that the Taliban and its instrumentalities count as a "foreign state" entitled to immunity because the Executive Branch still formally recognizes Afghanistan as a state, even though it does not recognize the Taliban as its government. After that ruling, the Supreme Court decided a different case, Exxon Mobil v. Corporación CIMEX, addressing how courts should determine whether an entity qualifies for sovereign immunity under a related statute.

## What the Court is being asked

The Ashton family members argue the appeals court got it backwards: deciding whether an entity counts as a foreign state entitled to immunity is a job for courts interpreting the relevant statute, not a question answered automatically by whatever the Executive Branch recognizes. They ask the Court to send the case back to the appeals court so it can reconsider the immunity question using the approach the Court's intervening decision set out, without yet deciding who should ultimately win.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, and the Court has now invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views before proceeding further; no action on the petition itself has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The Ashton family members are asking the Court to act. If the Court takes the unusual step the petition requests—sending the case back without full briefing—the appeals court would have to re-examine whether the Taliban and its instrumentalities get immunity, using the framework from the intervening decision, without the Supreme Court deciding who wins. If the Court instead denies the petition or takes no further action, the Second Circuit's ruling that the Taliban and its instrumentalities are immune, blocking the family members' efforts to collect on their judgments, stays in place.
