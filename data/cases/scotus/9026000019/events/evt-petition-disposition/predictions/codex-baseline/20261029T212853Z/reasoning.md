## Anchor and adjustments

The committed `metrics/statpack.md` sal-v4 segment table supplies the relevant risk-set anchor, not the terminal baseline-band rate. Pooling every shown Term strictly before 2026, OT2017–OT2025, gives **660 grant-family resolutions / 12,913 weighted resolved private-petitioner risk-set cases = 5.111%**, calculated from the exact `prefix_est_grant_rate` and `prefix_weighted_resolved` fields in `metrics/statpack.json`. I exclude OT2026 and do not substitute the broader mixed-era docket rate. This is an arithmetic result from the committed pack, not a newly refreshed corpus-wide estimate.

I move the grant probability to **9%**, primarily because the response request is an affirmative signal not encoded by the frozen baseline tier, and because the petition identifies a concrete opposing state-high-court treatment of firearm disabilities. I do not treat a response request as the equivalent of a grant, or as evidence that four Justices favor review. The upward adjustment is judgmental: the pack does not publish a response-request-conditioned grant rate.

The strongest substantive feature is a real tension in application of the petty-offense doctrine. The QP and petition pp. 3–5 argue that firearm restrictions and inability to expunge a domestic-abuse conviction make a six-month offense sufficiently serious to require a jury. Blanton v. City of North Las Vegas, 489 U.S. 538, 542–545 (1989), retrieved through CourtListener, treats six months or less as presumptively petty but permits sufficiently severe additional statutory penalties to rebut that presumption. Thus the maximum term does not answer the entire federal question. The petition's assertion that a jury would have acquitted does not independently establish certworthiness.

I verified the petition's Nevada authority as **Andersen v. Eighth Judicial District Court, 2019 NV 42**, decided September 12, 2019. The petition spells the name differently. That decision required a jury following Nevada's adoption of its own firearm disability and discussed both state and federal constitutional protection. Its distinction between a state's own statutory penalty and a consequence imposed by federal law matters: the Louisiana petition alleges a state disability as well. This supports taking the question seriously, but one differently structured state statute and decision do not establish a fully developed, square nationwide split. I did not survey every jurisdiction or assume all collateral consequences count identically.

The appendix substantially limits the upward adjustment. App. 35a–39a shows the appellate court declined to reconsider its pretrial jury ruling under discretionary law-of-the-case principles; it also reports the State's arguments that a constitutional challenge was insufficiently particularized and not served on the attorney general. Those are possible preservation and reviewability complications, not findings that an adequate independent state ground certainly bars Supreme Court review. App. 41a remands for resentencing to specify restrictions on sentencing benefits, creating an additional finality question that the short petition does not resolve in detail. I do not assume that remand automatically defeats jurisdiction. The prior protective order also records firearm restrictions before this conviction (App. 52a–53a), making the petitioner's individual circumstances less clean than an otherwise unrestricted defendant's. That does not itself answer an offense-based Blanton inquiry.

In this case an additional distribution is highly likely for an unusually direct reason: the Court requested a response before the scheduled conference, and that response is still outstanding. Hence **0.98 for the distribution increment** can coexist with only **0.09 for a grant**. It is not a forecast that the Court will repeatedly reconsider the petition on its merits.

The CVSG cut has 164 weighted resolved invited-view cases, with 29.3% granted and 5.5% GVR, versus 13,556 without an invitation, with 4.1% granted and 2.3% GVR. That describes a selected terminal population, not this petition's invitation probability. I assign **0.015** to a future CVSG because the federal statutory background gives some possible federal interest, but this is principally an ordinary state criminal jury-right dispute.

The provisioned docket shows no amicus-brief entries; context's null count is not a measured zero. As a descriptive cross-check only, the pack's known zero-amicus bucket has 11,109 weighted resolved cases, with 2.0% granted and 1.8% GVR. That is also a terminal count, could still change here, and supplies no independent likelihood multiplier. Neither it nor the terminal relist cut replaces the risk-set anchor.

I assign **0.10 to summary disposition conditional on a grant**: a straightforward GVR catalyst is absent from the material reviewed, and clarification of the Blanton exception would more naturally require plenary consideration. I assign **0.08 to a dissent or statement conditional on denial**, reflecting a meaningful constitutional question but a short petition with vehicle obstacles rather than an obviously clean test case. Per-Justice writing probabilities are unconditional, allow more than one author, and need not sum to one. The small preferences for Thomas and Gorsuch are my qualitative judgments, not measured frequencies from the pack. I omit cert vote predictions because the public denial order generally would not establish them.

The **0.52 stakes score** concerns potential significance if the question is decided, not grant odds: recognizing a jury right tied to firearm disabilities could affect a class of misdemeanor prosecutions and trial administration. It is not a forecast of the result of a Second Amendment merits challenge.
