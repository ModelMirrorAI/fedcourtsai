# Predicted course — Nguyen v. Louisiana, No. 26-19

**State forecast from.** Paid petition, distributed once (for the 9/28/2026
conference). Louisiana waived a response on Aug 5. The Court then called for a
response on Sep 9, due Oct 9. No CVSG, no relist so far.

**Relist / further distribution.** I expect the petition to be distributed
again once Louisiana's response comes in (or its time runs out). That should
put it at a November conference. Most likely it is decided at that first
post-response conference. One further relist is possible but unlikely.

**CVSG.** None. The respondent is a State in a criminal case, and no federal
interest calls for the Solicitor General's views.

**Disposition.** Denied. *Blanton v. City of North Las Vegas* already sets the
rule: an offense carrying six months or less is presumptively "petty", and the
presumption is overcome only by statutory penalties that show the legislature
treats the offense as serious. Collateral firearm disqualification is the
federal-law consequence that the petition leans on. State courts and lower
federal courts have mostly held that it does not overcome the presumption. The
Nevada Supreme Court (*Andersen*, 2019) went the other way, but that decision
rested on Nevada's own statute. So any split is shallow. The petition is six
pages long and argues mostly by appeal to the word "serious". The call for a
response suggests that at least one chambers wants a fuller look, most
plausibly out of interest in the petty-offense doctrine's historical footing.

**If granted.** The Court would take the question as written but reframe it:
do statutory collateral consequences, especially loss of firearm rights under
18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(9), count in the *Blanton* seriousness inquiry? Or more
broadly, is the petty-offense exception consistent with the Sixth Amendment's
original meaning? Plenary review is the likelier route. A summary reversal of
a state court that applied *Blanton* as written is improbable, and no
intervening decision invites a GVR.

**Separate writing on denial.** Probably none. If any Justice writes, Justice
Gorsuch is the most likely, given his skepticism of jury-trial exceptions not
grounded in founding-era practice. Justice Jackson or Justice Thomas are the
other plausible writers.
