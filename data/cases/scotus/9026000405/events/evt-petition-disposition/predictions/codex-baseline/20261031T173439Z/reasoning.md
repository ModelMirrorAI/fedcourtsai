## Why 2.5% for any grant

The positive evidence is a plausible, identifiable circuit disagreement, rather than a naked request for error correction. The petition's question presented and its argument at pages 6–9 contrast the Ninth Circuit's judicial determination of statutory scope with other circuits' referral of substantial coverage questions to Labor. I checked two cited lower-court authorities without searching for this petition's disposition. In *Moe v. United States*, 326 F.3d 1065, 1068–69 (9th Cir. 2003), the court distinguishes judicial scope determinations from administrative entitlement decisions. In *Fuqua v. United States Postal Service*, 956 F.3d 961, 964–65 (7th Cir. 2020), the court applies the substantial-question framework and treats the Secretary's coverage determination as controlling. These authorities support a real difference in approach; they do not by themselves prove that these particular facts would yield opposite final judgments today.

I nevertheless move below the 5.19% anchor for this vehicle and present posture:

- The federal respondents waived a response, and the snapshot shows no call for a response. I treat this as modest negative attention evidence, not as an assertion that a waiver prevents a later grant. Nothing in the record demonstrates that the Court itself has endorsed the waiver's assessment.
- The FECA ruling actually challenged is the unpublished January 15, 2025 summary-affirmance order, reproduced at petition appendix pages 17a–19a. The published June 23, 2026 opinion concerns the family's distinct assault claim and reverses its dismissal; it is not a fresh published ruling elaborating the FECA conflict. The petition explains that the earlier mandate was withheld while the family appeal continued. I do not infer untimeliness merely from the two dates, but the procedural layering makes this less clean than a single final opinion squarely developing the split.
- The district court initially stayed the employee's tort claims to permit a Labor determination. Its later dismissal followed his election not to pursue that route (petition pages 5–6 and appendix C). The Court would therefore review a claimant's refusal to obtain an initial agency decision, rather than an actual final agency coverage ruling.
- The district court had more than a bare government assertion of possible coverage. Appendix D, pages 32a–35a, describes Labor's memorandum identifying a substantial likelihood of coverage for the alleged home assault and related torts. The appendix also describes an employment investigation behind the home encounter. The absence of a pleading expressly alleging disability or on-duty injury is therefore not dispositive of the statutory issue.
- *Moe* itself ruled for the government because psychological harm accompanied by physical injury fell within FECA. It preserves judicial scope review, but it does not establish the petition's categorical proposition that filing in the Ninth Circuit would necessarily produce a favorable result on this record. The district court also cites Ninth Circuit authority for staying an FTCA action when coverage presents a substantial question. The distinction between injury type and case-specific coverage deserves attention, but complicates the claimed outcome conflict.

There are no amicus-brief entries in the three-entry snapshot. The statpack's zero-amicus bucket has n=11,109 resolved petitions and a 3.8% combined grant/GVR rate. I use that only as a weak cross-check: it measures amicus count at disposition, may change after this snapshot, and does not isolate an independent effect beyond docket attention. The statutory issue has recurring importance, which keeps the probability above a near-zero error-correction forecast, but the thin cert-stage attention and vehicle weaknesses outweigh the split signal. The resulting 2.5% is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted estimate.
