## In brief

The Court declined to hear a federal employee's bid to let courts, rather than the Labor Department, decide if a workplace-injury law barred his damages claim.

## What happened

Gengshu Scott He, a former State Department employee, and his family sued the government for incidents involving a State Department security officer, including an alleged confrontation at their Virginia home. Lower courts dismissed He's own tort claims, ruling that a law covering federal workers' job injuries required the Labor Department to decide first whether it covered his claims, since He had declined to seek that agency's determination. The court of appeals also dismissed his discrimination and retaliation claims, but allowed his family's separate assault claim to proceed. He asked the Supreme Court to review the dismissal of his own claims.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether, when a federal employee sues the government for common-law harm and does not claim any disability, death, or injury connected to his job duties, a court must still let the executive branch decide first whether a workplace-injury compensation law applies before the employee's lawsuit can go forward. Federal appeals courts disagree on how much deference such a law requires.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference after the government waived its right to respond. The Court denied the petition on November 9, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

He asked the Court to take up his case. With the petition denied, the lower court's decision dismissing his claims stands: He's tort, discrimination, and retaliation claims remain dismissed, and the disagreement among federal appeals courts over how much deference the executive branch gets in these cases remains unresolved by the Supreme Court. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning, only that it left that ruling in place.
