## In brief

Companies tied to asbestos litigation ask the Court to review whether a South Carolina receiver can control an English corporation's lawsuits without recognition abroad.

## What happened

John and Margaret Tibbs sued numerous companies in South Carolina, alleging asbestos exposure caused Mr. Tibbs's lung cancer. One defendant, Cape Intermediate Holdings Limited (CIHL), an English company, never appeared in the case. A South Carolina trial court had earlier appointed a receiver to act on behalf of Cape in a separate asbestos case, and that receiver accepted service for CIHL here and sued Charter Consolidated, ESAB Corporation, Central Mining & Investment Corporation, and others, claiming they were CIHL's alter egos. The petitioners argued the receivership was unconstitutional and conflicted with a Third Circuit ruling limiting receivers' power over out-of-state corporations. The South Carolina Supreme Court upheld the receivership, with some modifications to its scope, and denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether constitutional limits on state courts' power prevent a state-appointed receiver from making litigation decisions and controlling other internal affairs of a corporation based outside the state, when no court where that corporation is legally organized has recognized the receiver's authority.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed, the respondents waived their right to respond, several amicus briefs were filed, and the case was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026. The docket shows no action since that distribution.

## What each outcome would mean

Charter Consolidated and the other petitioners are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the South Carolina Supreme Court's ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether the receivership arrangement is constitutional, deciding nothing about the outcome at this stage. If the Court denies review, the South Carolina Supreme Court's decision upholding the receiver's authority over CIHL, including its power to pursue claims against the petitioners, would remain in place, and the case would continue toward trial on the receiver's claims.
