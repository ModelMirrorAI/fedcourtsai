## In brief

Companies tied to an English asbestos defendant ask the Court to decide whether a South Carolina court-appointed receiver can control that foreign corporation's litigation decisions.

## What happened

John and Margaret Tibbs sued numerous companies in South Carolina state court, alleging asbestos exposure caused Mr. Tibbs's lung cancer. One named defendant, an English company now identified as Cape Intermediate Holdings Limited (CIHL), never appeared; a South Carolina trial court had already appointed a receiver to act on its behalf in a related asbestos case. The receiver accepted service for CIHL and filed claims against Charter Consolidated, ESAB Corporation, Central Mining & Investment Corporation, and others, alleging they were CIHL's alter egos. Those companies challenged the receiver's authority and personal jurisdiction over them. The South Carolina Supreme Court upheld the receivership, as narrowed, and rejected the companies' arguments, including that a federal appeals court had reached a different result in a similar case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether constitutional limits on state power prevent a state court from letting a court-appointed receiver make litigation decisions and manage other internal matters for a corporation based in another state or country, when that corporation's home jurisdiction has not recognized the receiver's authority.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference. The Court then asked the respondents to respond, with that response now due December 28, 2026 after an extension was granted.

## What each outcome would mean

Charter Consolidated and the other petitioners are asking the Court to overturn the South Carolina Supreme Court's decision upholding the receivership. If the Court grants review and later rules for the petitioners, it would limit or reject the receiver's authority over CIHL's litigation decisions, benefiting the petitioners. If it rules for the respondents, the receivership as structured would stand. If the Court denies review, the South Carolina Supreme Court's decision upholding the receivership remains in effect, leaving the receiver's authority intact.
