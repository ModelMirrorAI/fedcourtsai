## Why above the prior, but still denial

The petition's strongest feature is a concrete claimed conflict, not merely an assertion of serious legal error. Its introduction and pages 16–23 juxtapose the Third Circuit's Whittaker decision with the South Carolina Supreme Court's express treatment of that decision. Both concern the same receiver appointed by the same trial court and control over an out-of-state corporation's litigation choices. The alleged English injunction against the receiver makes the international conflict tangible rather than speculative (petition pages 7–8, 12–13, 28–29). The allegation of repeated receiverships makes the question potentially recurring. These features warrant a substantial upward adjustment from an ordinary private arrival.

There are nevertheless substantial reasons not to equate a forceful petition with a probable grant:

- **The split is contestable.** As the petition itself explains, Whittaker involved a New Jersey board's authority to commence bankruptcy, interpretation of the appointment order, and the effect of that order elsewhere. Here the state court limited a prejudgment receiver to insurance recovery and claims tied to the pending local litigation. It distinguished bankruptcy control from these functions and disputed whether the constitutional discussion was necessary. Petitioners have a plausible alternative-holding response, but that does not erase the differences in orders, procedural settings, or corporate domicile. See petition pages 11–13 and 17–23.
- **Finality is a genuine vehicle risk.** The receiver's third-party claims await trial. Petitioners invoke separable finality and the Cox exception to defend immediate review under section 1257; they do not present an ordinary completed state-court merits judgment. Their argument may succeed, but the Court could prefer to avoid the threshold dispute or await a cleaner vehicle. See petition pages 1–2 and 34–35. I am identifying uncertainty, not concluding jurisdiction is absent.
- **Several constitutional theories must be connected to the precise order.** General limits on territorial authority do not themselves settle whether litigation-specific receivership powers amount to forbidden control of internal affairs. A broad ruling could require separating asset administration, corporate decision-making, personal jurisdiction, and international recognition. This reduces the attractiveness of immediate summary correction.
- **Other vehicles may absorb attention.** The petition identifies pending Whittaker petitions, Nos. 26-159 and 26-263 (page 3). Those create a possible hold-and-GVR route, but also competition for plenary review. I have not retrieved their status or outcomes. The provisioned petition also reports denial of certiorari in the distinct Atlas Turner/Welch receivership case (pages 8–9). That is legitimate pre-snapshot context, not this petition's outcome; it modestly restrains my estimate without establishing that the present, broader challenge will be denied.

Balancing a substantially stronger-than-baseline conflict and international stakes against these vehicle issues and the one-sided record yields 22%, rather than either the unadjusted 5% prior or a grant-majority estimate. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted statistical model. The largest uncertainty is how a responsive brief would characterize the asserted split and finality issue.
