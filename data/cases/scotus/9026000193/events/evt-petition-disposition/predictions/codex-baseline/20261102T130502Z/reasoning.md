## Anchor and adjustment

The primary anchor is the context's frozen `cert_anchor.rate`, **0.0518752467429925**, under `sal-v6` with a ten-Term lookback. The matching statpack table's strictly prior Term rows, 2017–2025, describe baseline-band reached rates of roughly 4–6%; I do not use the terminal baseline rate or the current Term to replace this anchor. Hall is the private petitioner; the state official is the respondent, not a reason to use the state-petitioner floor.

The paid-segment amicus cut is a useful cross-check: the 2+ bucket has **1,446 weighted resolved petitions**, with 18.2% plenary grants and 4.5% GVRs, or approximately 22.7% grant-family outcomes. It is a marginal, disposition-time association, not a measured fourteen-brief effect or an independent likelihood multiplier. This case's organized support, direct constitutional question, and unusually clean overruling vehicle justify moving materially above both the broad anchor and that coarse 2+ category.

The upward case is concrete. Hall lost eligibility for tuition assistance because she selected a religious vocational major. The parties and all panel members agree that Locke directly governs; there is no factual dispute masking the constitutional issue at the dismissal stage. Richardson expressly invited overruling in a published concurrence (petition appendix 14a–21a). The petition, pp. 9–29, develops an overruling argument rather than merely alleging a misapplication, and pp. 29–33 identify consequences for similar programs in other states. The reply, pp. 1–12, persuasively distinguishes a merits disagreement over Virginia's history from a genuine procedural obstacle. The BIO identifies no separate preservation or jurisdictional defect in the materials I reviewed. Those features make this substantially more plausible than an ordinary once-distributed petition.

The main downward adjustment is equally specific: the Court would have to remove an exception it has expressly preserved, not resolve a split or correct a departure from its precedent. The BIO, pp. 7–17, stresses that Trinity Lutheran, Espinoza, and Carson repeatedly distinguished ministerial training from other educational benefits, and that Virginia presents the historical case for this exception particularly strongly. I checked Carson's discussion against its actual opinion through CourtListener (596 U.S. 767, 788–89; opinion 6351409). It retains Locke's vocational-degree limitation rather than declaring Locke defunct. That materially tempers the reply's claim that later decisions have already dismantled the exception. The lower court's faithful adherence to Locke is a clean vehicle if the Justices want reconsideration, but not an independent certworthiness error. No circuit conflict is established here, and none is inferred from amicus support.

Thus 40%, rather than the 5.19% anchor, reflects unusual substantive and institutional strength, while remaining below even odds because willingness to revisit this surviving exception is unproven. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted empirical probability. Fourteen briefs do not establish four votes to grant.
