## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a former insurance agency manager's bid to revive his claim for unpaid overtime pay.

## What happened

Jerry Merritt worked as an agency manager for Texas Farm Bureau and related companies, classified as an independent contractor with full control over his schedule. After retiring, he sued under the Fair Labor Standards Act, claiming he was really an employee owed overtime pay. A trial court agreed he was an employee and found he worked 816 hours of overtime, but a jury decided the companies never knew, and had no reason to know, that he was working those extra hours. The trial court upheld that verdict, and the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals unanimously affirmed, rejecting Merritt's argument that being allowed to work unlimited hours meant the companies automatically owed him overtime regardless of their knowledge.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a worker who is given free rein to work as many hours as he wants is automatically owed overtime pay for those hours, or whether the worker must separately prove his employer actually knew, or should have known, that he was working extra hours.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on October 9, 2026, and on October 13, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Merritt, the employee who asked the Court to take up his case, sought to overturn the Fifth Circuit's ruling in favor of the companies. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Fifth Circuit's decision in place: the jury's verdict that the companies owed Merritt no overtime pay stands, and his lawsuit remains over. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning, only that it chose not to review the case.
