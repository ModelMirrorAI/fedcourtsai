# Predicted reasoning — Balwani v. United States, No. 25-1330

**Disposition.** The Court will deny the petition, most likely on the order list following the September 28, 2026 conference, without calling for a response and without a separate writing.

**Relists.** The docket shows a single distribution (June 17, 2026, for the September 28 conference), following the government's June 9 waiver of its right to respond. The Court cannot grant a petition on which the respondent has not been heard, so the only route to a grant runs through a call for a response entered at or after the long conference, a brief in opposition, and a redistribution. I expect that not to happen: the petition sat distributed for three months over the summer without a response being requested, which is the window in which a Justice's chambers interested in a waived petition usually asks for one. I put roughly one chance in eight on any further distribution at all, most of that being a post-conference call for a response or a reschedule rather than a substantive relist, and a call for a response would itself be the signal that this forecast is wrong.

**CVSG.** None. The United States is the respondent, so there is no Solicitor General to invite; the Court's analogous move would be a call for a response, addressed above.

**Which question, if taken.** If the Court took the case it would take Question 1 as written, the standard-of-review question for an unpreserved Napue claim the prosecution exploited in closing, and it would leave Question 2 behind. Question 2 asks the Court to review a harmless-error holding on Rule 702, which is error-correction dressed as a split, and the Ninth Circuit's opinion decided it on the "any error was harmless" ground rather than on the credentials-only rule the petition describes. A grant on Question 1 would likely be reformulated to ask whether plain-error review governs a Napue claim not raised at trial, dropping the petition's premise that the prosecution "affirmatively exploited" a known falsity, since the panel assumed rather than found a duty to correct and said the record was unclear whether the testimony was more than a mistaken recollection.

**Summary disposition.** Unlikely. No intervening decision of this Court bears on either question; Glossip v. Oklahoma (2025) predates the Ninth Circuit's amended opinion and concerned a confessed Napue violation on state postconviction review, so it is not a GVR trigger. Conditional on a grant, I put about three in ten on a cert-stage disposition (a summary reversal on the plain-error framing being the only realistic form), and seven in ten on plenary review.

**Dissent from denial.** None expected. The claimed Napue violation was never found, only assumed, and the defendant is a well-resourced white-collar petitioner whose convictions a unanimous, ideologically mixed panel affirmed at length with no judge calling for an en banc vote. That is not the profile that draws a statement respecting denial. If one came it would be a short statement about the prosecution's duty to correct testimony it had the means to check, but I put that at a few percent.

**If granted (conditional only).** The likely ground would be that plain-error review does apply to an unpreserved Napue claim under Puckett, with the Court affirming, and the case would turn on whether the defense's pretrial effort to admit the recording preserved the claim. A holding that Napue errors are exempt from plain-error review would be a significant departure and is the less likely outcome even on a grant.
