## What happened

Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani was convicted in federal court in California of fraud counts arising from his role at the blood-testing company Theranos. On appeal, the Ninth Circuit found that a government witness had given testimony that was actually false about Theranos's work with the military, and that two other prosecution witnesses had given specialized scientific opinions without the trial court ever screening that testimony for reliability, as required before such testimony can be admitted. The appeals court nonetheless upheld the convictions, reviewing the false-testimony issue under a more forgiving standard because it found the objection at trial had not clearly flagged the issue, and treating the missing reliability screening as harmless because the witnesses were qualified by background and experience. Balwani asked the full Ninth Circuit to reconsider; that request was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions. First, when prosecutors not only let false testimony stand uncorrected but repeatedly relied on it in closing arguments the defense had no chance to answer, should courts apply the toughest form of review to that constitutional problem, rather than a more lenient standard reserved for errors that were not flagged clearly at trial. Second, when a trial judge completely skips the required screening of whether an expert-type opinion is reliable, may an appeals court excuse that gap simply by finding the witnesses were qualified by their credentials, without examining whether their methods and reasoning were actually reliable.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed after a court-approved extension, and the government waived its right to respond. The case has been distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, where the Justices will decide whether to take up the case.
