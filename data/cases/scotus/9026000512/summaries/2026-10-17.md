## In brief

A doctor convicted at a second trial asks the Court to let defendants challenge double-jeopardy violations immediately, before sentencing, rather than waiting for a full appeal.

## What happened

Muhammad Saeed, a physician, was tried on charges including fraud and sexual offenses tied to his prescribing practices. A first jury acquitted him of charges accusing him of improperly prescribing drugs but could not reach a verdict on related fraud and sexual-offense counts. At a second trial on those remaining counts, the state again presented evidence about his prescribing practices; the jury convicted him on all counts. Saeed argued this violated double jeopardy principles barring the state from relitigating issues a jury already resolved in his favor. The trial court rejected this argument, and the Arizona Court of Appeals dismissed his appeal for lack of jurisdiction, reasoning no appealable judgment existed because he had not yet been sentenced. The Arizona Supreme Court denied review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a defendant who has been found guilty but not yet sentenced may immediately seek federal habeas review, a procedure for challenging a conviction's constitutionality, when he has a plausible claim that the state improperly reused an issue a prior jury already decided in his favor. A related question asks whether a conviction can be treated as invalid, without requiring formal entry of judgment or sentencing, when such a violation occurred.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and docketed, with the state's response due November 16, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Saeed, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether defendants in his situation may seek immediate federal habeas relief before sentencing; a grant alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the lower courts' rulings stand, leaving Saeed's conviction intact and requiring him to pursue any further challenge through ordinary appeal channels after sentencing, rather than through immediate habeas review.
