## Why below the anchor

1. **The federal question and the procedural vehicle do not line up cleanly.** QP1 asks about immediate federal habeas relief under section 2241 following a guilty verdict but before sentence. This petition instead seeks direct review under section 1257 of a state trial court's denial of a motion to vacate. The record does not present a federal habeas judgment resolving section 2241. The petition's own jurisdiction and procedural-history sections make that mismatch evident (petition pp. 1, 14–22; appendix A–C).

2. **Finality is a substantial obstacle, not an assumed categorical bar.** The December 31, 2025 appellate order expressly dismisses because neither the verdicts nor the motion ruling is appealable before sentence; the Arizona Supreme Court denied review on May 7, 2026 (appendix A–B). The petition seeks an extension of immediate double-jeopardy review, but a completed second trial is different from preventing an impending second trial. Abney v. United States, 431 U.S. 651, 657–662 (1977), retrieved through CourtListener opinion 109677, concerns federal appellate jurisdiction under section 1291, not a freestanding federal habeas entitlement. Its discussion of section 1257 and Harris recognizes immediate review in the pretrial double-jeopardy setting, so I do not infer that absence of a sentence alone always defeats Supreme Court review. My inference is narrower: this posttrial state-law appealability posture is an unattractive vehicle for the requested extension.

3. **The lack of sentencing is intertwined with the defendant's absence.** The petition describes his departure and missed arraignment (pp. 14–15), and the November 5, 2025 order attributes the failure to sentence to his continued absence (appendix D). That November order was later vacated and replaced by the December finality-based dismissal; I do not describe its original fugitive-related ground as the operative appellate holding. The underlying absence nevertheless weakens the petition's practical claim that ordinary review unjustifiably forces him to await sentencing. I make no separate finding about habeas custody, which this record does not adjudicate.

4. **No developed split is shown, and the underlying estoppel issue is fact-intensive.** The reasons-for-granting section (pp. 23–30) principally argues by analogy to Abney, Ashe, and Martinez, rather than identifying conflicting appellate holdings on this precise posttrial remedy. The trial court found that evidence of prescription practices supported different issues in the second trial and was not simply relitigation of a fact necessarily resolved by the acquittals (October 13 ruling, appendix F, pp. 12–14). Petitioner disputes that characterization. Resolving it would require careful comparison of the trials, not merely correcting a clear legal rule. My low grant estimate does not adjudicate the constitutional claim against him.

Together these features justify reducing the approximately 5.16% reached-band anchor to 1%. The double-jeopardy interest and the possibility that a Justice sees an important remedial gap keep the probability above zero. There is no identified intervening controlling decision supporting a routine GVR. I assign a 12% summary-route probability conditional on the unlikely grant; the remaining 88% conditional probability goes to a route opening further merits proceedings.
