## Case-specific adjustments

**A real, potentially important legal disagreement supports an upward adjustment.** The first question concerns whether chilling effects may enter Rule 54(d)(1) discretion, not simply whether these particular litigants deserve sympathy. The petition identifies a Ninth Circuit approach in tension with the Eighth Circuit's treatment of civil-rights plaintiffs. I independently checked the costs discussion in *Stanley v. University of Southern California*, 178 F.3d 1069, 1079–80 (9th Cir. 1999), through CourtListener: it treats failure to consider indigency and chilling effects as grounds for finding an abuse of discretion. That confirms an important component of the petition's asserted conflict, although it is not an exhaustive survey or a check of every authority's subsequent treatment. The broader conflict characterization appears in petition pages 8–11; the appellate court's own reasoning appears at appendix pages 5a–8a.

**Vehicle complications offset that signal.** The lower court expressly allows indigency to justify withholding costs in an appropriate case. Its central reason for reversal was that the district court had not addressed counsel's 2013 joint recommendation to split expert fees, and that the plaintiffs had not supplied evidence explaining why that offer should not be relied upon. It also reasoned that this particular partial award was unlikely substantially to chill meritorious litigation. Those passages make the case less clean than an abstract ban on considering any chilling effect. The dispute involves unusually large court-appointed expert fees under Rule 706, a decades-old funding recommendation, and uncertainty about whether counsel or a third-party program was meant to fund the plaintiffs' share. The petition's account of oral argument is advocacy I could not verify from a transcript. Sources: petition pages 4–8 and 11–13; appendix pages 4a–8a and 15a–16a.

**The second question is principally record-specific error correction.** The petition raises a plausible concern that the appellate court substituted an inference about resources for district-court factfinding, but that claim turns on the meaning and evidentiary status of the earlier offer. The appended opinion describes a failure to meet the plaintiffs' burden rather than announcing a new general appellate factfinding standard. The Excessive Fines Clause argument is presented as something petitioners would have pursued on remand, not a constitutional holding actually reached below; that creates an additional obstacle to taking that issue here. I give this branch some weight as a possible narrow corrective route, not the same cert value as a clean, independently dispositive legal split.

**The observed docket supplies no additional escalation signal.** There is a waiver, only one distribution, and no recorded request for a response or CVSG. I treat the lack of requested adversarial briefing as modest negative evidence, not proof that review is impossible. A conference still lies ahead, so the Court retains the opportunity to request a response. The lower-court appendix lists amici, but those are not Supreme Court amici and I do not count them as such.

Taken together, the legal conflict prevents a very-low-probability dismissal of the petition, but the vehicle and posture justify moving modestly below the 5.12% risk-set anchor to **4.5%**. The precision expresses the recorded probability, not a claim that the evidence supports tenths-of-a-percentage-point accuracy.
