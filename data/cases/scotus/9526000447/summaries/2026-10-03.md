## In brief

Swift Transportation asked the Supreme Court to pause a New Mexico court order while it prepared an appeal; the application was denied.

## What happened

Plaintiffs sued Swift over a fatal highway accident, alleging a Swift trailer was involved. After disputes over Swift's discovery compliance, the trial court appointed an examiner with broad power to demand "all documents and information" from Swift, including passwords. Swift challenged the order as an unconstitutional general search unsupported by specifics, but the trial court rejected that challenge. Swift then sought relief from the Supreme Court of New Mexico, which summarily denied its petition on August 20. With a contempt hearing looming over Swift's noncompliance with the examination order, Swift asked the U.S. Supreme Court to stay enforcement while it prepared a petition for certiorari.

## What the Court is being asked

The underlying dispute concerns whether a court-appointed examiner, given essentially unlimited authority to demand a company's records and passwords, satisfies constitutional requirements that searches and seizures be specific and limited in scope. The immediate request before the Court was narrower: whether to temporarily block enforcement of the examination order, including a contempt hearing, while Swift pursued Supreme Court review of that constitutional question.

## Where it stands

Justice Gorsuch, acting as Circuit Justice, denied Swift's emergency application for a stay on October 2, two days after it was filed.

## What each outcome would mean

Swift, as the applicant, sought a pause of the state court's examination order while it pursued Supreme Court review. Because the application was denied, no stay is in place: the examination order and the trial court's contempt proceedings against Swift can go forward, and Swift must contend with that enforcement while separately pursuing its planned certiorari petition. The denial does not decide the underlying constitutional question about the examiner's authority; it only leaves the state court's order in effect for now.
