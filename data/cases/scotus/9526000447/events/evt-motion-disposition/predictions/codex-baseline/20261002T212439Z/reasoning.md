## Substantive assessment

The application, printed pages 1–8, describes a wrongful-death action, contested production of vehicle-location information, and a court-appointed examiner investigating the maintenance, loss, and recovery of responsive records. Swift acknowledges belatedly locating additional material and accepting some sanctions, but disputes the breadth and legitimacy of the examination. Its strongest point is the quoted authority to demand all requested documents, information, and passwords, coupled with the examiner's previous work for plaintiffs. The potential privacy injury is more substantial than the examiner's monetary retainer alone.

I do not adopt the application's characterization of an unlimited general warrant as an established fact. The available August 11 state-court opposition, EA Appendix 59–62 and 75–79, offers a competing account: identified discovery failures justified an expert inquiry; the order had a defined investigative purpose; Swift had opportunities to be heard; and privilege protections remained. Those are advocacy positions, not findings I independently verified. They nevertheless make a clean, facial constitutional violation less evident. The Supreme Court application invokes conflict with existing precedent and a need to clarify business-search doctrine, rather than documenting a developed split between appellate courts.

The governing stay threshold is demanding. **Hollingsworth v. Perry, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010)** requires a reasonable probability of certiorari, a fair prospect of reversal, and likely irreparable harm, with equities considered in close cases. I verified that passage through CourtListener. Even crediting a disclosure injury, I give Swift relatively little chance of satisfying the review-and-reversal components on this disputed discovery record.

Finality is an uncertainty, not a categorical bar. The application invokes **Fisher v. District Court, 424 U.S. 382, 385 n.7 (1976)** for the finality of a terminated original state-appellate proceeding. The verified footnote specifically concerns a proceeding whose only decided issue was the lower court's jurisdiction; it is narrower than an assertion that every extraordinary-writ denial supplies finality for every discovery challenge. I give the jurisdictional argument some credit but do not treat it as dispositive. The emergency application also argues that further state stay relief is unavailable, while acknowledging contempt review as a possible route. Its footnote 1 reports that plaintiffs proposed deferring contempt sanctions until Supreme Court consideration of the anticipated petition. That report weakens certainty about imminent coercion, although it does not establish that the trial judge agreed.

The response-request, referral, and amicus probabilities are judgmental increments, not published conditional base rates. I put them at **20%, 25%, and 4%** respectively: the asserted intrusion could attract judicial attention, but the disputed facts, narrow litigation setting, and short horizon favor uncomplicated denial. No per-Justice voting forecast is warranted by these inputs. The **0.36** stakes score recognizes the possible wider consequences for compelled corporate-data inspection while distinguishing those consequences from the much narrower immediate dispute and low grant probability.
