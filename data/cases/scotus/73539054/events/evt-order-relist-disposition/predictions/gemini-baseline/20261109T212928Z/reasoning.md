## 2. Analysis of the Provisioned Record & the Three Questions Presented

I read the provisioned snapshot (`record/snapshots/2026-11-09.json`), `record/context.json`, and all provisioned filings in `record/documents/` (`questions-presented.txt`, `petition.txt`, `brief-in-opposition.txt`, `cert-reply.txt`, `appendix.txt`, `decision-below.txt`, and `lower-court-docket.txt`), as well as the Supreme Court's slip opinion in *FCC v. AT&T, Inc.*, 608 U.S. 531 (2026) (No. 25-406, consolidated with *Verizon Communications, Inc. v. FCC*, No. 25-567, decided June 4, 2026).

This case arises from the same 2020–2024 FCC enforcement sweep as *AT&T* and *Verizon*, in which the FCC assessed monetary forfeitures under 47 U.S.C. § 503(b)(4) against the four major wireless carriers for failing to reasonably protect customer location information ("CPNI") shared with location-based service aggregators after the 2018 Securus disclosures ($12.24 million against Sprint; $80.08 million against T-Mobile). Below, the D.C. Circuit (Pan, J., joined by Pillard and Garcia, JJ.) denied Sprint and T-Mobile's petitions for review (*Sprint Corp. v. FCC*, 151 F.4th 347 (D.C. Cir. 2025)), and denied rehearing en banc with no noted dissents on March 24, 2026 (`lower-court-docket.txt`).

### A. Question 1: Seventh Amendment / Involuntary Waiver & Refund Request (GVR Request)

Petitioners do not seek plenary review on Question 1; instead, they request a **GVR** in light of *FCC v. AT&T, Inc.*, 608 U.S. 531 (2026), so the D.C. Circuit can consider whether the FCC's forfeiture orders misled Sprint and T-Mobile into paying the penalties rather than awaiting a 47 U.S.C. § 504(a) collection suit with a jury trial *de novo*.

A GVR on Question 1 is unlikely for four compounding reasons:

1. **The D.C. Circuit's Seventh Amendment holding was expressly endorsed by *AT&T*:** In *AT&T* (8–1, Roberts, C.J.), the Court held that § 503(b)(4) forfeiture orders do not violate the Seventh Amendment because they do not create a binding legal obligation to pay and any DOJ collection action under § 504(a) requires a full jury trial *de novo* on both facts and law. In doing so, the Court explicitly cited the D.C. Circuit's decision below with approval (*see* Slip Op. at 5, 8 n.1, citing *Sprint Corp. v. FCC*, 151 F.4th 347, 359–360 (D.C. Cir. 2025)).
2. **Footnote 5 of *AT&T* refused to disturb the Second Circuit's judgment on the identical posture:** In *AT&T*, the carriers raised the exact same argument in their reply brief—that the FCC's forfeiture orders misled them into paying and that they should receive a refund or remand. In Footnote 5 (Slip Op. at 14 n.5), the 8-Justice majority noted the argument ("We express no view on the merits of this argument, what relief may be available to the carriers, or in what proceeding") and nevertheless **affirmed** the Second Circuit's judgment denying Verizon's petition for review outright, rather than vacating and remanding for the Second Circuit to consider a refund or involuntary-waiver claim. Only Justice Thomas dissented on that ground. Having affirmed the Second Circuit in *Verizon* despite Footnote 5, the Court has little reason to GVR the D.C. Circuit in *Sprint* on an even weaker preservation record.
3. **Strict Statutory and Appellate Forfeiture Below:** As the Solicitor General's BIO (at 11–14) details, petitioners here never argued to the FCC or to the D.C. Circuit that their decision to pay the forfeiture and seek Hobbs Act review under 47 U.S.C. § 402(a) was an involuntary or misled waiver of their § 504(a) rights, nor did they seek a refund on that basis. Under 47 U.S.C. § 405(a), the D.C. Circuit is statutorily barred from considering arguments not presented to the Commission, and under settled D.C. Circuit rules arguments omitted from opening briefs are forfeited.
4. **D.C. Circuit Precedent Already Recognized *De Novo* Legal and Factual Review in § 504(a) Suits:** Justice Thomas's dissent in *AT&T* emphasized that AT&T and Verizon litigated in circuits (such as the Fifth Circuit under *United States v. Stevens*, 691 F.3d 620 (5th Cir. 2012)) where § 504(a) defendants were barred from challenging the legal validity of the forfeiture order. Here, however, the D.C. Circuit panel below explicitly held—months before *AT&T*—that in a § 504(a) trial *de novo*, "all issues of fact and law are subject to the trial *de novo*" (151 F.4th at 360), relying on long-standing D.C. Circuit precedent (*Pleasant Broadcasting Co. v. FCC*, 564 F.2d 496, 500 (D.C. Cir. 1977); *Action for Children's Television v. FCC*, 59 F.3d 1249, 1261 (D.C. Cir. 1995)).

### B. Question 2: Whether Device Location Data Is CPNI Under 47 U.S.C. § 222(h)(1)(A)

Petitioners seek plenary review on whether device location data qualifies as CPNI under § 222(h)(1)(A).

1. **Unanimous Circuit Consensus (0–3 Split):** All three Courts of Appeals that have considered the question—the D.C. Circuit below (151 F.4th at 353–356), the Second Circuit in *Verizon Communications Inc. v. FCC*, 156 F.4th 86, 96–99 (2d Cir. 2025), and the First Circuit in *T-Mobile USA, Inc. v. FCC*, 158 F.4th 189, 196–198 (1st Cir. 2025)—have unanimously held that location data falls squarely within the plain text of § 222(h)(1)(A). (The Fifth Circuit in *AT&T* vacated on Seventh Amendment grounds without reaching the statutory questions, and Verizon did not even petition for certiorari on the CPNI issue in No. 25-567.)
2. **Severe Vehicle Flaws (§ 405(a) Exhaustion Bar on the "Voice vs. Data" Argument):** Petitioners' core argument on Question 2 is that only voice service is a Title II "telecommunications service," and location information is generated whenever a device pings cell towers even when the customer is not making a voice call. However, both the D.C. Circuit (`decision-below.txt`, 151 F.4th at 354–356) and the First Circuit (*T-Mobile*, 158 F.4th at 197–198) explicitly held that Sprint and T-Mobile **forfeited** this argument under 47 U.S.C. § 405(a) by failing to raise the voice-versus-data distinction before the FCC.

### C. Question 3: Standard of Review for "Continuing Violations" Under 47 U.S.C. § 503(b) and *Loper Bright*

Petitioners contend that the D.C. Circuit contravened *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), by reviewing the FCC's count of separate "continuing violations" (one violation per location-based service provider/aggregator that retained access after the Securus disclosures—73 for T-Mobile and 11 for Sprint) under an arbitrary-and-capricious standard rather than *de novo*.

1. **No Circuit Split:** Every circuit to address these exact forfeiture orders—the D.C. Circuit (151 F.4th at 360–362), the Second Circuit (*Verizon*, 156 F.4th at 102–103), and the First Circuit (*T-Mobile*, 158 F.4th at 202–203)—reached the same conclusion and upheld the FCC's per-provider calculation.
2. **No *Loper Bright* Conflict:** The D.C. Circuit cited *Loper Bright* (151 F.4th at 353, 360) and exercised independent legal judgment to hold as a matter of statutory interpretation that § 503(b) does not restrict the Commission to finding a single overarching "policy" violation when a carrier separately fails to terminate unauthorized access across dozens of distinct contracts with third-party aggregators.
