# Filings and vehicle quality

The provisioned questions presented and petition, especially printed pages 1–5 and the first argument section, frame three requests: a GVR after **FCC v. AT&T**, a narrower statutory definition of protected location information under Section 222, and independent judicial interpretation of the Section 503 penalty cap. The petition and October 9 reply say payment under mandatory-sounding FCC orders was not an informed surrender of a jury-trial route; the government's intervening concessions should permit the D.C. Circuit to reconsider that premise. The reply, printed pages 3–8, persuasively distinguishes a request for reconsideration from a request that this Court itself award a refund. This is the principal source of upward adjustment.

The federal opposition, printed pages 6–11, is nevertheless unusually concrete. It argues that AT&T confirmed rather than displaced the pertinent statutory framework, that the FCC orders already explained the availability of a de novo trial, and that the misleading-order contention was not adequately raised below. It also reports that Verizon unsuccessfully sought rehearing to obtain comparable reconsideration in its own case. That is preexisting history of a different case, not this petition's outcome. Petitioners distinguish the posture and the questions presented, but the comparison reduces the prospect of an automatic companion-style GVR. The Solicitor General's position here is **deny**, not acquiescence in remand; this filed opposition is not an invited SG brief.

I checked the provisioned `decision-below.txt` against the caption, consolidated case numbers 24-1224/24-1225, judgment date, and appendix description. It is the relevant published D.C. Circuit decision. Its Seventh Amendment analysis, printed pages 15–18, rests on the carriers' ability to await enforcement and their choice to pay. The FCC-order language may still undermine the voluntariness premise, but the intervening decision does not obviously require rejection of the entire framework.

The alternative plenary questions are weaker vehicles. The opposition, printed pages 11–14, identifies agreement with the Second Circuit on device-location information; petitioners' reply identifies differences in reasoning and implications for data-only service, not a square opposite result on this record. More decisively, footnote 6 of the lower opinion expressly finds the statutory penalty-count challenge forfeited and reviews the preserved challenge as one about reasonable exercise of agency discretion. That makes a clean Loper Bright correction much less straightforward than the petition's characterization suggests. I credit the reply's preservation argument as a dispute, not as a resolved answer. The provisioned lower-court docket reports rehearing en banc denied and no noted dissent in the entries it examined; that limited report does not establish a complete absence of disagreement.

The petition, opposition, reply, and decision below are not marked empty or OCR-derived. No party letter, supplemental brief, related-docket snapshot, or same-question list was provisioned, and I draw no affirmative factual inference from their absence.
