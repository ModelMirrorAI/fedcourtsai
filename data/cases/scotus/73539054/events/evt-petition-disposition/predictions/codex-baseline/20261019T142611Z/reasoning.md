## Adjustments from the anchor

**Why materially above 5.1%.** The petition identifies an intervening decision about the same FCC forfeiture machinery and asks first for a GVR, a narrower action than a new merits hearing. Its argument is concrete: the carriers paid more than $92 million under orders commanding payment, while the later AT&T decision and government concessions described those orders as not independently compelling payment. The petition, pp. 17–23, and reply, pp. 3–8, argue that the lower court should reconsider whether payment validly waived jury rights. The reply also explains why Verizon's unsuccessful rehearing request is procedurally different. That is a credible remand route, even if the ultimate constitutional challenge has failed. The industry amici and prospective importance of the two statutory questions also distinguish this from a routine error-correction petition.

**Why still substantially below 50%.** The opposition, pp. 9–11, gives a case-specific reason why AT&T may not change the result: the D.C. Circuit already understood that petitioners could await a de novo enforcement proceeding. The retained opinion, appendix pp. 17a–18a, confirms that premise. The government also contests preservation of the misleading-order theory. Its report that the Court affirmed Verizon's judgment and later denied rehearing on a similar remand request weighs against automatic follow-on relief. I treat those related-case events as pre-snapshot evidence reported in the parties' filings, not as this petition's outcome. The reply's distinction is meaningful, so I do not treat the Verizon history as dispositive.

For question 2, the reply, pp. 8–10, identifies a difference between Second and D.C. Circuit reasoning concerning data-only customers, but both courts sustained the treatment of the location information actually at issue. The other cited circuits addressed the Title I/Title II boundary, not this precise CPNI application. That is weaker than an established conflict requiring opposite results on the presented facts. For question 3, the penalty-cap issue could attract interest in enforcement of independent judicial statutory interpretation. But appendix p. 37a n.6 expressly finds a statutory challenge forfeited. The reply, pp. 10–12, disputes the breadth of that finding and distinguishes statutory phrases; that dispute itself makes the vehicle less clean. I have not independently read the underlying appellate briefs to settle preservation.

Together these considerations support P(any grant) = 0.18. Roughly 0.15 of total probability rests on a cert-order resolution and 0.03 on plenary review, consistent with the 0.83 conditional summary-route claim after rounding. The number is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted statistical estimate. The main uncertainty is whether the Court regards the intervening concessions as sufficient reason to let the lower court reconsider despite its existing waiver analysis.

## Other judgments and limitations

The 0.28 additional-distribution probability allows for closer consideration of the GVR and preservation questions without confusing one existing distribution with an existing relist. The 0.005 CVSG probability reflects that the Solicitor General's substantive views are already before the Court as counsel for respondents. The 0.07 conditional denial-writing probability allows some judicial interest in jury waiver or agency deference, but the companion litigation has already provided a vehicle for airing those concerns. These are subjective probabilities; the pack publishes no matched forward baseline for them.

The significance score of 0.62 reflects nationwide privacy obligations, major penalties, and administrative-law implications, independently of grant likelihood. The already-decided framework, terminated programs, and vehicle issues keep it below a foundational constitutional controversy.
