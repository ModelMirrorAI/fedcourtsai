## In brief

Sprint and T-Mobile ask the Supreme Court to revisit FCC fines over customer location-data practices, citing a later Supreme Court ruling on similar forfeiture orders.

## What happened

The FCC fined Sprint and T-Mobile a combined $92 million for allegedly failing to protect customers' location data sold to third parties through location-based service programs. The companies paid the fines and challenged them in the D.C. Circuit, arguing the location data was not the type of customer information covered by federal privacy law, that the FCC wrongly split one violation into many to avoid a penalty cap, and that the process violated their right to a jury trial. The D.C. Circuit denied their petitions, upholding the fines and the FCC's interpretation of the statute. After that decision, the Supreme Court decided a related case, FCC v. AT&T, involving similar forfeiture orders and the same legal questions.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked, first, whether it should send this case back to the lower court for reconsideration in light of its ruling in the related AT&T case and the government's changed legal positions there. Separately, the companies ask whether the data describing where a mobile device is located counts as protected customer information even when the device was not making a call, and whether courts—rather than the FCC—should independently decide how many separate violations a single ongoing failure amounts to when calculating penalties.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed: the companies filed their petition, the federal government filed a brief opposing review, and the companies filed a reply. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, and the docket does not yet show any decision from that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Sprint and T-Mobile, as petitioners, are asking the Court to act. If the Court grants the request to send the case back (a GVR), the lower court would reconsider the forfeiture orders in light of the AT&T decision without the Supreme Court deciding who is right, potentially affecting whether the companies get a refund. If the Court instead takes up the broader legal questions and eventually rules, affirming would leave the fines and the FCC's legal interpretations in place; reversing would favor the companies' arguments. If the Court denies review entirely, the D.C. Circuit's decision upholding the fines stands.
