## In brief

Sprint and T-Mobile ask the Court to undo FCC fines for mishandling customer location data, citing a later Supreme Court ruling on similar penalties.

## What happened

The FCC fined Sprint and T-Mobile a combined $92 million for selling customers' location data to outside companies without adequate safeguards, after some of those companies misused the data. The companies paid the fines and challenged them in the D.C. Circuit, arguing the location data was not the type of information the law protects, that the FCC miscounted violations to avoid a statutory cap on penalties, and that the process violated their right to a jury trial. The D.C. Circuit rejected all these arguments and upheld the fines. While the companies' appeal to the Supreme Court was pending, the Court decided a related case involving similar FCC fines against other carriers.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to send the case back to the lower court for a fresh look in light of its recent ruling in a similar case, or instead to decide two questions directly: whether the kind of location data at issue counts as protected customer information under the law, and whether courts, rather than the FCC, should independently decide how many separate violations a company's conduct represents when a law caps the penalty for a single ongoing violation.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including the government's brief opposing review and the companies' reply, and was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Sprint and T-Mobile, as petitioners, are asking the Court to overturn the D.C. Circuit's decision upholding the fines. If the Court grants the request to send the case back, the lower court would reconsider the case without the Supreme Court deciding who is right, in light of the newer ruling. If the Court instead grants full review and later rules for the companies, the fines or the legal reasoning behind them could be struck down or narrowed; a ruling for the FCC would leave the fines in place. If the Court denies review entirely, the D.C. Circuit's decision upholding the $92 million in fines stands.
