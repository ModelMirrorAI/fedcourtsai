## In brief

Sprint and T-Mobile ask the Court to review FCC fines for mishandling customer location data, or send the case back for another look given a related ruling.

## What happened

The FCC fined Sprint and T-Mobile a combined $92 million for failing to protect customers' location data, which third parties misused without customer consent. The companies paid the fines and asked a federal appeals court to overturn them, arguing the data was not covered by the relevant privacy law, that the penalty exceeded a statutory cap, and that the process violated their right to a jury trial. The D.C. Circuit rejected all these arguments and upheld the fines. While the case was pending further review, the Supreme Court decided a related case, FCC v. AT&T, involving similar forfeiture orders against other carriers.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether it should send this case back to the lower court for reconsideration in light of its ruling in the related AT&T case and the government's legal concessions there. If it does not do that, the companies ask the Court to decide two further questions: whether the customer location data at issue counts as protected information under the law only when tied to phone calls specifically, and whether courts, rather than the agency itself, should decide how many separate violations a company's conduct amounted to for purposes of a cap on penalty amounts.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including the government's brief opposing review and the companies' reply, and was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, then redistributed for a conference on November 13, 2026. No order on the petition has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Sprint and T-Mobile, as petitioners, are asking the Court to act. If the Court sends the case back for reconsideration, the lower court would take another look without the Supreme Court deciding who is right, in light of the intervening AT&T decision. If the Court instead grants full review, it would hear arguments and decide the legal questions itself, with no outcome yet determined. If the Court denies review, the lower court's decision upholding the $92 million in fines against Sprint and T-Mobile stands.
