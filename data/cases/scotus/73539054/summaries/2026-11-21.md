## In brief

Sprint and T-Mobile ask the Court to revisit forfeiture penalties the FCC imposed for mishandling customers' location data, citing a recent related ruling.

## What happened

The FCC fined Sprint and T-Mobile a combined $92 million for failing to protect customer location data that third parties misused without consent. The companies paid the penalties and challenged the FCC's orders in the D.C. Circuit, arguing the data was not protected information under the law, that the penalties exceeded the statutory cap, and that the process violated their right to a jury trial. The D.C. Circuit denied their petitions, upholding the FCC's orders in full. While their request for rehearing was pending, the Supreme Court decided a related case involving similar FCC forfeiture orders against other carriers. Sprint and T-Mobile then asked the Supreme Court to take their case too.

## What the Court is being asked

The companies raise three questions: first, whether the Court should send the case back to the lower court for reconsideration in light of its recent decision in a similar case, without deciding who should ultimately win; second, whether the data-protection law covers only information about where a customer was during a phone call, rather than all location information a device generates; and third, whether courts, rather than the FCC, should decide how many separate violations occurred when a company fails to fix a single ongoing problem, since that determines how large a penalty can be.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a government brief opposing review and a reply from the companies. It has been distributed for the Court's conference twice, most recently in November 2026, and the docket shows no action on it since.

## What each outcome would mean

Sprint and T-Mobile are asking the Court to overturn the D.C. Circuit's decision upholding the FCC's penalties. If the Court grants the petition, it could send the case back for further review in light of the recent related decision, without deciding the outcome, or it could take up the case fully to decide the data-protection and penalty-calculation questions. If the Court denies the petition, the D.C. Circuit's ruling stands, leaving the $92 million in penalties against Sprint and T-Mobile in place and upholding the FCC's interpretation of the law.
