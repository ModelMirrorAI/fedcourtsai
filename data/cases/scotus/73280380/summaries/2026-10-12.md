## In brief

Attorneys sanctioned for filing a 2022 lawsuit challenging Arizona's election procedures ask the Court to review whether those sanctions were proper.

## What happened

Attorneys for two candidates in Arizona's 2022 elections sued county and state election officials, seeking an order requiring hand-counted paper ballots instead of electronic voting systems. A federal district court dismissed the suit for lack of standing and later sanctioned the attorneys under court rules governing truthful pleadings and vexatious litigation, finding they had made false or misleading claims and pursued relief that could not practically be granted so close to the election. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the sanctions in a divided decision, and later declined, over several judges' dissent, to rehear the case as a full court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a judge may impose sanctions partly to discourage future lawsuits a judge views as undermining public confidence, whether sanctions may rest on legal arguments merely because a court sees them as novel or unlikely to succeed, whether a complaint must be read in the way most favorable to the filer when a court decides whether to sanction its authors, and whether seeking a court order affecting an election must be timed with the same caution as a request to change election rules shortly before a vote.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference multiple times, most recently for October 9, 2026, after the Court requested and received a response from the opposing side; the docket does not yet show a ruling on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The attorneys, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on the sanctions questions and decide them; granting review decides nothing in advance about who should win. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision upholding the sanctions against the attorneys stands, and the denial would not mean the Court agrees the sanctions were proper—only that the lower court's ruling remains in effect.
