## Case-specific adjustment

The upward consideration is substantial subject-matter importance. QPs 1 and 2 ask whether Sullivan actual malice is a prerequisite to employee-speech balancing and whether that rule resolves the academic-speech question reserved in Garcetti. The retrieved Garcetti majority confirms that it left application of its official-duty analysis to scholarship and teaching undecided. See *Garcetti v. Ceballos*, 547 U.S. 410, 425 (2006), CourtListener lead opinion 9434945. That gives the academic-freedom premise a real foundation; it does not establish that petitioner's proposed actual-malice solution is correct or that this case cleanly presents it.

The most important downward consideration comes from the petition itself. Its procedural history, printed pages 8–9, says the Sixth Circuit affirmed because Pesta did not show retaliatory causation: termination was attributed to research misconduct rather than the content or viewpoint of his paper. It also says the appellate court did not explicitly reach the district court's alternative Garcetti rationale. A decision expanding protected academic speech would therefore not necessarily remove the stated basis for judgment. My vehicle assessment is an inference from these admissions, not independent verification of the appellate opinion.

The petition's answer is largely to dispute the assessment of the record and to characterize causation as disguised balancing (printed pages 15–18). Its claims about university bias, approvals for restricted data, and the scientific importance of the study remain advocacy here. I do not endorse its scientific claims. QP 3 is especially tied to competing accounts of misconduct, disruption, and motive rather than a clean legal conflict.

The asserted split also has a mismatch. On printed page 12, the petition acknowledges that the cited disagreement about how knowingly or recklessly false speech enters balancing is not precisely its proposed across-the-board actual-malice prerequisite. The cited concurrence and examples of differing treatment do not, on this record, establish square holdings on an outcome-determinative question presented by this judgment. Combined with the waived response and no recorded request to respond, these problems outweigh the issue's general importance. I reduce the approximately 5.12% anchor to 3%, while retaining a meaningful possibility that the Court sees a useful academic-freedom vehicle despite them.
