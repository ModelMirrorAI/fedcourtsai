## In brief

A former tenured professor asks the Court to revive his First Amendment lawsuit over his firing from a public university for research misconduct.

## What happened

Bryan Pesta, a formerly tenured professor at Cleveland State University, published a study on genetics and cognitive ability using restricted federal data. After outside critics complained, the National Institutes of Health found he had exceeded the scope of his data-access approval, and the university disciplined him and eventually stripped his tenure and fired him. Pesta sued university officials, claiming the university retaliated against him for the content of his research rather than for any real misconduct. The district court ruled for the university officials, finding Pesta was fired for research misconduct rather than for his speech, and alternatively that the officials would have been within their rights even if speech content was the reason. The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, and it later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, before weighing a public employee's free-speech interests against a government employer's interests, courts must first require the government to show the employee's speech involved deliberate or reckless falsehood. The petition also asks whether that same falsehood requirement would resolve a question the Court left open in an earlier case about academic freedom at public universities, and whether the balancing test was properly applied to Pesta's situation regardless of which legal standard governs.

## Where it stands

The respondents waived their right to file a response. The petition was distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026, and the docket shows no action on it since.

## What each outcome would mean

Pesta, the professor, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the legal question itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision against Pesta stands, leaving intact the rulings that he was lawfully terminated and did not show his firing was based on the content of his speech.
