## Evidence raising the probability

The questions presented and petition, especially printed pages 14–23 and 30–33, identify a concrete dispute: must an employment-discrimination plaintiff expressly invoke motivating-factor causation at summary judgment, even when the evidence could support it? The petition contrasts the Ninth and Second Circuits with the D.C. and Eleventh Circuits. This is a legal preservation issue rather than merely a request to reassess workplace facts, and it recurs beyond this plaintiff. Experienced Supreme Court counsel presents a focused single question.

The provisioned decision below matches the petition: the D.C. Circuit's June 2, 2026 judgment in No. 24-5243 expressly declined to consider the motivating-factor theory because Tarquinii had not developed it in the district court. Thus the question was passed upon on appeal; the failure to raise the theory below is the challenged rule, not automatically an unrelated forfeiture defeating review. The judgment did not separately hold that the evidence necessarily failed under motivating-factor causation. The petition also identifies alleged discriminatory comments by the supervisors involved in termination, making its causation argument more than wholly abstract.

Independent primary-opinion checks lend substance to the conflict, with qualifications. In *McCreight v. AuburnBank*, 117 F.4th 1322, 1332 n.6, 1333 (11th Cir. 2024), the Eleventh Circuit required the mixed-motive theory to be raised by summary judgment, while distinguishing theories from claims and explaining why the actual arguments did not suffice. In *Bart v. Golub Corp.*, 96 F.4th 566, 574 n.2 (2d Cir. 2024), the court treated the single-/mixed-motive distinction as immaterial at summary judgment for the framework it was discussing. These support a real methodological tension. The Ninth Circuit authorities are assessed from the petition's quoted passages, not independently retrieved here.

## Evidence limiting the probability

The lower judgment is an unpublished, unanimous affirmance applying existing D.C. Circuit precedent, not a new published ruling or an en banc confrontation. The provisioned lower-court-docket extract records no rehearing petition in the single postjudgment entry it examined; that limited extract is not proof that no rehearing was ever sought. There is no recorded lower-court dissent.

The district-court opinion reproduced in the petition's appendix, particularly pages 26a–35a, supplies substantial vehicle friction. It describes an outside inspector-general investigation of nepotism, reviews independent support for the discharge, and finds no evidentiary nexus between discriminatory animus and termination. Those findings do not themselves establish failure under the unaddressed mixed-motive standard, but they give the respondent a plausible argument that correcting the preservation rule would not change the result. The plaintiff's largely factual pro se opposition also invites debate about failure to develop an argument versus failure merely to use a label.

Relatedly, the petition's shorthand can overstate the conflict. *McCreight* does not simply announce a magic-words requirement: it addresses what the plaintiff actually argued and distinguishes complaint-stage pleading from summary-judgment presentation. *Bart* establishes an integrated evidentiary approach more directly than it resolves the precise outer boundary of forfeiture. Different records might therefore explain part of the apparent disagreement. This federal-sector employment case could also require care before treating all private-employer precedents as interchangeable. I do not assume an unbriefed statutory issue supplies an independent ground of decision.

Finally, the waiver and first-distribution posture supply no affirmative signal that the Court has engaged the dispute. The Court could request a response, which would materially change the information set; it has not done so in this record. There is no identified lead case or intervening decision supporting a hold or GVR. These considerations offset most, but not all, of the split-and-importance adjustment: 6% is slightly above the frozen 5.17% anchor and appreciably above the no-amicus reference, not the double-digit probability I would assign a comparably framed conflict after a substantive response and a relist.
