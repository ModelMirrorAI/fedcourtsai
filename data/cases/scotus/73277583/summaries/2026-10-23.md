## In brief

Kendrick Beaird asks the Supreme Court to decide whether courts must defer to the Sentencing Commission's commentary when interpreting the federal Sentencing Guidelines.

## What happened

Beaird pleaded guilty to possessing a firearm after a felony conviction. At sentencing, the district court applied a sentencing enhancement based on commentary defining a "large capacity magazine" as one holding more than 15 rounds, which increased his guidelines range. Beaird argued courts should not defer to that commentary, but the Fifth Circuit held itself bound by circuit precedent requiring deference under this Court's 1993 decision in Stinson v. United States, and affirmed his sentence of 72 months. Beaird's petition to the Supreme Court also raised Second Amendment and Commerce Clause challenges to the underlying firearm statute, but the Court granted review only on the deference question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether its longstanding rule—that courts must give controlling weight to the Sentencing Commission's explanatory commentary on the Guidelines unless that commentary is plainly wrong—remains correct, particularly in light of a more recent decision limiting similar deference to agencies interpreting their own regulations. The question affects how much independent judgment courts must exercise when a Guideline's text and the Commission's commentary on it may point in different directions.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari limited to the deference question, and the case was argued on October 13, 2026. It now awaits decision. The United States, while defending Beaird's conviction, agrees with him that the lower court applied too much deference and asks the Court to send the case back for further review; a court-appointed attorney was asked to argue the opposing position, defending the existing deference rule.

## What each outcome would mean

Beaird, who lost below, asks the Court to reverse. If the Court holds that no deference (or only limited deference) is owed to the commentary, it would vacate the Fifth Circuit's decision and send the case back for the lower courts to reconsider the sentencing enhancement without simply deferring to the commentary—a result Beaird and the United States both support, though for the government this would not necessarily mean a lower sentence. If the Court instead holds that the existing deference rule still applies, as the court-appointed amicus urges, the Fifth Circuit's decision and Beaird's sentence would stand. The Court could also rule in a way that applies some, but not unlimited, deference, with consequences depending on how that standard applies to Beaird's case on remand.
