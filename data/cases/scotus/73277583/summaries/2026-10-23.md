## In brief

Beaird asks the Court to decide whether courts must defer to the Sentencing Commission's commentary explaining how its sentencing guidelines apply.

## What happened

Kendrick Beaird pleaded guilty to possessing a firearm as a felon. At sentencing, the district court applied a sentencing guideline enhancement for offenses involving a "large capacity magazine," based on commentary defining that term to include any magazine holding more than 15 rounds. Beaird's firearm held 17 rounds. He argued the court should not defer to that commentary, citing this Court's decision in Kisor v. Wilkie, which limited a similar deference doctrine for agency rules. The Fifth Circuit ruled that its own precedent required deference to the commentary regardless of Kisor, and affirmed Beaird's sentence. Beaird petitioned for review, also raising Second Amendment and Commerce Clause challenges to the underlying firearm statute, which the Court did not take up.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether its 1993 decision in Stinson v. United States, which told courts to treat the Sentencing Commission's explanatory commentary as binding unless plainly wrong, still states the correct rule, particularly after the Court's later decisions limiting similar deference given to agencies interpreting their own regulations.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to this question, and the case has been argued. The government, though a respondent, told the Court it supports sending the case back for further proceedings. A Court-appointed lawyer was asked to argue in defense of the Fifth Circuit's approach, since neither Beaird nor the government did. The case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Beaird petitioned the Court to review his sentence. If the Court holds that courts owe no deference, or only limited deference, to the commentary, it would set aside the Fifth Circuit's decision and send the case back for the lower courts to reconsider whether Beaird's magazine really counts as "large capacity" without simply following the commentary's definition. If the Court holds that the commentary still controls as the Fifth Circuit found, Beaird's sentence would stand. The government and Beaird both ask the Court to send the case back; the Court-appointed amicus curiae defends the result below.
