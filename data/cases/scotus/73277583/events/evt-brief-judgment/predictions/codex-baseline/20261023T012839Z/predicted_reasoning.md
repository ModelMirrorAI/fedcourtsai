# Forecast of the Court's judgment

The Court will vacate the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remand. The judgment below will be disturbed, but the Court will not itself order a lower sentence or reverse Beaird's felon-in-possession conviction. I forecast a unanimous vote for vacatur: Roberts, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson in the majority, with Thomas and Gorsuch concurring in the judgment rather than joining the majority's retained-deference rationale.

The majority will hold that Stinson's analogy to deference toward an agency's interpretation of its own regulations brings with it Kisor's limits. A court must first exhaust ordinary interpretive tools and identify genuinely ambiguous guideline text before treating interpretive commentary as controlling; permissible deference will also depend on the interpretation's reasonableness and the requisite authority, expertise, and considered judgment. The Fifth Circuit's separate, more reflexive Stinson regime will not survive that analysis. The majority will retain a bounded role for deference rather than eliminate deference to all interpretive commentary. Thomas and Gorsuch will favor independent judicial interpretation without binding deference.

The rule will apply across interpretive Guidelines commentary, not merely the firearm guideline at issue. Its application to this particular sentence will remain for remand. The Court will not decide whether a standard seventeen-round magazine is a large-capacity magazine under Section 2K2.1(a)(3), whether existing circuit precedent independently sustains that interpretation, or whether any calculation error was harmless. Vacatur accordingly will not promise Beaird eventual resentencing.

The Court will reach only the granted commentary-deference question. It will not resolve the petition's Second Amendment or Commerce Clause challenges. The expected procedural route is a reasoned merits opinion in the October Term 2026 decision cycle, most likely by June 2027, rather than a cert-order disposition. A DIG is a secondary risk because of the potential obstacles to practical relief, not the expected exit; the provisioned record supplies no recusal basis for an equally divided result.
