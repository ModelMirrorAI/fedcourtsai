## Legal and factual posture

The Fifth Circuit affirmed a 72-month sentence and rejected the Guidelines-commentary challenge as foreclosed by circuit precedent. The petitioner's opening merits brief, statement of the case at pages 8–10, explains the disputed calculation: application of § 2K2.1(a)(3) and its commentary to a magazine capable of holding 17 rounds increased the range from 51–63 to 63–78 months. Those are facts attributed to the provisioned filing, not an independent examination of the sentencing record. The appendix confirms that the appeal's commentary issue was foreclosed by Martin. The Court granted only the Stinson question, not the other attacks on the firearm conviction.

The petitioner seeks independent interpretation without controlling deference, or alternatively Kisor's constraints, followed by vacatur and remand. His opening brief's Parts I–IV and reply distinguish Guidelines, policy statements, and commentary; emphasize procedural safeguards for Guidelines amendments; and argue that the Court's recent deference decisions undercut Stinson. This is a substantial textual and institutional argument, not merely an objection to a particular numerical cutoff.

The United States also requests vacatur, but on a narrower basis: Stinson incorporates Seminole Rock deference, so Kisor clarifies its limits, including genuine ambiguity after conventional interpretation. Its opening summary at pages 11–14 and remand discussion at pages 28–32 stress that commentary would nevertheless frequently merit controlling weight. Its reply rejects simply relabeling all commentary as policy statements. The parties' agreement on vacatur is evidence, but the appointed amicus supplies a genuine defense of the judgment. Agreement between the litigants does not itself settle the appropriate interpretation.

The historical authorities preserve the central distinction. Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36, 38, 42–46 (1993), gives interpretive commentary authority subject to constitutional, statutory, and inconsistency limits. Kisor v. Wilkie, 588 U.S. 558, 574–579 (2019), limits deference to agency regulatory interpretations; it does not itself decide this sentencing case. Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), distinguishes implied delegation from authority actually delegated by statute. Whether the sentencing statute supplies that distinction here is contested, and is the substance of my forecast rather than an established legal conclusion.

## Why P(disturbed) is 0.28 rather than the 0.698 anchor

The transcript supplies unusually strong case-specific evidence against the ordinary reversal/vacatur prior:

- **Roberts**, early in petitioner's argument, questions why advisory Guidelines require a separate interpretive exercise rather than identifying the Commission's recommendation and moving to § 3553(a). His later question about express delegation leaves room for a statutory rationale preserving Stinson.
- **Sotomayor** challenges why Kisor changes this framework at all and presses the government on disruption from its purportedly small doctrinal adjustment. **Jackson** repeatedly defends a common Commission-specified baseline and resists treating commentary as separable from the recommendation it explains.
- **Kavanaugh** repeatedly invokes Stinson, Williams, congressional practice, and Booker's advisory framework. His closing questions to the appointed amicus concern how to preserve Stinson without weakening Loper Bright or Kisor. That sounds more like testing the boundaries of an affirmance than seeking a ground for vacatur.
- **Alito** probes practical consequences of overruling Stinson and the Commission's procedures. **Kagan** presses the relationship with Williams and the policy-statement category. Their questions support preservation more than a wholesale shift to independent interpretation, though Kagan's precise rationale remains uncertain.
- **Barrett** tests whether § 1B1.7 supplies the relevant instruction, whether changing the label alters the substance, and whether the government's distinction really changes Stinson. Her concern about treating commentary as policy statements cuts the other way, making her the least secure member of my predicted majority.
- **Gorsuch** directly attacks the claimed statutory delegation and the conceptual coherence of binding interpretation of advisory Guidelines. **Thomas** questions why courts would have less interpretive freedom with Guidelines than with statutes. These are the strongest dissent signals; Thomas's position is less explicit than Gorsuch's.

Bench questions are not votes. I discount a simple count because multiple Justices may be testing consequences, a coalition may coalesce around the government's narrower Kisor approach, and preserving Stinson in substance does not guarantee affirming this particular judgment. Nevertheless, several Justices criticize not only abolition of deference but also the government's proposed modification. The government's oral willingness to accept continued Stinson deference further weakens the inference from its formal request for vacatur. This justifies a substantial downward adjustment from the unconditional merits disturbance rate.

My rounded judgment distribution is **affirmed 0.70, vacated 0.26, reversed 0.01, mixed affirmance/reversal 0.01, DIG 0.02**, with negligible weight on an equally divided Court absent a nonparticipation signal. Thus P(disturbed) is exactly **0.28**. The main loss scenario is vacatur for application of Kisor, not a direct determination that Beaird must receive a lower sentence. A DIG belongs in the undisturbed complement. The 7–2 vote forecast is less certain than the judgment direction, particularly for Barrett and Thomas; it is not a claim that the dissent necessarily agrees on every rationale.

## Stakes and remaining uncertainty

The **0.76 big-case score** reflects the grant's systemic reach across federal sentencing commentary and its relationship to administrative deference, rather than the likelihood that Beaird wins or the size of his individual range dispute. The broad statutory-delegation rationale is less secure than retention of substantial commentary authority: the Court could instead rely primarily on statutory stare decisis or issue a fractured rationale. The semantic claims deliberately choose a specific majority basis and a generally applicable but Commission-confined scope, rather than disguising those uncertainties as a survey of every possible holding.
