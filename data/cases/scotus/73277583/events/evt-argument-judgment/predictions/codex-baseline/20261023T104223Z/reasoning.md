I read the case-level snapshot `record/snapshots/2026-10-14.json` and `record/context.json`. The baseline ends before October 14, 2026 and includes the October 13 argument. ## Anchor and adjustment

The grant date falls in October Term 2025, notwithstanding argument in October Term 2026. The applicable ten-Term baseline window is therefore 2015–2024 inclusive. The committed `metrics/statpack.md` merits section publishes the required excluded-count column; `metrics/statpack.json` supplies the matching counts. Within that window the pack has rows for 2017–2024, not 2015–2016. Pooling the available rows gives 360 disturbed judgments out of 516 parsed judgments, or 69.77%, exceeding the 30-judgment floor. Those 516 parsed judgments cover 539 qualifying grants, or 95.73%; 56 cert-order dispositions excluded before the cohort are not part of the 539. I used these as committed-artifact counts, not a claim about the live corpus's present contents. I did not access the corpus service or independently establish a current corpus-wide pull/snapshot vintage. Missing earlier rows are not evidence of zero grants, and the gap between parsed and granted can include pendency as well as parse gaps.

I move sharply below that generic disturbance prior to P(disturbed) = 0.24 because the argument contains unusually concrete, repeated support for affirmance across an apparent majority. This is an evidentiary adjustment, not a fitted model or a measured historical effect of oral-argument questions. Certiorari's grant, seven pre-grant distributions, and the context's `high` salience band do not establish a merits result and did not supply my anchor.

The legal baseline was checked against Stinson v. United States, 508 U.S. 36, 38, 42–46 (1993), CourtListener opinion 112854. Its actual rule makes explanatory commentary authoritative subject to constitutional, statutory, and guideline-consistency limits. Its reasoning includes Williams and an expressly imperfect analogy to agency interpretation of regulations. That combination supports both sides' competing descriptions; the precedent alone does not settle whether Kisor must now supply an ambiguity threshold.

## Why the argument changes the call

Roberts challenged the need for an additional interpretive exercise when the resulting range is advisory and distinguished the Commission from executive agencies. Alito questioned how a court could select a magazine-capacity threshold without the Commission's policy judgment and pressed disruption and harmless error. Kavanaugh repeatedly developed the Williams/1B1.7 basis for Stinson, emphasized stability after Booker, and asked the amicus how affirmance could be framed without weakening Loper Bright or Kisor. Those were not just generic requests for clarification: taken together they sketched a route to a decision retaining Stinson.

Sotomayor directly resisted both no-deference and Kisor-based changes, stressing workable administration and statutory stare decisis. Kagan explored the policy-statement authority and whether changing commentary treatment would also undermine Williams. Jackson repeatedly emphasized a common sentencing baseline and the distinction between ascertaining the Commission's recommendation and choosing whether to follow it. These exchanges make the three liberal Justices' support for retaining substantial deference more likely than an ordinary ideological model of criminal sentencing might suggest.

Barrett is the important uncertainty within my predicted majority. Her questions about the Commission's ability to adopt an express guideline on commentary, practical procedural similarities, and probation-office reliance fit retention of Stinson. But she also tested the government's attempt to reclassify commentary as policy statements, and the present wording of 1B1.7 is less explicit than her hypothetical. I treat her as a probable, not certain, vote for affirmance.

Gorsuch's sustained objections to both government and amicus were much stronger and more directional than the other Justices' exploratory questions: he disputed implicit delegation, questioned the express-delegation rationale, emphasized the distinction between considering and following guidance, and resisted reliance and disruption arguments. I predict his dissent. Thomas's questions were fewer and less determinative. His skepticism of the amicus's account and his statutory-versus-guidelines hypothetical support a dissent forecast, but Thomas is the least secure assignment in my lineup. An 8–1 affirmance is a substantial alternative. Bench questions are not votes, and the 7–2 point lineup is appreciably less certain than the binary prediction of an undisturbed judgment.

The government's alignment with Beaird deserves weight in the other direction: both litigants request vacatur, and Kisor offers a narrower corrective than abandoning deference. But the Court-appointed amicus supplies genuine adversarial defense of affirmance. Moreover, in argument the government acknowledged that retaining Stinson would serve its policy interests, while maintaining its legal argument for Kisor. I regard that exchange as weakening the practical push for the government's proposed middle course, not as a withdrawal of the government's request for vacatur.

## Remedy uncertainty and calibration

I allocate judgment probabilities as follows: affirmed 0.73; vacated 0.22; reversed 0.015; affirmed-in-part-reversed-in-part 0.005; DIG 0.025; equally divided affirmance 0.005. The disturbance mass is exactly 0.24. These are subjective calibration allocations, not separately scored claims.

The key distinction is between leaving the Fifth Circuit's judgment standing now and Beaird ultimately serving the same sentence after a remand. The district court imposed 72 months after the disputed enhancement moved the range from 51–63 to 63–78 months. Its statement that it would impose the same sentence is a reason a later remand might not benefit him, but it does not make a Supreme Court vacatur an affirmance. The government expressly seeks vacatur even while arguing Beaird may lose on existing precedent, guideline interpretation, or harmless error afterward. I therefore leave meaningful probability on vacatur despite the weak prospect of a shorter sentence. A majority could also clarify Stinson's limits while affirming; that would weaken my semantic forecast even though the binary call was correct.

The stakes score is 0.59: this is broader than an isolated firearm enhancement because the rule reaches federal sentencing commentary generally, but narrower than a decision invalidating a firearms statute or resetting administrative deference across the executive branch. My forecast of a generally applicable commentary rule is separate from my forecast of its doctrinal basis, as reflected in the two semantic claims. No author or separate-writing assignment is necessary to the judgment and lineup prediction.
