## 4. Adjustments from the Base Rate: Why Oral Argument Flipped the Case from Vacatur to Affirmance

Starting from the **69.8%** disturbed base rate, the pre-argument briefing posture initially pointed toward an even higher probability of vacatur (`vacated`): after opposing certiorari in its BIO, the United States filed a merits brief *Supporting Vacatur*, arguing that because *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), borrowed the deference standard of *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410 (1945), the Court's clarification of *Seminole Rock*'s inherent limits in *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558 (2019), applies to Guidelines commentary, and the Fifth Circuit erred in *United States v. Vargas*, 74 F.4th 673 (5th Cir. 2023) (en banc), and *United States v. Martin*, 119 F.4th 410 (5th Cir. 2024), by refusing to apply *Kisor*.

However, the **October 13, 2026 oral argument transcript (`argument-transcript.txt`)** dramatically shifted the balance from `vacated` to `affirmed` (driving my **P(disturbed) down from 0.70 to 0.38**, i.e., **P(affirmed) = 0.62**):

1. **Broad Cross-Ideological Bench Resistance to Both Petitioner and the SG's *Kisor* Position:**
   At least six Justices—Chief Justice Roberts, Justice Sotomayor, Justice Kagan, Justice Kavanaugh, Justice Jackson, and Justice Alito—pushed back forcefully against *both* Petitioner's call to overrule *Stinson* and the Solicitor General's proposal to import *Kisor* into the Sentencing Guidelines commentary framework:
   - **Chief Justice Roberts** immediately questioned Petitioner's premise on the first question of the argument (Tr. 6): *"Of course, after Booker, the guidelines are advisory. And I'm not sure why we should invite the lower courts to engage in somewhat it strikes me as an academic exercise of interpreting. Why don't they just look at what the Commission meant in the guidelines and move on to the 3553(a) factors? I just think -- I'm not quite sure that, you know, Stinson is not a more workable regime."* He later added (Tr. 20) that he did not see what *Stinson*, *"which involves an agency in the judicial branch and issues non-binding advice, really has much to do with Loper Bright at all."*
   - **Justice Kavanaugh** echoed the Chief Justice (Tr. 10), observing that *"Stinson plus Booker together suggests that we've already been down this road, it's been that way for 30 years. 1B1.7 of the -- of the guidelines itself says the commentary has the force of law... So, given all that, why are we reinventing the wheel when Stinson did this 30 -- 30-some years ago?"* When questioning Deputy SG Feigin (Tr. 85–86), Justice Kavanaugh warned that modifying *Stinson* would trigger *"years and years of trying to figure out how much weight you give to particular commentary"* and called the SG's *Kisor* position *"a solution in search of a problem,"* noting that *"stability's better served by amicus's position, not yours."* Most tellingly, during Court-appointed amicus Anthony Dick's argument (Tr. 118), Justice Kavanaugh asked for drafting advice: *"if I end up agreeing with your position, I would be concerned about making sure that we do anything to -- to undermine Loper Bright or Kisor, and I would want to make that crystal-clear. Your advice on how to do that."*
   - **Justice Sotomayor** defended *Stinson*'s grounding in U.S.S.G. § 1B1.7 and *Williams v. United States*, 503 U.S. 193 (1992) (Tr. 11–12), and sharply criticized the SG's *Kisor* proposal (Tr. 75–77): *"for this sliver of cases, you're asking us to create chaos in the courts below? You're asking us to potentially open up 66,000 cases last year... It's not the question of different results. It's the process you're changing. And what you're opening up is a whole new avenue of legal debate about whether something's ambiguous, unambiguous... That's what Kisor would do. Right now, courts just follow it and then choose if there is an argument about a meaning in the guideline to depart."*
   - **Justice Jackson** repeatedly argued across all three rounds (Tr. 7–9, 43–49, 57–59, 89–92, 102–104, 120–121) that Guidelines text and commentary are an inextricably linked package expressing the Commission's non-binding baseline recommendation, and that superimposing *Kisor* contradicts Congress's goal of sentencing uniformity and data collection.
   - **Justice Kagan**—the author of *Kisor*—stressed that *Stinson*'s opening pages rested on *Williams* and 28 U.S.C. § 994(a)(2)'s express authorization of policy statements regarding application of the Guidelines before ever mentioning *Seminole Rock* (Tr. 16–18, 28–31, 65–66), and pressed Deputy SG Feigin on whether switching to *"Kisor land, which is a little bit less deferential,"* would itself provoke a wave of resentencing demands (Tr. 67–68).
   - **Justice Alito** stressed that defining terms like "large capacity magazine" is *"essentially a policy call by the Sentencing Commission"* rather than a legal question a judge can answer with dictionaries (Tr. 21–24), emphasized the advisory post-*Booker* landscape and the district court's explicit statement that it would impose the same 72-month sentence regardless (Tr. 25–26), and raised alarm over the thousands of resentencings that unsettling *Stinson* could trigger (Tr. 60–62).
   - **Justice Barrett** expressed concern about probation officers calculating Presentence Investigation Reports without being able to treat Application Notes as authoritative (Tr. 42), noted that commentary goes through notice and comment and congressional submission alongside the Guidelines (Tr. 40–41), and questioned Deputy SG Feigin on why the Court needed to modify *Stinson* with *Kisor* when *Stinson* itself already precludes commentary that is flatly inconsistent with a Guideline (Tr. 69–71).

2. **The Deputy Solicitor General's Remarkable Concession at Argument:**
   When pressed by Justices Sotomayor, Kavanaugh, and Jackson on why the Court should not reject importing *Kisor* and instead adopt the Court-appointed amicus's position preserving *Stinson* intact, Deputy SG Feigin explicitly gave the Court permission to affirm on three separate occasions:
   - To Justice Sotomayor (Tr. 77): *"if the Court wants to say that the guidelines don't need to follow Kisor, I mean, as -- as a policy matter, I think we'd be very happy with that because it would ensure the kind of consistency that the kind of caricatured version of Stinson would provide. We're simply working with the Court's precedents as best we can."*
   - To Justice Kavanaugh (Tr. 86): *"yes, there would certainly be less upheaval under that. And as I mentioned to Justice Sotomayor, if the Court wants to go that way, I mean, frankly, in -- in -- in many policy respects, we'd prefer that approach."*
   - To Justice Jackson (Tr. 90): *"if the Court wants to do that, I -- I -- I don't have any real push-back for you."*
   Once the Solicitor General signals at the lectern that the United States would actually *prefer* the Court-appointed amicus's rule on policy and stability grounds and only argued for *Kisor* out of doctrinal caution, the institutional friction against affirming against the SG's brief drops dramatically.

3. **Only Two Justices Clearly Favored Disturbing the Judgment:**
   Only **Justice Gorsuch** (Tr. 31–34, 62–65, 78–80, 105–116) and likely **Justice Thomas** (Tr. 10, 56, 93–94, 114–115) signaled support for overruling or displacing *Stinson*. Even recognizing that oral argument questioning is an imperfect proxy for final votes, a bench where six or seven Justices actively resist both vacatur theories—and where a pivotal center-right Justice (Kavanaugh) explicitly asks the Court-appointed amicus how to draft the opinion affirming so as not to undercut *Loper Bright* or *Kisor*—points strongly toward `affirmed`.

## 5. Key Uncertainties and Why P(disturbed) Remains 0.38

Despite the strong oral-argument tilt toward affirmance, I keep **P(disturbed) at 0.38** (rather than < 0.25) for two reasons:
1. **The *Vargas* Dicta / *Kisor* Compromise Route (`vacated`):** In *United States v. Vargas*, 74 F.4th 673, 682 (5th Cir. 2023) (en banc), the Fifth Circuit wrote that *"under Stinson, the commentary controls even unambiguous guidelines."* Even Justices who want to preserve broad deference to Guidelines commentary (such as Justices Barrett, Kagan, and Kavanaugh) agreed at argument that commentary cannot override *unambiguous* Guideline text that cannot bear the construction (Tr. 69–71, 81). As I forecast in `predicted_reasoning.md`, the Court can easily make that point while **affirming** under *Stinson*'s own text (*"inconsistent with, or a plainly erroneous reading of, that guideline"* and *"if the guideline which the commentary interprets will bear the construction,"* 508 U.S. at 38, 46). However, there remains a ~35–38% chance that at conference the Justices decide the cleanest doctrinal vehicle to disapprove *Vargas*'s "even unambiguous guidelines" language is the SG's formal synthesis—holding that *Kisor* clarifies *Stinson*, stressing that Guidelines commentary almost always satisfies *Kisor*, and formally entering a **vacatur and remand** (`vacated`) so the Fifth Circuit can apply that clarified framing (or harmless error) on remand.
2. **Low Risk of DIG:** Because the district court stated that it would impose the same 72-month sentence regardless of the Guidelines range (J.A. 21) and Application Note 2 (>15 rounds) would pass muster under almost any deference test, one might wonder about a DIG, but the Court granted certiorari specifically on Question II knowing those facts (which the BIO highlighted at pp. 13–14) in order to resolve the deep 6–6 circuit split on *Stinson*. Thus, a merits opinion is overwhelmingly likely.
