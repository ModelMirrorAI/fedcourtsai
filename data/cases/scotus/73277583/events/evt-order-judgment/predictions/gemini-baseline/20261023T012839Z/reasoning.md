# Merits Prediction Reasoning: *Kendrick Jarrell Beaird v. United States* (No. 25-5343)

## 1. Procedural Posture and the Grant Order

On April 20, 2026, after seven conference distributions, the Supreme Court granted Kendrick Jarrell Beaird's in forma pauperis petition for a writ of certiorari limited to Question 2:

> Whether *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), still correctly states the rule for the deference that courts must give the commentary to the Sentencing Guidelines.

The Court denied review on Question 1 (facial and as-applied Second Amendment challenge to 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1) under *Bruen* and *Rahimi*) and Question 3 (Commerce Clause challenge to § 922(g)(1) under *Alderman*).

Below, Beaird pleaded guilty in the Northern District of Texas to possessing a firearm as a felon in violation of 18 U.S.C. § 922(g)(1). Probation enhanced his base offense level under U.S.S.G. § 2K2.1(a)(4)(A) based on a prior Texas conviction for possession of a controlled substance with intent to deliver under Tex. Health & Safety Code § 481.112(a). Because Texas defines "deliver" to include "offering to sell," Beaird objected that his prior conviction was an inchoate offense outside the text of U.S.S.G. § 4B1.2(b) (2021) (before the November 1, 2023 amendment moving inchoate offenses into § 4B1.2(d)), and was included only via Application Note 1 to § 4B1.2. Under the en banc Fifth Circuit's decision in *United States v. Vargas*, 74 F.4th 673 (5th Cir. 2023) (en banc), *Stinson* remains binding irrespective of *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558 (2019), so Guidelines commentary is authoritative unless it is flatly inconsistent with the Guideline text, even when the Guideline text is unambiguous. Bound by *Vargas*, the Fifth Circuit panel summarily affirmed Beaird's conviction and 46-month sentence (App. 1a–2a).

Crucially, the docket record also discloses that **Anthony J. Dick** of Jones Day was appointed as **Court-appointed amicus curiae** to defend the judgment below.

## 2. Why the Judgment Below Is Almost Certain to Be Disturbed (`probability = 0.88`, `judgment = "vacated"`)

### A. Neither Party Defends the Fifth Circuit's *Vargas* Rule on the Question Granted

In its Brief in Opposition (at 10–11), the Solicitor General explicitly agreed with Beaird that the Fifth Circuit's en banc rule in *Vargas* is wrong on the threshold legal question:

> "The government agrees with petitioner that *Kisor* sets forth the authoritative framework for realizing whether to defer to the Sentencing Commission's commentary—a framework under which a court defers to the commentary only when the relevant guideline itself is genuinely ambiguous after exhausting the traditional tools of construction." (BIO 10–11.)

The Solicitor General opposed certiorari only on vehicle and forward-looking importance grounds: (1) arguing that the Sentencing Commission's November 1, 2023 amendment adding § 4B1.2(d) prospectively placed inchoate offenses in the Guideline text itself, and (2) contending as an alternative merits defense that Texas "offering to sell" or § 4B1.2(b) still warranted affirmance even under *Kisor* (BIO 11–13).

However, the Court rejected the government's vehicle arguments and granted certiorari limited strictly to the abstract deference question ("Whether *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36 (1993), still correctly states the rule for the deference that courts must give the commentary to the Sentencing Guidelines"). Furthermore, the appointment of a Court-appointed amicus curiae confirms that the Solicitor General is not defending the Fifth Circuit's holding that *Stinson* operates unconstrained by *Kisor* (or *Loper Bright*).

### B. Doctrinal Inevitability: *Stinson* Expressly Rested on *Seminole Rock*

In *Stinson v. United States*, 508 U.S. 36, 44–45 (1993), the Court considered several analogies for Guidelines commentary (legislative history, advisory committee notes, and agency constructions of legislative rules) and expressly held that "the commentary be treated as an agency's interpretation of its own legislative rule," citing *Bowles v. Seminole Rock & Sand Co.*, 325 U.S. 410, 414 (1945). *Stinson* adopted *Seminole Rock*'s formulation verbatim: commentary is authoritative "unless it is plainly erroneous or inconsistent with the regulation" (i.e., the Guideline).

When the Supreme Court cabined *Seminole Rock* and *Auer v. Robbins* in *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. 558, 573–580 (2019)—and actually cited *Stinson* in footnote 4 as one of the cases applying the *Seminole Rock* doctrine—it held that a court may not defer to an agency's interpretation of its own regulation unless the regulation is "genuinely ambiguous" after exhausting all "traditional tools of construction."

Six circuits (the Third, Fourth, Sixth, Ninth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits in cases such as *Nasir*, *Campbell*, *Riccardi*, *Castillo*, *Maloid*, and *Dupree*) recognized that *Kisor* modified *Stinson*. Six other circuits (including the Fifth Circuit in *Vargas*, 74 F.4th at 680–683) held that because the Supreme Court in *Kisor* did not expressly overrule *Stinson*, lower courts remain bound by *Stinson*'s broader "not inconsistent" standard under *Rodriguez de Quijas* vertical stare decisis.

Once the question is before the Supreme Court itself, the vertical stare decisis barrier vanishes. Even the *Vargas* majority rested heavily on the premise that it was for the Supreme Court, not the Fifth Circuit, to declare that *Kisor* modified *Stinson*. Across the current bench:
- **Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson** all accept at minimum that *Kisor* governs *Seminole Rock*/*Auer* deference and requires genuine textual ambiguity before any deference attaches to commentary.
- **Justices Gorsuch and Thomas** (and potentially **Kavanaugh** and **Barrett**) went further in *Kisor* and *Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo*, 603 U.S. 369 (2024), criticizing *Auer*/*Seminole Rock* deference altogether, and in criminal sentencing specifically, Justice Gorsuch and Justice Thomas have repeatedly emphasized the rule of lenity and separation-of-powers concerns when unconfirmed or non-legislative commentary expands criminal punishment.
- Only **Justice Alito** (or possibly a tiny minority) might be inclined to preserve *Stinson* in its pre-*Kisor* form on the institutional theory advanced by *Vargas* and *Maloid* that the Sentencing Commission is a judicial-branch entity whose Guidelines and commentary work together differently from executive-branch regulations, or would vote to affirm on alternative grounds that Beaird's Texas conviction qualifies even under *Kisor*.

### C. Why `vacated` Rather Than `reversed` Is the Modal Judgment

When the Supreme Court rejects a court of appeals' threshold deference standard or legal framework without resolving the downstream application of that standard to the specific provision in the first instance (exactly as it did in *Kisor v. Wilkie*, 588 U.S. at 590–591, where it vacated the Federal Circuit's judgment and remanded for the lower court to apply the reinforced *Auer* standard), the standard disposition is **vacated and remanded** (`vacated`).

Here:
1. The Court granted certiorari **only** on the general *Stinson* deference question, not on the specific question whether Texas "offering to sell" under Tex. Health & Safety Code § 481.112(a) falls within the text of pre-2023 U.S.S.G. § 4B1.2(b).
2. In the Fifth Circuit, Beaird moved for summary affirmance recognizing that *Vargas* foreclosed his challenge, and the Fifth Circuit issued a two-paragraph per curiam opinion holding that his arguments were foreclosed (App. 1a–2a). Neither the district court nor the Fifth Circuit ever conducted the *Kisor* inquiry (whether § 4B1.2(b) is genuinely ambiguous, whether "deliver" or "controlled substance offense" encompasses an offer to sell, or whether the government's alternative harmlessness or textual arguments hold).
3. As the Court regularly states, it is "a court of review, not of first view." Once it holds that *Stinson* does not permit deferring to Guidelines commentary absent genuine ambiguity under *Kisor* (or holds that *Stinson* is overruled/modified), the natural disposition is to **vacate** the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remand for further proceedings consistent with its opinion.

### D. Calibration Against the Merits Base Rate and Residual Undisturbed Scenarios

From `metrics/statpack.md` (Merits decisions on cert-granted cases, OT2017–OT2025), the historical disturbed rate across prior Terms (OT2017–OT2024, strictly prior to the OT2025 grant Term) is roughly **79.3%** (435 disturbed out of 548 parsed argued grants across OT2017–OT2024).

This case sits significantly **above** the ~79% pooled merits base rate because:
1. The grant specifically targets a circuit rule (*Vargas*) resting on lower-court vertical stare decisis reluctance to apply *Kisor* to *Stinson*.
2. The Solicitor General confessed error on the Fifth Circuit's legal rule (agreeing that *Kisor* governs *Stinson*), necessitating a Court-appointed amicus curiae. Historically, cases where the SG agrees with the petitioner on the threshold legal error of the court below and the Court appoints an amicus to defend the judgment have an exceptionally high vacatur/reversal rate (exceeding 85%).

Why not higher than 0.88 (i.e., what accounts for the remaining 12% `undisturbed` probability mass)?
1. **Alternative-ground affirmance (`affirmed`, ~8%):** The Solicitor General argued in the BIO (at 11–12) that even under *Kisor*, Beaird loses because either (a) offering to sell under Tex. Health & Safety Code § 481.112(a) is an act of distribution or attempted transfer covered by § 4B1.2(b), or (b) § 4B1.2(b) is ambiguous enough to trigger *Kisor* deference to Application Note 1. If the Court reaches the application to § 4B1.2(b) rather than remanding, it could agree with Beaird on the *Kisor* standard yet **affirm** the Fifth Circuit's judgment on the ground that the error was harmless or the result unchanged.
2. **Institutional distinction preserving *Stinson* (`affirmed`, ~3%):** A majority could be persuaded by the Court-appointed amicus (echoing the Tenth Circuit in *Maloid* and parts of the Fifth Circuit en banc in *Vargas*) that the Sentencing Commission is a unique judicial-branch body that promulgates commentary alongside Guidelines through notice-and-comment and congressional review, rendering *Kisor*'s administrative-law restrictions inapposite.
3. **Procedural exit / DIG (~1%):** Because the Sentencing Commission amended § 4B1.2 in November 2023 to move inchoate offenses into the text (§ 4B1.2(d)), there is a small residual risk of a dismissal as improvidently granted if a complication arises, though the Court was fully apprised of the 2023 amendment in the BIO before granting Question 2.

Putting these together, I assign `probability = 0.88` that the judgment below is disturbed, with `vacated` as the modal judgment label (`judgment = "vacated"`).
