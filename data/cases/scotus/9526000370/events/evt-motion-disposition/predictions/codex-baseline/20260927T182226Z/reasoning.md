## Why the application moves far above that anchor

The applicants are state corrections officials seeking to prevent a substantial institutional transfer, not an ordinary individual litigant seeking error correction. The application says the receiver will assume healthcare control for roughly 25,000 prisoners on October 19, before the Ninth Circuit's December argument. Its account describes a minimum five-year appointment and broad personnel, budget, contracting, and operational authority. The scope of the transfer is also apparent in the appended appointment order. The prospect of transferring control and then returning it after appeal makes a preservation-of-control stay concretely attractive rather than purely symbolic. See application pp. 3–5, 13–15, 29–31 and the available appointment-order text.

There is a plausible legal route that does not require rejecting prison-condition remedies altogether: whether this receivership is sufficiently necessary and minimally intrusive under the PLRA, given intervening leadership changes, increased resources, and the injunction's implementation schedule. Those claimed improvements come from the applicants, not an independent audit. Their CASA-based historical-equity argument supplies an additional review-worthy issue, but I treat its extension to this receivership as an argument, not as a holding already settling the case. I did not independently verify the application's other recent-case characterizations. The forecast rests primarily on the scope and timing of the remedy and the competing record, not on assuming those characterizations are conclusive.

## Why the probability is not higher

The application is advocacy, and its appendix materially qualifies its presentation. The district court's August 5 order says intermediate measures included monitoring, hearings, status conferences, and a staffing pilot; it disputes the State's compliance methodology and finds ongoing constitutional injury. The available February receivership opinion places the remedy after almost fourteen years of failed correction and describes preventable deaths, suicides, and untreated pain. Increased spending or a new administration does not itself disprove those findings. See application App. 3–6 and App. 22–23. A stay risks prolonging grave injuries to people who cannot obtain alternative care. The State's account of a short delay is therefore not a reason to discount prisoner harms to zero.

The September 1 Ninth Circuit order denies a stay but expedites the appeals. Crucially, Judge Forrest's partial dissent favors an **administrative stay and referral to the merits panel**, not an unequivocal stay for the full appeal. I give that procedural disagreement limited weight. An expedited appellate remedy also supports letting that court address the dispute first. Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434–35 (2009), independently located through CourtListener, places special weight on likelihood of success and irreparable injury; an important state interest alone is insufficient.

Balancing the distinctive institutional stakes and imminent transfer against the adverse factual findings and demanding stay standard produces a moderate grant lean, not high confidence. The 0.62 is my judgmental forecast, not a fitted estimate for a measured sovereign-applicant subgroup. Its large departure from 0.105 depends on those case-specific distinctions; the strongest reason it could be wrong is that the Court credits the lower court's necessity findings and sees the appeal as the appropriate place to resolve the factual dispute.
