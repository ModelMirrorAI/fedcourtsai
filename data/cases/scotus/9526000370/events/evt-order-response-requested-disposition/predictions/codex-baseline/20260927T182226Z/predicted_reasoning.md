# Forecast of the Court's action

The Court will grant the application to stay implementation of the Arizona prison-healthcare receivership pending appellate proceedings. My probability of an unqualified grant in the disposing entry is **0.60**. This does not forecast reversal of the underlying constitutional findings or elimination of the existing healthcare injunction. A temporary administrative pause alone would not establish the predicted substantive disposition, and a mixed grant-and-denial order belongs to the ungranted side of this forecast.

The September 18 record already shows Justice Kagan's request for a response, due September 25 at 4 p.m. EDT. It shows no referral to the full Court and no Supreme Court amicus entries. I forecast referral to the full Court with probability **0.93**. The already-satisfied response-request rung receives **0.00** for its vacuous increment; this is not a claim that no response was requested. I forecast a rise above zero amicus entries with probability **0.65**, most likely one to three additional qualifying entries or distinct submitted briefs. The amicus filing mentioned in the Ninth Circuit appendix is not a Supreme Court filing and does not change the frozen count.

I expect a disposing order before the receivership's scheduled October 19, 2026 start, most likely during the first half of October after the response and any reply. The September 25 response deadline is a fact in the baseline, not evidence that a response actually arrived. I do not forecast another request as a necessary intermediate step.

The likely basis for interim relief is preserving state control while the appellate courts assess whether this unusually extensive transfer of governmental authority satisfies the PLRA's remedial limits. The planned transfer of personnel, contracting, and budget authority makes a later reversal operationally difficult to unwind. An interim order can preserve the existing injunction while postponing the receivership; it need not decide whether federal courts categorically lack authority to impose such a remedy. I expect a short order rather than a full merits opinion resolving that broader question.

The principal contrary route is denial in light of the district court's findings that prolonged noncompliance continues to injure prisoners and that lesser interventions have failed. That is a substantial alternative, not an assumption that the receivership would cause no countervailing benefit.
