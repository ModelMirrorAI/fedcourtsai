## In brief

Arizona officials ask the Supreme Court to pause a federal court order putting a private receiver in charge of state prison healthcare while their appeal continues.

## What happened

This long-running lawsuit began in 2012 when Arizona prisoners sued over prison healthcare conditions. A federal district court eventually found constitutional violations and issued a detailed injunction in 2023 requiring reforms. After finding continued noncompliance, the district court in 2026 ordered a receivership, stripping Arizona's corrections department of authority over prison healthcare and handing it to a court-appointed receiver for at least five years. Arizona appealed to the Ninth Circuit and sought a stay pending that appeal. Both the district court and a Ninth Circuit panel, over one judge's dissent, denied a stay, though the appeal itself was expedited with argument set for December 2026.

## What the Court is being asked

The state asks the Court to pause the receivership order while the appeal proceeds. It argues that placing a private receiver over a state prison system violates the rule that such an extreme remedy must be used only as a last resort, that the lower court skipped less intrusive options, and that a recent Supreme Court ruling on the limits of federal courts' equitable powers raises doubts about whether courts may impose receiverships on unwilling state institutions at all.

## Where it stands

The application for a stay was submitted to Justice Kagan, who requested a response from the prisoners, which was filed, followed by the state's reply. The docket shows no ruling yet on the stay request.

## What each outcome would mean

Arizona's corrections officials are asking for the stay. If granted, the receivership would be paused, leaving the department in control of prison healthcare while the Ninth Circuit appeal proceeds. If denied, the receivership would take effect as scheduled, transferring authority over healthcare operations to the court-appointed receiver during the appeal. Either way, the underlying appeal over whether the receivership itself was proper would continue in the Ninth Circuit.
