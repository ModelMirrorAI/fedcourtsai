## In brief

A former border officer convicted of using excessive force and falsifying a report asks the Court to overturn his convictions.

## What happened

Miguel Angel Delgado, Jr., a CBP officer, was convicted after a bench trial of two counts of depriving people of their constitutional rights under color of law and one count of falsifying a report to impede an investigation. The charges stemmed from two incidents in which Delgado used force against border crossers, causing injuries, and then gave what the trial court found was a false account of one incident. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, finding the evidence sufficient to support the convictions. Delgado petitioned the Supreme Court, and the government, opposing review, argues the lower court's fact-specific rulings were correct and create no conflict among courts.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the appeals court properly applied the rule that courts must weigh the full context of a police encounter, not just the instant force was used, when deciding if force was excessive. It is also asked whether proving a civil rights violation was willful requires showing the officer knew his conduct was unlawful, or whether proof that his conduct was merely unreasonable suffices; what level of injury is needed to turn such a violation into a felony; and whether the separate false-report conviction can stand given these disputes.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court asked for a response from the government, which was filed, along with several agreed deadline extensions, after a waiver of response was initially submitted. The case now awaits the Court's decision on whether to take it.

## What each outcome would mean

Delgado, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case and overturn his convictions. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision affirming his convictions and sentence stands. If the Court grants review, it will decide the case fully rather than simply deciding who wins; a grant does not itself overturn or uphold anything. The case would then proceed to briefing and argument on the merits.
