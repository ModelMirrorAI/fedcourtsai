## In brief

A former border officer convicted of civil-rights and obstruction crimes asks the Court to review how the Fifth Circuit assessed his conduct.

## What happened

Miguel Angel Delgado, Jr., a U.S. Customs and Border Protection officer, was convicted after a bench trial of depriving two people of their constitutional rights during use-of-force incidents and of falsifying a report about one incident. The trial court sentenced him to concurrent prison terms. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, finding the evidence sufficient to support willfulness, excessive force, and the falsification charge. Delgado now asks the Supreme Court to review that decision, arguing the appeals court misapplied Supreme Court precedent on excessive-force analysis, misjudged the intent required for the civil-rights crime, misapplied the injury threshold for felony liability, and wrongly upheld the falsification conviction as resting on the same flawed findings.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition raises four questions: whether the appeals court wrongly limited its constitutional excessive-force review to the moment force was used rather than the full situation, as the Court has said is required; whether a conviction for willfully violating someone's rights needs proof the officer knew he was acting unlawfully, or can rest on evidence only showing his conduct was objectively unreasonable; whether the injuries here were serious enough to support felony rather than lesser charges; and whether the records-falsification conviction can stand if it depends on those other findings being correct.

## Where it stands

The government waived its right to respond, but the Court later requested a response, which has now been granted two extensions and is due October 26, 2026. The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, and the docket does not yet show any action taken on it.

## What each outcome would mean

Delgado, the officer who was convicted, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide these legal questions, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision affirming Delgado's convictions stands, leaving his sentence and convictions in place. Either way, the decision to grant or deny does not itself resolve who is right on the underlying legal questions.
