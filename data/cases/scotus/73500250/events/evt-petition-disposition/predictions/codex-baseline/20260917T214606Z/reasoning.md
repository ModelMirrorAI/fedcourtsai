## Adjustments from 5.12% to 9%

**The response request is real positive evidence.** The Court affirmatively sought the government's position after a waiver. I move above the private baseline for that attention and for a potentially actionable claim of noncompliance with a recent Supreme Court decision. The uplift is judgmental: no response-request-conditioned cert rate was supplied, and I do not present the adjustment as an estimated likelihood ratio. It is not a CVSG, and the government's response could dispose of the concern.

**QP 1 is the strongest narrow route but has a substantial vehicle problem.** Barnes v. Felix, 605 U.S. 73, 79–84 (2025), rejects categorical exclusion of earlier relevant events from excessive-force analysis. The retrieved opinion supports that proposition and leaves the separate officer-created-danger issue open. The petition argues that the panel used broader context only for willfulness, not constitutional reasonableness (petition pp. 9–15). But the appended opinion actually discusses initial resistance, Delgado's escalation, the threat he perceived, his opening a secure door, and the absence of a weapons check within its constitutional analysis (Appendix A, pp. 11a–16a). That makes the asserted methodological conflict contestable. A failure to cite Barnes is not itself a demonstrated refusal to apply its rule. The appellate disposition was also a deferential sufficiency review following a bench trial, rather than adoption of an express temporal exclusion rule (Appendix A, pp. 1a–2a).

**QP 2 does not clearly establish opposing legal rules.** The petition's willfulness argument identifies a serious conceptual distinction between subjective culpability and objectively excessive force (petition pp. 16–21). Yet the panel separately states the willfulness requirement and relies on training, demeanor, and concealment as circumstantial evidence of that state of mind (Appendix A, pp. 9a–10a). Whether that inference is convincing on this record differs from replacing willfulness with negligence. The retrieved discussion in United States v. Cote, 544 F.3d 88, 100–101 (2d Cir. 2008), also treats angry contemporaneous statements as evidence supporting criminal intent, complicating a simple Second-versus-Fifth-Circuit divide. I have not independently verified every authority in the petition's claimed split.

**QP 3 overstates what its key comparator demonstrates.** The petition describes Cote as demanding an injury greater than de minimis (petition pp. 22–24). The retrieved Cote passage instead distinguishes the amount of force from the seriousness of injury: it permits the bodily-injury element without serious or significant injury where the force is not de minimis. That is not a verified categorical inter-circuit disagreement over the minimum severity of bodily injury. Moreover, the appended panel opinion describes a bleeding nose laceration and head symptoms, so the case is not obviously a clean test of liability for trivial contact (Appendix A, pp. 11a, 13a–14a). I do not infer that every possible injury-threshold issue is foreclosed; the alleged split is simply less clean than the petition presents.

**QP 4 largely repackages the factual dispute.** The panel identifies false contemporaneous explanations, report discrepancies, training obligations, and federal investigative review as support for intent to obstruct (Appendix A, pp. 17a–19a). The petition's dependency theory does not on this record establish a distinct conflict about Section 1519 requiring plenary review. Multiple related questions broaden the petition without necessarily improving its vehicle quality.

Taken together, the affirmative response request warrants a material uplift, but the appended opinion and the checked Cote passage restrain it. **9%** expresses a genuine but minority prospect of review; denial remains the clear modal outcome. The 60% conditional summary-route forecast allocates 5.4 percentage points of the total probability to relief in the cert order and 3.6 points to a grant opening further proceedings. It is a judgment about the narrow Barnes route, not a measured prior-Term summary-order baseline. Because Barnes was decided May 15, 2025, before the March 3, 2026 judgment below, I discount a routine intervening-precedent GVR.
