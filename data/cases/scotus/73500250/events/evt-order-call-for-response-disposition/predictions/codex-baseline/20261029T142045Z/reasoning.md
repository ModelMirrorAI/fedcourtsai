## Why the petition is more likely denied

1. **Barnes claim versus the opinion itself.** The petition, printed pp. 9–14, characterizes the appellate constitutional analysis as temporally confined. But the appended opinion, pp. 12a–16a, considers initial resistance, initiation and escalation of the encounters, opening the secure door, earlier threatening words, and the officer's failure to seek assistance or check for weapons. These discussions occur under the excessive-force/objective-unreasonableness headings, not exclusively under willfulness. That directly weakens the proposed categorical conflict with Barnes. Failure to cite Barnes does not by itself show refusal to apply its rule. This is a reading of the staged opinion, not a claim that the Justices have accepted its analysis.

2. **Willfulness is stated as a separate requirement.** The petition, pp. 15–22, alleges a split between subjective criminal intent and an effectively objective civil standard. The opinion at pp. 9a–10a expressly invokes open defiance or reckless disregard of a definite constitutional requirement and says circumstantial evidence can establish intent. Training, demeanor, and concealment are offered as bases for inferring intent; they are not textually declared substitutes for intent. The contested question therefore looks substantially like sufficiency of the evidence rather than an express rejection of Screws. The cases the petition cites may support a genuine disagreement, but I have not independently verified that they present opposing holdings on materially equivalent facts.

3. **The injury question may conflate different thresholds.** The petition, pp. 22–25, alleges three approaches to felony bodily injury, but its account of the Second Circuit emphasizes nontrivial force and its account of Brugman emphasizes constitutional injury. Neither description alone establishes a clean statutory disagreement over the felony enhancement. Actual bleeding from a nose laceration and the separately described neurological symptoms also make it uncertain that this defendant would prevail under the supposedly stricter rule. I treat the asserted split as advocacy requiring checking, not an established grant-level conflict.

4. **The record is fact-intensive and the report conviction adds a vehicle issue.** The opinion at pp. 7a–8a applies deferential evidentiary-sufficiency review. The petition challenges several inferences across two encounters after a bench trial, rather than isolating a single dispositive legal ruling. Its § 1519 challenge is framed as derivative, whereas the opinion at pp. 17a–19a separately discusses deliberate false statements, a written report, reporting training, and anticipated investigation. Success on one § 242 issue would not automatically settle the independent false-report count. The concurrent sentences shown in the record make this consequential to the requested relief, though they do not eliminate reviewability.
