Judge Kelly concurred in the judgment but thought this might be one of the rare records in which verification was objectively reasonable. The issue is preserved, and there is evidence on which an instruction could matter. That combination moves the petition above the baseline despite the government’s waiver. The waiver leaves the government’s argument on review unknown. (Question presented, petition pp. 3–9, app. 14a–18a, 26a.)

Three considerations limit the increase. First, the split dates to the Ninth Circuit’s 1988 decision, and the panel applied a rule the Eighth Circuit adopted in 2009. Second, even in the Ninth Circuit, the defendant must prove by clear and convincing evidence that he could not reasonably have learned the performer’s age. The producers in the Ninth Circuit case proffered official photo identification and other documents. Kucera principally cites representations by the minors and his co-defendant. His personal contact gave him the opportunity to check, the distinction the Supreme Court drew between producers and distributors in *United States v. X-Citement Video*, 513 U.S. 64, 76 n.5 (1994). Third, a new trial on the production counts would leave two trafficking convictions outside the question presented. The panel reports a 300-month total sentence, and the effect of relief on that total is uncertain. The trafficking verdicts themselves do not settle whether his mistake was reasonable because the instructions allowed conviction on proof of an opportunity to observe the minors.
