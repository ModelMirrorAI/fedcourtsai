## Petition against opposition

The question presented is whether the First Amendment bars consideration of viewpoints furthered by academic research when the government awards or terminates competitive grants (`questions-presented.txt`). The petition, pp. 11–24, says the Ninth Circuit's ruling conflicts with Rust and Finley, substantially restricts changes in grant policy, and affects recurring litigation beyond the University of California. The appendix's appellate opinion, particularly pp. 15a–25a, confirms that the constitutional issue is central to the surviving injunction, rather than an incidental argument.

The opposition's strongest cert argument is **the absence of a circuit split**, together with an opportunity for other courts to address the issue (BIO pp. 18–27). It also offers a substantial doctrinal answer: the government is not merely defining a program prospectively but selectively cancelling previously awarded research grants because of perceived viewpoints. Its invocation of Finley's reservation concerning discriminatory applications is not frivolous. Finley rejected the facial challenge while expressly leaving open as-applied objections involving invidious discrimination (524 U.S. 569, 586–587; verified against the CourtListener combined opinion, ID 118240, and the official U.S. Reports source identified in `retrieval.md`). That makes plenary consideration more plausible than an immediate summary reversal, even if four Justices find the government's alleged conflict worth reviewing.

The reply, pp. 3–9, addresses that distinction directly: in the government's view, permissibly refusing a subsidy initially and ending the same subsidy later should not have categorically different First Amendment consequences. It also argues that the case does not involve using funding to control independently financed speech. I treat these as contested legal arguments, not established holdings. The boundary between defining a competitive funding program and suppressing private viewpoints is sufficiently important and unsettled in this posture that review is attractive even without conflicting appellate judgments.

Vehicle quality supplies a further, limited upward adjustment. The petition reports a partial EPA settlement, overlapping NEH litigation, and separate litigation involving additional agencies, so I do not assume every original claim or agency remains live. But it identifies surviving NSF and NEH grants with expiration dates extending to November 2029 (petition pp. 11, 24). The reply reports that proceedings on the relevant claims have been stayed by agreement pending this petition and any Supreme Court proceedings (reply pp. 8–9). These reduce the immediate risk of mootness. The preliminary-injunction posture, possible alternative statutory grounds, and the contested scope of Tucker Act preclusion still prevent treating this as a certain grant. The government's jurisdictional discussion is its own characterization, not an independently resolved absence of jurisdictional difficulty.
