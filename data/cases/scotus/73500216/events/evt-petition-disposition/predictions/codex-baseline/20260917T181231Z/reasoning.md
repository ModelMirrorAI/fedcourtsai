## Why below the 5.12% anchor

1. **The procedural vehicle is restrictive.** This is an ineffective-assistance claim adjudicated on the merits in state court, then reviewed under Section 2254(d)(1). The Fifth Circuit assumed implied bias could qualify as clearly established law but nevertheless found the state decision reasonable. Developing a useful prospective constitutional rule would not automatically overcome that separate obstacle. Appendix A pp. 4a, 7a–10a supplies this reasoning; I am not asserting that AEDPA prevents all Supreme Court review.
2. **The split is less clean than the petition's headline suggests.** The petition itself acknowledges that no circuit treats prior victimization alone as sufficient. Its favorable examples involve additional features such as concealment, repeated equivocation during questioning, ongoing abuse, or victimization during the trial itself. Pearson's juror voluntarily disclosed her history before selection, and the record supplies little detail about that history. These differences leave considerable room for consistent outcomes under a shared extreme-circumstances standard. Petition pp. 11–14; Appendix A pp. 8a–9a.
3. **Independent precedent checking weakens a categorical circuit divide.** The en banc majority in *Fields v. Brown*, 503 F.3d 755, 774–75 (9th Cir. 2007), rejected implied bias in a similar-experience setting involving a juror's wife, emphasizing honest disclosure and opportunities for follow-up. CourtListener majority opinion 9565854, the passage beginning with its refusal to imply bias, confirms the petition's own contrary example. This is not identical to Pearson's personal victimization, but it undermines the claim that the Ninth Circuit necessarily would grant him relief. *Smith v. Phillips*, 455 U.S. 209, 222 (1982), confirms that the illustrative extreme-circumstances discussion is Justice O'Connor's concurrence; it is not itself a majority holding resolving this QP.
4. **The decision is unpublished and record-bound.** Appendix A expressly notes nonpublication. The panel relies on the paucity of detail, the voluntary disclosure, and its earlier *Buckner* decision. A broadly controlling categorical rule is not clearly presented. The absence of a provisioned BIO further limits confidence that the petition has identified a square, outcome-determinative conflict.

The counterweight is meaningful: a life-without-parole sentence, counsel's apparent failure to hear an important disclosure, a recurring constitutional issue, and no panel finding of nonexhaustion. Those prevent me from rounding the grant probability to zero. My 2% is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted quantitative model or a claim that the individual concerns are independent.
