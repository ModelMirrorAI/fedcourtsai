## In brief

Donnie Pearson asked the Supreme Court to hear his case about a juror's possible bias, but the Court declined to take it up.

## What happened

Pearson was convicted in Texas state court of a sex offense against a child. During jury selection, a juror sent a note saying she had been molested as a child but thought she could be fair; no one questioned her further, and defense counsel did not seek to remove her. She served on the jury that convicted Pearson. In later habeas proceedings, Pearson argued his trial lawyer was ineffective for not challenging or striking that juror. Texas courts and then a federal district court rejected the claim. The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding the juror was not the kind of biased juror the law presumes incapable of fairness, even though she had experienced the same type of crime as the one charged.

## What the Court is being asked

The case concerns when a juror who was previously the victim of a crime similar to the one on trial must be treated as automatically unable to be fair, even if she says she thinks she can be impartial. Federal appeals courts disagree on how to make that judgment. Pearson's petition asked the Court to set a clearer standard and decide whether his juror's situation qualified, which would in turn affect whether his lawyer's failure to remove her amounted to constitutionally inadequate representation.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Pearson, as the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case and ultimately rule that his juror should have been treated as biased, which could have helped his claim that his trial lawyer was ineffective. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Fifth Circuit's decision in place, meaning Pearson's conviction and the denial of his habeas claim stand. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fifth Circuit's reasoning; it simply declines to review the case further, leaving the lower court's ruling as the final word in this case.
