## In brief

The Court declined to hear a Texas prisoner's challenge to his conviction based on his trial lawyer's failure to remove a juror who disclosed past abuse.

## What happened

Donnie Ray Pearson was convicted in Texas state court of sexually assaulting a child and sentenced to life without parole. During jury selection, a juror sent a note saying she had been molested as a child but thought she could be fair; no lawyer questioned her further, and she served on the jury. Pearson later argued in state and federal habeas proceedings that his trial lawyer gave him ineffective assistance by not challenging or striking her. State courts and a federal district court rejected the claim, and the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding the juror was not presumed biased merely because she had suffered a similar crime.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a juror who was the victim of the same type of crime as the one being tried must be treated as automatically unable to be fair, when she herself only says she thinks she can be impartial rather than stating it without qualification. Pearson's petition says federal appeals courts are split on how to decide when a juror's past victimization requires this automatic disqualification, and asks the Court to set a uniform rule.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and on October 5, 2026 the Court denied it.

## What each outcome would mean

Pearson, as the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case. With the petition denied, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the rulings that rejected Pearson's ineffective-assistance claim and upheld the denial of habeas relief, so his conviction and sentence remain intact. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fifth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision as the final word in Pearson's case.
