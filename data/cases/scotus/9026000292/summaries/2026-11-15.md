## In brief

Families of terrorism victims and insurers ask the Court to let them seize blocked Afghan central bank funds to satisfy judgments against the Taliban.

## What happened

Petitioners are families of victims of the September 11 attacks and other terrorist acts, plus insurers that paid billions in 9/11-related claims, who together hold large court judgments against the Taliban. They sought to collect by reaching roughly $3.5 billion in blocked funds, held at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, belonging to Afghanistan's central bank, which the Taliban now controls. The district court denied their turnover requests, and the Second Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, ruling that the funds could be seized only if the bank already counted as a Taliban-controlled entity at the moment the funds were first blocked by the government, not later when the Taliban actually took over the bank. The full Second Circuit declined to rehear the case, over dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide, under the federal law letting terrorism victims collect judgments from a terrorist group's blocked assets, whether a plaintiff can reach assets that came under the terrorist group's control only after the assets were originally blocked, or whether the law applies only to assets already connected to the terrorist group at that earlier blocking date.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and then distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026; the docket does not show any action taken at that conference. Petitioners later filed a motion for leave to submit a supplemental brief, and the case was distributed again for a conference on November 13, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The families and insurers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to reverse the Second Circuit's ruling, which left the lower court's denial of turnover in place. If the Court grants review and later rules for petitioners, they could proceed to seize the blocked funds to satisfy their judgments against the Taliban. If it rules for the Taliban and the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, the funds would remain beyond petitioners' reach under the lower court's timing rule. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's decision stands, leaving the funds protected from execution under that rule.
