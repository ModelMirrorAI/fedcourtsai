## Upward and downward adjustments

**Reasons to move above 24.8%.** The question presented is a discrete question of statutory timing: must an asset owner's terrorist-party or instrumentality status exist when the assets were blocked, rather than when execution is pursued? The petition, pp. 16–20, identifies competing approaches in the Ninth and Eleventh Circuits and distinguishes the Second Circuit's new blocking-date rule. Its account of Stansell III is particularly relevant because it addresses when instrumentality status is measured; Bennett's asset-ownership analysis is less directly on point. These are petitioners' characterizations, not independently verified holdings: my CourtListener lookups did not locate the cited Stansell opinion.

The petition, pp. 14–16, describes a panel dissent and separate en banc dissents, including five judges across the en banc dissenting opinions. It attacks a rule allegedly adopted without adversarial briefing. The embedded panel opinion, App. 8a, independently confirms that the court announced its timing rule for the first time. The snapshot records support from Iowa and 30 other states, although I did not read that brief. Taken together, the developed statutory disagreement, substantial judicial dissent, and broad governmental interest warrant an upward adjustment. Financial importance alone would not.

**Reasons not to predict a grant.** The petition does not establish that another circuit has decided this same central-bank takeover and asset-blocking sequence. A dispute over past terrorist affiliation may not squarely conflict with this dispute over later affiliation. The embedded panel opinion distinguishes the pre-judgment plaintiffs from these judgment creditors, and finds TRIA overrides immunity but its instrumentality prerequisite unsatisfied. Thus I do not treat immunity as an unexamined, necessarily dispositive alternative ground already adopted against these petitioners. Nevertheless, the petition itself, pp. 12–14, records the district court's recognition-power rationale. Unresolved sovereign-identity and recognition questions could make correcting the timing rule insufficient to establish a right to these funds.

Retrieved source R1, Faiq's October 5 amicus opposing certiorari, pp. 8–20, disputes a square split and asserts independent immunity, recognition, and Afghanistan-versus-Taliban obstacles. It also contests a practical sanctions incentive behind petitioners' reading. These are advocacy positions, not adjudicated facts. R1 was docketed as an amicus, not a respondent's BIO. Its counterarguments temper the petition's account but do not establish that this Court would accept them. citeturn575440view0

The November 9 relist can reflect interest in review, preparation of a denial statement, or consideration of soliciting federal views. It does not identify which. These competing signals lead to 34%, an increase of about nine percentage points over the frozen anchor, rather than an above-even grant forecast.
