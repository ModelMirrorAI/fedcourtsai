## Petition strength and vehicle limitations

Question 1 asks whether presidential responsibilities warrant pausing the deadlines for the President's private civil claims. Question 2 challenges sanctions resting on advocacy of that position. The petition, pp. 9–17, argues conflict with this Court's precedents and institutional importance, rather than demonstrating a developed inter-circuit split on presidential tolling. Its Florida appellate citation, Alexander, supports presidential control of the timing of offensive litigation; the petition does not establish an opposing limitations holding on the precise question here. The institutional argument has force, especially where penalties might chill reasonable efforts to develop constitutional law. Novelty alone, however, does not establish a recurring disagreement requiring review.

The bound Eleventh Circuit opinion matches the lower-court docket numbers and November 26, 2025 judgment in the snapshot. It supplies an important counterweight to the petition's characterization. Appendix A, pp. 19a–21a, applies the ordinary extraordinary-circumstances and diligence framework to Trump's decision to delay filing, with Clinton v. Jones as additional support. The directly checked primary opinion in Menominee Indian Tribe of Wisconsin v. United States, 577 U.S. 250, 257 (2016), confirms that the extraordinary circumstance must be outside the litigant's control. The constitutional-accommodation argument therefore requests an extension or refinement of existing principles, not enforcement of an already established presidential tolling rule.

The sanctions vehicle is particularly imperfect. Appendix A, pp. 25a–32a, discusses forfeited notice and hearing arguments, deferential review of bad faith, abusive pleading, and other frivolous theories. At 31a the panel expressly sets aside the tolling arguments and identifies other unchallenged defective claims. These are the lower court's findings, not my independent findings about disputed conduct. Their presence means a favorable answer on the tolling advocacy question need not eliminate the sanctions judgment. The petition, pp. 16–17, responds that protected advocacy contaminated the fee award and invokes fee causation under Goodyear. That preserves a plausible narrower remand route, but does not remove the factual and independent-ground complications. Appendix A at 32a also records forfeiture of a particular fee objection; I do not equate that with forfeiture of every possible fee-causation theory.

The petition is 257 PDF pages and marked truncated, with explicit pipeline omission markers in later appendix items. Its own argument and the appellate passages relied upon above are available. I do not treat omitted appendix material as silence. There is no separate decision-below file or opposition to weigh, so uncertainty remains about respondents' presentation and the complete sanctions record. The missing opposition is expected at this moment, not a provisioning defect.

## Other elicited probabilities and stakes

The 99% distribution-increment probability concerns any further recorded distribution from the current count of one. It largely reflects ordinary processing after a requested response, not a 99% chance of a substantive post-conference relist; this moment masks that claim. A new CVSG is only 2.5%: the United States is already represented as a respondent, and its position can enter through ordinary respondent briefing without a separate invitation.

Conditional on a grant, I assign 35% to resolution in the cert order itself, leaving plenary review the more likely grant route. The petition expressly requests alternative vacatur and remand of the sanctions judgment, but identifies no intervening Supreme Court decision requiring a routine GVR. I assign 8% to a dissent or statement conditional on denial; a silent denial is much likelier. The independent order-writer probabilities are unconditional, allow overlap, and allow writings on a grant as well as denial. Thomas and Alito have the largest individual probabilities, but no Justice is more likely than not to write.

The stakes score of 0.65 reflects the institutional implications of presidential private litigation and sanctions against counsel, alongside unusually prominent parties. It is separate from review likelihood and restrained by the narrow limitations and fee-award setting.
