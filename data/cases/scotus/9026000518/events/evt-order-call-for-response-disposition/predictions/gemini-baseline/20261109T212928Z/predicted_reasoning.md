## Predicted Disposition (`denied`, P(grant) = 0.04)

The Court will deny the petition for a writ of certiorari once the response is received and considered at conference.

1. **Question Presented 1 (Article II Equitable Tolling of a President's Private Civil Claims):**
   - If the Court were to grant review, it would take either QP 1 and QP 2 together or limit review to QP 2 (inherent-authority sanctions). However, the Court will not grant QP 1 because the case is a poor vehicle with no circuit split.
   - As Chief Judge William Pryor's unanimous Eleventh Circuit opinion explains (Pet. App. 14a–18a), the dismissal of the civil RICO and predicate claims rests on an independent, non-constitutional ground: even if the four-year RICO statute of limitations were equitably tolled during the petitioner's first presidential term, the Amended Complaint still failed to state a valid RICO claim on the merits (failing to plead valid predicate acts under the Defend Trade Secrets Act, obstruction of justice, or wire fraud, and failing to plead enterprise or pattern). Because affirmance of the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal is independently supported by merits pleading deficiencies that the petition does not challenge, resolving QP 1 could not revive the dismissed claims.
   - Furthermore, there is no conflict among the courts of appeals on whether Article II responsibilities categorically or presumptively toll limitations periods for a sitting President's personal civil lawsuits as plaintiff, and the factual record below noted that petitioner initiated several personal lawsuits while in office.

2. **Question Presented 2 (Inherent-Authority Sanctions):**
   - The second question challenges nearly $1 million in inherent-authority sanctions imposed jointly and severally on the petitioner and his counsel (`Chambers v. NASCO, Inc.`, 501 U.S. 32 (1991)). While the call for a response reflects at least one Justice's interest in examining the substantial monetary sanction imposed below rather than letting the petition pass on waivers alone, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed the sanctions based on an extensive, fact-bound record of bad-faith litigation conduct—including shotgun pleadings, factual misrepresentations of public reports and indictments, and multiple meritless legal theories beyond the equitable-tolling argument (Pet. App. 20a–31a).
   - Because the sanctions order did not rest solely on the advocacy of the Article II tolling argument, the case does not cleanly isolate the legal question framed in QP 2.
