I adjust upward for the Court's actual call for a response, the institutional Article II issue, and the sanctions question's potential significance for constitutional advocacy. A requested response is a low-threshold sign of interest, not four votes to grant. The argument protecting a reasonable request to extend precedent is more plausible as a narrow cert vehicle than a general presidential exemption from ordinary filing deadlines. These considerations initially support moving meaningfully above 5.16%.

The strongest counterweight is vehicle quality. The petition identifies no square disagreement between courts of appeals over either question. Its claim of conflict with this Court is principally an argument that general separation-of-powers and sanctions principles were misapplied. Appendix A, pp. 19a–21a, applies the ordinary diligence and extraordinary-circumstances requirements and emphasizes the voluntary decision to defer suit. Clinton v. Jones, 520 U.S. 681 (1997), concerns litigation against a President rather than his own claims, so it is not a direct holding against QP1; nevertheless, its rejection of categorical private-litigation immunity is an obstacle to the requested extension. A CourtListener excerpt independently confirmed that Clinton permits case-management accommodation while rejecting a categorical term-long stay. I do not infer that official-act criminal immunity automatically extends to a private plaintiff's limitations period.

More importantly, the sanctions were not affirmed solely because counsel advocated presidential tolling. Appendix A, pp. 25a–32a, applies a subjective-bad-faith standard and discusses abusive pleading, independent frivolous claims, forfeited objections, and other litigation conduct. At pp. 30a–31a, the panel expressly finds other frivolous claims even setting tolling aside. That undermines the petition's framing of sanctions as punishment simply for advancing one unresolved constitutional argument. The opinion also identifies forfeiture of a fee objection at p. 32a. These are serious obstacles to presenting QP2 cleanly.

The petition, pp. 16–17, does engage this problem: it invokes Goodyear's causal limits on compensatory sanctions and argues that other defects do not establish the entire award. Its alternative request for a sanctions-only vacatur leaves a real, but small, intervention route even if QP1 fails. I therefore do not reduce the probability below the anchor or to near zero. Balancing the call for response against the record-specific independent grounds produces 9%, rather than either an unadjusted 5.16% or a high-tier grant probability. The broad corpus lookup returned mostly unrelated emergency applications and one capital petition; none supplies a matched Article II or sanctions analogue, and I give those rows no substantive weight.
