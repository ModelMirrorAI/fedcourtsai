Sources: metrics/statpack.md, modern-cert, originating-circuit, relist, CVSG, and sal-v4 per-Term sections.

I adjust substantially down from 16.888% to **1.5%**, for the following case-specific reasons. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted likelihood-ratio model.

1. **The apparent relist signal is weak here.** The first distribution precedes the August docketing and sits between the motion to proceed as a veteran and its denial. The later distribution follows ordinary cert response processing. This makes two recorded distributions poor evidence of two substantive considerations of the petition. I flag the issue but neither alter the record nor substitute another band. The forecasted increment still starts at two. Source: the provisioned snapshot's March 19, May 19, June 8, August 19, and August 26 entries.

2. **The petition's strongest cert argument overstates its cited split.** Question 1 concerns a generally important APA issue, but its supporting comparison is not as clean as claimed. In James Madison Ltd. v. Ludwig, 82 F.3d 1085, 1095 (D.C. Cir. 1996), the D.C. Circuit recognizes record supplementation for omitted adverse documents and background needed to determine whether the agency considered relevant factors. In Lands Council v. Powell, 395 F.3d 1019, 1030 (9th Cir. 2005), the Ninth Circuit lists four limited exceptions, insists they be narrowly applied, and warns against de novo review. The petition describes that passage as listing eight exceptions and portrays the two circuits in much sharper opposition. The retrieved passages instead show material overlap. That does not establish that all circuits agree or that the lower courts decided Jones correctly; it does reduce my confidence that this petition demonstrates a square, outcome-determinative conflict. Sources: petition, printed pages 27–29; CourtListener opinion 717539, supplementation passages; opinion 3029990, exceptions passages.

3. **The vehicle is fact-dependent.** The asserted error depends on which records and policy documents were before the agency, why particular documents were added, and whether they supplied new factual justifications or described governing authority. The petition itself quotes the district court applying a record-limited standard and reports the Fourth Circuit finding no reversible error. That presentation looks more like disputed application of a shared standard than a clear competing appellate rule. I have not independently read the lower opinions or the administrative record, so this is a vehicle assessment from the petition, not a finding that its factual allegations are false. Sources: petition, statement of the case, printed pages 1–4 and 17–22.

4. **The second question adds complications rather than a clean alternative.** It combines installation-access authority, the commander's asserted lack of authority over a former employee, debarment procedures, and record supplementation. The claim that absence of a criminal violation necessarily defeats administrative debarment needs more than the factual comparisons offered. I do not treat the petition's characterizations of military authority or constitutional protections as established law. Sources: questions-presented.txt and petition, printed pages 30–34.

5. **There is little affirmative attention signal.** The provisioned docket shows a response waiver, no subsequent request for a response, no CVSG, and no amicus entries. The context's amicus field is null, so I do not convert that into a verified zero. The waiver is not dispositive, but neither it nor the petition supplies a strong counterweight to the vehicle concerns. The petition invokes Loper Bright, which its own questions identify as a 2024 decision; it does not establish a new intervening decision after the 2025 appellate proceedings that compels a hold or GVR. Sources: snapshot; questions-presented.txt; petition, printed pages 21–26.

## Other probabilities and uncertainty

The **7% further-distribution** forecast is an increment from two, informed by the apparent fee-motion origin of the first distribution and weak substantive attention signals. The terminal relist table is useful population context but supplies no measured forward hazard for this posture. The **0.2% CVSG** forecast reflects the absence of a prior invitation and the government's existing respondent role; a requested response is not a CVSG. Both are subjective probabilities, not published conditioned rates.

The **30% summary-route** probability is conditional on a grant, not 30% unconditional and not multiplied into the claim field. If the Court sees a sufficiently clear record-review error, summary correction is plausible; if it takes the broader exceptions question, further briefing is more plausible. The **1% denial-writing** forecast is likewise conditional on denial. Neither number rests on a claimed case-specific measured rate.

The **0.25 significance score** concerns stakes if decided, not cert odds. A general rule limiting extra-record support for agency decisions could matter widely, while this vehicle concerns one person's installation debarment and bundles several individualized disputes. That combination supports modest but nontrivial significance.

Main limitations are the absence of a separately provisioned lower-court opinion or opposition, uncertainty about the administrative-record additions, and the mixed motion/petition distribution history. The petition also gives a November 11, 2025 appellate judgment date in its jurisdiction paragraph, whereas the snapshot gives October 16, 2025; I use the snapshot's date and do not infer a jurisdictional defect from this inconsistency. I would revise upward on a cleanly demonstrated conflicting appellate holding or a substantive response request, neither established by the evidence used here.
