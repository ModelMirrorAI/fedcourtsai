## Why 13%, rather than 5% or 30%

**Upward evidence:** Question 1 alleges a recurring disagreement about cumulative Strickland prejudice, with concrete opposing authorities, rather than merely dissatisfaction with a sentence. The petition distinguishes found from assumed deficiencies and identifies examples in the First, Second, Seventh and Ninth Circuits and Iowa against authorities in the Fourth, Sixth and Eighth Circuits (petition pp. 16–24). Review directly from the state postconviction judgment also avoids making AEDPA deference the immediate standard of review. The two-Justice state dissent on mitigation supplies evidence of a substantial disagreement about the sentencing record (petition pp. 14–15, 26–27; BIO pp. 14–15). The capital stakes and proposed-order allegations justify serious consideration above an ordinary baseline petition.

**Downward evidence:** The BIO raises a specific vehicle objection: the cumulative-error theory was not presented below, the opinion did not decide that legal question, and no rehearing petition was filed (BIO pp. 16–18). I treat this as the State's disputed contention, not a proven jurisdictional bar. Moreover, the BIO quotes lower-court language addressing evidence in conjunction and the overall balance, and says additional mitigation was duplicative or exposed damaging rebuttal (BIO pp. 13–17). This makes a factual disagreement over application of an accepted totality standard a live alternative to the petition's claimed categorical split. The State also distinguishes cases with established deficiencies from those assuming them (BIO pp. 20–22). Those responses do not conclusively dissolve the split, but they reduce this case's attractiveness as the vehicle to settle it.

Question 2 is independently troubling but less clean as a cert question. The petition describes an initial wholesale adoption, a state supreme court remand, and a largely nonsubstantive amended order; the judge reportedly retained errors deliberately to initial them (petition pp. 9–13). The BIO answers that there was an open process, an opportunity to respond, and an actual judicial reconsideration, and distinguishes Jefferson's ex parte features, disputed reliance on material outside the record, and pre-AEDPA deference posture (BIO pp. 23–26). The petition itself acknowledges important differences in the practices it compares (pp. 27–31). The state dissent, according to the BIO, did not dissent on this issue. Thus the question presents serious fairness concerns without an uncontested, square constitutional conflict.

These considerations produce **13% any grant**: materially above the matching 5.12% prior but well short of a prediction that four Justices will take this particular vehicle. I have not treated the gravity of a death sentence as synonymous with review likelihood. The missing reply is important because it may answer the preservation objection; the forecast should be discounted for that incomplete adversarial record.
