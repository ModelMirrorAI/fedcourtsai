## In brief

A South Carolina death row inmate asks the Court to review how courts assess ineffective-assistance claims and whether a judge's adoption of the state's proposed order violated his rights.

## What happened

Marion Lindsey was convicted of murdering his estranged wife and sentenced to death. He later sought post-conviction relief, arguing his trial lawyers inadequately investigated and presented mitigating evidence about his background and mental state. The state trial court denied relief by signing an order drafted by the state that contained numerous errors; South Carolina's Supreme Court sent the order back for revision, and the trial court then reissued largely the same order with only cosmetic changes. South Carolina's Supreme Court affirmed the denial of relief, over a two-justice dissent, concluding that Lindsey's lawyers' alleged shortcomings, evaluated one by one, did not change the outcome of his sentencing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in deciding if a defendant was harmed by his lawyer's mistakes, courts must weigh all the mistakes together rather than judging each one separately. It is also asked whether a judge in a death penalty case violates constitutional fair-process protections by adopting the state's proposed ruling essentially unchanged, without independently explaining his own reasoning.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including South Carolina's opposition and Lindsey's reply, and the case was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026. The docket does not yet show any decision from that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Lindsey, as the petitioner, asks the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on both questions and decide them, without presuming either side is correct. If the Court denies review, South Carolina's Supreme Court ruling upholding Lindsey's conviction, death sentence, and the denial of post-conviction relief remains in place, though that denial would not itself mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning.
