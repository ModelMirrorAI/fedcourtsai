## Why 0.62 rather than approximately 0.70

The strongest petitioner argument is the difference between imposing an obligation and enforcing a voluntarily accepted one. The petition describes explicit direct-payment and shortfall provisions and substantial up-front consideration (petition pp. 7–12, 24–28). The state opinion does not merely interpret ambiguous contract language: it says that even if the agreement required indemnification, federal law made that obligation unenforceable (App. 7a–8a). That categorical federal proposition offers a reviewable basis for vacatur while leaving the decree's meaning and remedies unresolved. The merits argument is stronger than a free-standing appeal to equitable hardship.

There are substantial contrary considerations. The decree expressly includes otherwise excluded benefits in its definition of military retirement, and the challenged order awards support corresponding to the lost benefits. The opposition contends that the relevant language appeared in a later order, that the trial court relieved petitioner from the old judgment rather than enforced it, and that involuntary disability retirement did not trigger the promise as petitioner claims. It also challenges arrears preceding the stated longevity-retirement eligibility date (opposition pp. 11–14). The reproduced state opinion says the support award did not follow the ordinary state support factors (App. 8a). Those are meaningful obstacles to treating this as a clean contract-enforcement case; the grant does not settle them.

Mansell v. Mansell, 490 U.S. 581 (1989), concerned a property settlement, so assent alone does not distinguish its prohibition. Its footnote 5 treats the relevant res judicata question as state law, not an automatic federal rescue. Howell v. Howell, 581 U.S. 214 (2017), rejects reimbursement terminology as a means of avoiding the substantive restriction. Both precedents were checked through CourtListener MCP. Respondent also advances the statute's inclusion of court-approved property settlements and the anti-assignment argument under 38 U.S.C. § 5301 (opposition pp. 19–21); I treat these as serious arguments, not assume that any label of contract defeats them. Direct official statutory retrieval returned no usable passage, so that discussion rests on the provisioned adversarial filings, not a claimed independent statutory verification.

On balance, the overly categorical state-law disposition makes a limited vacatur my leading judgment, while precedent and the vehicle problems push disturbance below the approximately 0.70 pooled prior. My judgment-class allocation is: vacated 0.40, reversed 0.19, affirmed-in-part-reversed-in-part 0.03, affirmed 0.29, dismissed-as-improvidently-granted 0.08, and equally divided affirmance 0.01. The first three total 0.62; the last three total 0.38. This is a judgmental allocation, not a fitted statistical model.

## Votes, breadth, and stakes

The exact 7–2 lineup is much less secure than the disturbance probability. A limited contractual distinction can support a cross-ideological majority without relaxing the prohibition on direct benefit division. I place Thomas and Alito on the opposing side because the respondent's strongest route is to apply the statutory restriction and the existing decisions to the substance of this decree. This is a forecast, not evidence of their views on this case. Thomas's separate opinion in Howell rejected purposes-and-objectives preemption but nevertheless agreed with its judgment; that leaves his application here particularly uncertain. I have no briefing or argument signal from any current Justice. The nine names were checked against the official Court biographies page. No writing role is claimed.

The semantic forecasts deliberately separate ground from breadth: enforceability of voluntarily assumed personal obligations is the predicted doctrinal distinction; remanding contract-specific application rather than authorizing assignments generally is the predicted limit. A purely procedural disposition, or a majority finding all such obligations preempted, would contradict those forecasts.

The **0.56 big-case score** reflects potentially recurring and substantial consequences for military families' negotiated financial security, while recognizing the bounded statutory setting. It does not reflect the already-known cert grant or a prediction of broad constitutional change.
