## Why 12%, rather than 5% or a likely grant

**Upward evidence:** the Court requested a response after the United States waived. That is case-specific attention absent from the band anchor and the strongest procedural reason to raise the probability. The questions also identify an actual disagreement about predicate elements. Independently retrieved United States v. Mannava, 565 F.3d 412, 415–16 (7th Cir. 2009), treats incorporated offenses' elements as necessary to preserve jury unanimity. United States v. Hart, 635 F.3d 850, 855–56 (6th Cir. 2011), expressly disagrees in the unanimity setting. Thus the petition's asserted conflict is not merely rhetorical. The repeated use of related statutory language gives the issue significance beyond one conviction. Sources: petition pp. 8–19 and the two CourtListener opinions.

**Limiting evidence:** the conflict is not yet demonstrated here as opposite holdings on the identical cumulative-conviction question. Mannava addresses unanimity; the retained First Circuit opinion says it joins the Sixth, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits in permitting these two convictions. Moving from the predicate-elements disagreement to a square double-jeopardy split requires an additional step. Most importantly, the lower court expressly states that this defendant did not raise the double-jeopardy objection at trial and receives **plain-error review** (Appendix A, pp. 36a–38a). That complicates the petition's claim to be a perfect vehicle, though it is not an absolute bar: the panel reached the elements question and rejected error at the first step (pp. 47a–49a).

The broader invitation to revisit legislative authorization of cumulative punishment is less focused than the statutory question. The petition's invocation of Barrett does not by itself establish a routine intervening-precedent GVR: the supplied panel opinion already cites and discusses Barrett (pp. 38a–39a). I have not independently verified that authority's full holding and use only its treatment in the supplied record. No identified later controlling decision supplies a mechanical remand route. The missing end of the appellate analysis and absence of the requested opposition limit confidence in vehicle quality and in the government's anticipated answer.

Taken together, the response request and genuine underlying disagreement justify a meaningful upward adjustment from approximately 5%, but the indirect split, plain-error posture, and incomplete adversarial briefing keep denial clearly most likely. **12% is my judgmental forecast, not an estimated response-request coefficient or a fitted model.**
