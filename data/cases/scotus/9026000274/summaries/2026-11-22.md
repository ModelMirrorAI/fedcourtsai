## In brief

A man convicted of child pornography and minor-enticement crimes asks the Court to review whether his convictions and sentences for both violate double jeopardy protections.

## What happened

Francisco Ortiz Colón was convicted after trial in federal court in Puerto Rico of producing child pornography and of coercing and enticing minors, based on conduct involving nine minors he contacted online. He was sentenced to 360 months in prison, with punishments imposed separately for both sets of charges even though, he argues, the production offense was also the "predicate" crime that made the enticement offense illegal. The First Circuit affirmed his convictions, joining several other circuits in holding that the two offenses have different elements and so can both be punished. Ortiz asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether punishing someone separately for both crimes, when one crime's violation is also the very fact that makes the other crime illegal, violates the constitutional ban on being punished twice for the same offense. A related question is whether the "offense a person could be charged with," which the enticement law refers to, counts as a required element of that crime or merely as one way of committing it — a distinction that affects whether the two convictions are legally the same offense.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference. The government was then asked to respond, and the deadline for that response has been extended twice, most recently to December 2, 2026; a further extension request from the government is pending.

## What each outcome would mean

Ortiz is asking the Court to take up his case and overturn the First Circuit's ruling. If the Court grants review and ultimately agrees with him, his sentence on one of the two sets of charges could be vacated, reducing his overall punishment. If the Court denies review, or grants review but affirms the First Circuit, his convictions and full sentence on both sets of charges would stand. A denial would not mean the Court found the lower court's reasoning correct, only that the case will not be heard.
