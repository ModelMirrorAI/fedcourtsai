## In brief

A man convicted of child pornography and minor-enticement offenses asks the Court to decide whether his convictions on both counts violated double jeopardy protections.

## What happened

Francisco Ortiz-Colón was convicted at trial in Puerto Rico on nineteen counts involving nine minor victims, including producing child pornography and coercing and enticing minors online, and was sentenced to 360 months in prison. On appeal, he argued, among other things, that convicting and punishing him separately for producing child pornography and for enticement, when the pornography production also served as the "offense" element of the enticement charge, punished him twice for the same conduct. The First Circuit rejected this and other claims, affirming his convictions, though it remanded on a separate issue. Ortiz now asks the Supreme Court to review the double jeopardy question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether convicting and punishing someone separately for two crimes violates the constitutional ban on double punishment, when one crime's conduct is also used to satisfy an element of the second crime that requires proving the person committed some chargeable offense. A related question is whether that "chargeable offense" requirement counts as a formal element of the second crime or merely as one possible way of satisfying it.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the October 9, 2026 conference, and the government was asked to respond, with the response now due December 2, 2026 after an extension. The Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Ortiz, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up and reverse the First Circuit's ruling that upheld his separate convictions and sentences. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments and decide whether his double punishment was constitutional, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the First Circuit's decision affirming Ortiz's convictions and sentence stands, and the government's position prevails.
