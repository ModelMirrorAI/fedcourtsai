## In brief

A man convicted of child pornography and minor-enticement offenses asks the Court to decide whether his convictions on both counted as unconstitutional double punishment.

## What happened

Francisco Ortiz Colón was convicted after trial of producing child pornography and coercing and enticing minors, among other counts, based on conduct involving nine minors contacted through social media. He received separate, concurrent sentences for both the production counts and the enticement counts, even though the enticement counts relied on the production conduct as the required underlying offense. The First Circuit affirmed, agreeing with several other appeals courts that the two offenses have different legal elements and so can both be punished. Ortiz asked the appeals court to rehear the case; it did not do so, and he then asked the Supreme Court to review the double jeopardy question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a person can be convicted and punished separately for two crimes when the second crime's definition simply incorporates the first crime as an element, or whether that amounts to punishing the same conduct twice in violation of the constitutional protection against double jeopardy. The petition also asks whether the incorporated offense is something the government must separately prove as an element, or just one of several ways the crime can be committed.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond to the petition, but after the case was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, the Court asked the government to respond by November 2, 2026. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Ortiz, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the double jeopardy question itself, which would not by itself determine who wins. If the Court denies review, the First Circuit's ruling upholding Ortiz's separate convictions and sentences stands, and the case ends in the government's favor. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was correct, only that the decision remains in place.
