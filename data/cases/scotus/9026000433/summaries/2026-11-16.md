## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a mother's case seeking to hold a police officer liable for photographing her dying son and sharing the image with colleagues.

## What happened

Karen Brookins's son, Marcus Boone, died after jumping from a highway overpass during a lengthy standoff with Philadelphia police. A responding officer, Christopher Culver, photographed Boone as he lay injured below and sent the photo to other officers; it later circulated on social media and reached Brookins. She sued Culver and the City under the Constitution's due process protections and state law. The district court found Culver's conduct violated Brookins's rights but granted him qualified immunity, a legal shield protecting officials from liability unless the law was already clearly established, because no prior case in that circuit had recognized the right. The Third Circuit affirmed, 2-1, with one judge dissenting in part.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks whether a person suing a government official for conduct so outrageous it "shocks the conscience" must only show that the conduct was outrageous in that way, or must also separately show that the conduct violated a specific right long recognized by history and tradition. Brookins argues several appeals courts allow the first showing alone to establish a violation, while others, including the court that ruled against her, require both.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed, and the respondents either waived their right to respond or that right was otherwise addressed; an amicus brief supporting Brookins was also filed. The Court considered the petition at its conference on November 13, 2026, and denied it on November 16, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Brookins asked the Court to take up and reverse the Third Circuit's decision, which had ruled against her and in favor of Culver and the City. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Third Circuit's decision in place: Culver retains qualified immunity, and Brookins's claims against him end. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling standing without the Supreme Court deciding the underlying legal question.
