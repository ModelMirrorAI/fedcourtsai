# Predicted Court Action: *Brookins v. City of Philadelphia, et al.*, No. 26-433

## Procedural Trajectory and Disposition

The Court will **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari at its November 13, 2026 conference without calling for a response or relisting the case.

1. **Distribution and Relist Forecast:** The docket currently records a single distribution (`distribution_count = 1`, distributed October 28, 2026 for the conference of November 13, 2026) following respondents' waivers of the right to respond (filed October 16 and October 20, 2026), with no prior relists and no Call for a Response (CFR). The petition will not be relisted or redistributed further; it will be disposed of on the first conference list. Although an amicus brief from Constitutional Law Professors was filed on October 29, 2026 and Judge Roth dissented in part below, the Court will not call for a response because the single question presented in the petition was concededly never raised or passed upon in the courts below, the Third Circuit's decision is unpublished, and the judgment below rests on the "clearly established" prong of qualified immunity.
2. **CVSG Forecast:** No Call for the Views of the Solicitor General (CVSG) is on the docket, and the Court will not issue one. This is a 42 U.S.C. § 1983 municipal and police-officer tort action arising out of local law-enforcement conduct in Philadelphia; it implicates no federal statute, federal agency program, treaty, or federal sovereign interest.
3. **Question Presented Forecast (Conditional on Grant):** The petition presents a single question: whether a plaintiff bringing a "shocks-the-conscience" claim against an executive official under the Due Process Clause must prove only that the official's conduct shocks the conscience (*Rochin v. California*, 342 U.S. 165 (1952); *County of Sacramento v. Lewis*, 523 U.S. 833 (1998)), or must also prove that the conduct invaded a fundamental right deeply rooted in history and tradition (*Washington v. Glucksberg*, 521 U.S. 702 (1997)). If the Court were to grant review, it would take that single question presented as written—or, if it opted for summary action instead, reformulate the inquiry to address the step-two qualified-immunity obviousness question under *Hope v. Pelzer*, 536 U.S. 730 (2002), and *Taylor v. Riojas*, 592 U.S. 7 (2020), that actually divided the Third Circuit panel below.
4. **Summary Disposition vs. Plenary Review (Conditional on Grant):** Conditional on a grant of certiorari, plenary review is likelier than a summary disposition in the certiorari order itself. No intervening Supreme Court decision warrants a grant, vacate, and remand (GVR), and while the Court occasionally issues per curiam summary reversals on egregious qualified-immunity facts under *Hope* and *Taylor*, Petitioner framed the petition solely around the doctrinal relationship between *Lewis* and *Glucksberg* rather than requesting summary reversal on the Third Circuit's step-two holding. If the Court granted on the question presented, it would set the case for full merits briefing and oral argument; if granted on the underlying qualified-immunity issue instead, it would issue a per curiam summary vacatur or reversal.
5. **Separate Writings on Denial:** The Court's order denying certiorari will issue without a noted dissent or statement respecting the denial. If any Justice were to write separately respecting the denial, it would most likely be Justice Sotomayor (potentially joined by Justice Jackson) addressing Officer Culver's conduct and the Third Circuit's application of *Taylor v. Riojas*, or Justice Thomas (or Justice Gorsuch) writing a statement respecting the denial on the relationship between *Lewis* and *Glucksberg* in substantive due process doctrine.

## Conditional Merits Outlook (If Granted)

If the Court were to grant plenary review on the question presented, it would affirm the judgment of the Third Circuit. A majority of the Court—consistent with *Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization*, 597 U.S. 215 (2022), and *Department of State v. Muñoz*, 602 U.S. 899 (2024)—would reject the proposition that executive action that "shocks the conscience" violates the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause without implicating any cognizable liberty or property interest, or would alternatively affirm on the threshold ground that Officer Culver is entitled to qualified immunity because no clearly established law as of March 2022 put him on notice that photographing a third party's body at a public scene violated a family member's own Fourteenth Amendment rights.
