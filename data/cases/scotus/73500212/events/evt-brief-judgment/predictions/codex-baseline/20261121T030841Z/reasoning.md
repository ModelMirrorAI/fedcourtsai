The forward snapshot of November 14 records a grant limited to Question 1, both merits briefs, and argument scheduled for January 11, 2027. This forecast uses the briefs. The August stay denial by Justice Kagan concerned whether district-court proceedings could continue pending review and carries little weight on her ultimate vote. The December 11 opinion in `decision-below.txt` matches the caption, appeal number 25-2935 and judgment in the petition appendix. The Ninth Circuit sustained the contempt findings, rejected the permanent ban on all commissions in its existing form, and remanded for changes to the remedy. The original California unfair-competition judgment and the petition’s second question about relief for nonparties fall outside the grant. [Petition, questions presented and App. 1a–49a](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-1311/409561/20260526163506450_2026-05-26%20Apple-Epic%20--%20Cert%20Petition%20and%20Appendix.pdf) · [Ninth Circuit opinion, pp. 18–21, 26–32, 39–42](https://www.courtlistener.com/opinion/10753351/)

The panel expressly allowed reliance on an injunction’s spirit even where conduct did not violate its strict letter. It used that reasoning for restrictions on link placement, warning screens and static URLs. That makes a correction more likely than the baseline suggests even if some of Apple’s conduct independently supports contempt. In its opening merits brief Apple identifies the notice problem precisely. The order tells Apple to let developers include links and buttons. It gives no price, formula or benchmark for a commission on a sale completed after the user follows a link. Rule 65(d)’s specificity requirement and *Taggart v. Lorenzen*, 587 U.S. 554, 560–65 (2019), give the Court a reason to insist that a defendant could ascertain the obligation before sanctions attached. [Apple merits brief, pp. 17–18, 31–39, 47–49](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-1311/424150/20260914154510009_2026-09-14%20No.%2025-1311%20Apple-Epic%20Merits%20Opening%20Brief.pdf)

Epic’s respondent brief explains why affirmance remains plausible even under *Taggart*. The district court found that Apple combined a 27% fee with outside payment costs exceeding the three-point gap from its 30% in-app commission. It also found that Apple deliberately made links hard to find and use. Together those conditions could amount to an actual prohibition on steering within the ordinary meaning of the order. *McComb v. Jacksonville Paper Co.*, 336 U.S. 187 (1949), permits enforcement against a new device for doing something already forbidden. Epic’s best textual example is the requirement that a button look like an ordinary link when the decree separately protects buttons. That gives the Court a reason to preserve possible contempt findings for particular practices. [Epic merits brief, pp. 15–17, 36–46](https://www.supremecourt.gov/DocketPDF/25/25-1311/432659/20261119164224177_25-1311%20CorrectedRespBrf.pdf)

The United States filed its merits amicus brief for neither party. It would reverse on the fee, affirm on the plain-button restriction and return the remaining issues. I follow its reading of the commission and its distinction between commissions and design rules. I favor a single vacatur because the limited grant concerns the governing standard, the findings address the combined effects of several practices, and a lower court can decide which findings and sanctions survive the correction. Epic itself requests remand if the Court changes the standard. *Taggart* used that disposition after correcting a contempt test. A formally mixed judgment along the government’s lines is my closest alternative. The 84% includes that path and outright reversal. The main chance of the judgment standing is a ruling that the lower courts already found a clear violation in practical effect.
