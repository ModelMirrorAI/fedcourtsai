## What happened

Apple and Epic Games have been fighting for years over Apple's App Store rules. A federal district court found that Apple's rules against "steering" users to outside payment options violated California's unfair competition law, and issued an injunction telling Apple to stop blocking developers from linking users to other ways to pay. Apple later adopted a new commission and design restrictions on such links, which Epic said still violated the injunction. The district court agreed, held Apple in civil contempt, and ordered a stronger remedy. The Ninth Circuit largely upheld that ruling, agreeing Apple's commission violated the injunction's own wording, while sending part of the remedy back for changes. Apple then asked the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court agreed to hear one part of Apple's request: whether a court may punish a company for contempt when its conduct fits only the general purpose, or "spirit," of a court order rather than something the order's actual wording clearly forbids. Apple argues this violates fair-notice principles and splits from rulings in other appeals courts; Epic responds that Apple's conduct actually broke the order's plain text, so the issue does not even arise here. The Court did not agree to review Apple's separate argument about whether the order improperly extended relief to developers beyond Epic.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to that one question in June 2026, and briefing is underway, with Apple's brief due in September and Epic's in November 2026. Separately, Apple asked for an emergency pause of related district court proceedings; a temporary administrative stay was granted for about a day, but Justice Kagan then denied Apple's fuller stay request.
