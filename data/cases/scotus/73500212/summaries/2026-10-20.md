## In brief

Apple is challenging, in a case about compliance with an antitrust injunction, whether courts may hold a party in contempt for violating an order's "spirit" rather than its explicit text.

## What happened

Epic sued Apple years ago over App Store rules, winning an injunction barring Apple from blocking developers' links to outside payment options. After Apple began charging a commission on those outside purchases, the district court held Apple in contempt, finding this violated the injunction's "spirit" even though its text never mentioned commissions. The Ninth Circuit affirmed the contempt finding but revised the remedy, sending the commission question back to the district court. Apple asked the Supreme Court to review both the contempt standard and a related question about the scope of court-ordered relief to non-parties.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a judge can punish someone for contempt of court based on what the judge believes an order was meant to accomplish, even when the order's actual words did not clearly forbid the conduct in question, or whether contempt requires that the order's text itself plainly covers what the person did.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to this contempt-standard question on June 30, 2026. The case has been fully briefed on the merits, with Apple's opening brief filed September 14, 2026, and multiple amicus briefs filed in September; it now awaits further briefing from Epic and, ultimately, argument and decision. Separately, an emergency request by Apple to pause related district court proceedings while the case is pending was denied by Justice Kagan on August 13, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Apple, which lost on this issue in the lower courts, is asking the Supreme Court to reverse the contempt finding against it. If the Court rules for Apple, the contempt finding tied to its commission on outside purchases would be overturned, which Apple argues would remove the basis for further district court proceedings setting its commission rate. If the Court rules for Epic, the Ninth Circuit's approach allowing contempt based on an order's underlying purpose would stand, and the lower court proceedings over Apple's commission would continue on that basis. The Court could also send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
