## In brief

PhRMA asks the Supreme Court to overturn a Ninth Circuit ruling that upheld Oregon's drug-pricing disclosure law against First Amendment and takings challenges.

## What happened

Oregon's HB 4005 requires drug manufacturers to report pricing information, including narrative explanations for price increases, to a state agency, which generally must post the information online. Trade secrets are normally shielded from posting, but Oregon can still disclose them if it decides that doing so serves the public interest. PhRMA, a pharmaceutical trade group, sued, arguing the reporting rule unconstitutionally compels speech and that the public-interest exception amounts to an uncompensated taking of trade secrets. A federal district court ruled for PhRMA on both claims. The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding the disclosures counted as commercial speech that survived First Amendment review, and that the takings claim failed because drug makers lack a reasonable expectation of secrecy in a heavily regulated industry.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law requiring companies to report product-specific pricing information to the government may be judged under a more lenient free-speech standard, and whether that standard was satisfied here based on the government's stated interest in reducing gaps in market information. It is also asked whether a business in a heavily regulated industry can be denied any claim that government-compelled disclosure of its trade secrets requires compensation, simply because it operates in that industry.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, and the Court has invited the Solicitor General to file a brief expressing the federal government's views on whether the case should be heard. No decision on whether to grant review has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

PhRMA is asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which went against PhRMA and in favor of Oregon's director of consumer and business services. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the free-speech and takings questions itself; a later decision could side with either party or send the case back without resolving it. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling upholding HB 4005 stays in place, and Oregon's reporting and disclosure requirements continue to apply to PhRMA's members.
