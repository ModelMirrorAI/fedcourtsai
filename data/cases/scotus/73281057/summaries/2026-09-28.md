## In brief

A pharmaceutical trade group asks the Court to strike down an Oregon drug-pricing transparency law, arguing it violates free speech and property rights.

## What happened

Oregon's HB 4005 requires drug manufacturers to file reports explaining price increases and lets the state publish those reports, including trade secrets, when officials decide disclosure serves the public interest. The Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) sued, claiming the reporting requirement is compelled speech barred by the First Amendment and that publishing trade secrets is an uncompensated taking of property under the Fifth Amendment. A federal district court agreed with PhRMA on both claims. A divided panel of the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, upholding the law under a lower level of free-speech scrutiny and rejecting the takings claim; one judge dissented on the speech issue. The full Ninth Circuit denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law requiring companies to report pricing information to the government can be judged under a relaxed free-speech standard, and whether that standard is satisfied merely because the disclosures aim to give buyers and sellers more equal information. It is also asked whether businesses in heavily regulated industries can be treated as automatically lacking any property-based objection when the government requires disclosure of their trade secrets.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, and after full briefing the Court asked the Solicitor General to submit a brief giving the federal government's views on whether the case should be heard. No decision on whether to grant or deny review has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

PhRMA, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the speech and property questions itself, without presuming either side correct. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling upholding the Oregon law stands, leaving the reporting and disclosure requirements in effect against PhRMA's members. A grant that leads to reversal would favor PhRMA; an affirmance would favor Oregon; a vacate would send the case back without resolving either claim.
