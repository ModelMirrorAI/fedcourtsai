## What happened

Oregon's Prescription Drug Price Transparency Act requires drug manufacturers to file reports explaining price increases, and lets a state agency publish those reports—including trade secrets—if it decides publication serves the public interest. The Pharmaceutical Research and Manufacturers of America (PhRMA) sued, arguing the reporting requirement forces unconstitutional speech and that publishing trade secrets takes property without compensation. A federal district court agreed with PhRMA on both claims. The Ninth Circuit reversed, over a partial dissent, holding the reporting requirement counts as commercial speech that survives a mid-level free-speech test, and that manufacturers in a heavily regulated industry cannot claim their trade secrets were unfairly taken.

## What the Court is being asked

PhRMA wants the Court to decide two things: first, whether courts should apply a relaxed level of free-speech review to government-mandated disclosures just because they concern a specific product, and whether Oregon's stated goal of leveling information between drug makers and buyers is enough to justify that mandate. Second, whether businesses that operate in a heavily regulated industry automatically give up any reasonable expectation that their trade secrets will stay confidential, such that they can never claim the government owes them compensation when it forces disclosure. Oregon's Director O'Day counters that the lower court simply applied existing free-speech and property tests to these particular facts, without creating any new rule or splitting from other courts.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, and the Court has invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views, with no further action yet recorded on the docket.
