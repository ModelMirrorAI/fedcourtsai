## Adjustments and limits

**Reasons to move above 5%.** First, the Court requested a response rather than letting the waiver stand. That is an affirmative attention signal, although it does not show four votes for review. Second, the petition frames an intelligible conflict about the level of generality at which a state interest is evaluated under Younger: the Third, Fifth, and Seventh Circuits versus the Fourth and Tenth. The strongest comparison is Harper v. Public Service Commission, 396 F.3d 348, 353–57 (4th Cir. 2005), which rejects an overgeneralized state-interest rationale in an interstate-commerce setting. Its opinion also cautions that a Commerce Clause label alone does not defeat abstention. Third, the requested rule could affect federal access for challenges to interstate enforcement beyond title lending. Finally, the petition itself identifies a companion petition, TitleMax of Virginia, No. 25-1359, and expressly requests a hold if the Court chooses that vehicle. That creates a derivative grant/GVR path for this particular docket even if it is not selected for argument. I use only the companion posture described in the provisioned petition, not independently retrieved subsequent developments.

**Reasons not to move higher.** This is an unpublished, unanimous Fifth Circuit disposition rather than a clean new categorical rule. App. 3a identifies the proceeding as quasi-criminal civil enforcement, placing it within the threshold category addressed by Sprint Communications, Inc. v. Jacobs, 571 U.S. 69, 78–82 (2013). App. 5a–7a gives a narrower account of TMX's arguments than the petition does: objections to personal jurisdiction, corporate separateness and liability; possible forfeiture of the Commerce Clause argument, which the panel leaves undecided; and the availability of eventual state-court constitutional review. Those features invite the view that the case concerns application of abstention principles to a particular enforcement record, not an unavoidable circuit conflict. Possible forfeiture is a vehicle risk, not an established independent holding.

The alleged conflict is also imperfect. Harper involved a different regulatory setting; the Tenth Circuit example involves tribal activities and strong federal primacy. They support tension but do not establish opposite outcomes on indistinguishable facts. Sprint's narrow categories help the petitioner in principle, but this panel expressly addressed that threshold and found a qualifying enforcement action. The petition's own account at pp. 11–12 describes a favorable hearing-examiner recommendation followed by an agency appeal, creating some possibility that the administrative proceeding changes the practical need for immediate Supreme Court intervention. I do not label the case moot. Finally, I cannot verify the asserted absence of vehicle problems against an unfiled BIO. I do not assume either that opposition will concede the split or that the underlying constitutional claims are correct.

Balancing these factors, **16%** represents a meaningful but limited upward adjustment from the matching 5.016% prior. It is a judgmental forecast, not an estimated call-for-response multiplier. Denial remains distinctly more likely than any grant. Broad political examples in the petition affect potential significance, not an assumption that the Court will reach those examples or adjudicate the Commerce Clause merits here.
