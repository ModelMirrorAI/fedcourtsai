## In brief

Alaska asks the Court to uphold its forfeiture of a bush pilot's airplane over a passenger's six-pack of beer, after state courts split on how to weigh his conduct.

## What happened

Kenneth Jouppi, an air-taxi pilot, was convicted of a misdemeanor for knowingly flying alcohol into Beaver, a village that bans alcohol. Alaska law requires forfeiture of any aircraft used for such importation. A trial court found forfeiting Jouppi's $95,000 plane unconstitutionally excessive, comparing his conduct to the legislature's worst-case targets. Alaska's intermediate appellate court sent the case back for more fact-finding. The Alaska Supreme Court reversed, holding, without further fact-finding, that forfeiting the plane did not violate the Constitution's ban on excessive fines, even assuming Jouppi knew of only one six-pack and had no other related misconduct.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court must decide whether, in judging if a fine or forfeiture is unconstitutionally harsh, a court should weigh how serious the underlying type of offense is in general, or instead focus on the specific defendant's own actions and culpability. Jouppi argues the law requires courts to look at what the particular offender actually did; Alaska and the federal government argue courts may instead defer heavily to the legislature's judgment about the offense category, including for property used to carry out the crime.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; it now awaits a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Jouppi, the plane's owner, asks the Court to overturn the ruling that upheld the forfeiture. If the Court agrees with Jouppi, it would rule that courts must judge excessiveness by examining the defendant's specific conduct, likely sending the case back for the forfeiture to be reassessed under that standard. If the Court instead sides with Alaska, the ruling allowing the plane's forfeiture would stand, and Jouppi would lose the aircraft. The Court could also rule more narrowly on how much deference legislatures receive without fully resolving the case either way.
