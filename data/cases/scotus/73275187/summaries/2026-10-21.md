## In brief

Jouppi challenged the forfeiture of his airplane, seized after his conviction for flying alcohol into a dry Alaska village, as an unconstitutionally excessive fine.

## What happened

Kenneth Jouppi, a bush pilot, was convicted of knowingly transporting alcohol into Beaver, a village that bans alcohol. Alaska law requires forfeiture of any aircraft used to import alcohol into such a village. Jouppi argued forfeiting his $95,000 airplane over a passenger's beer would be an unconstitutionally excessive fine. Alaska's courts went back and forth: a trial court found the forfeiture excessive, an intermediate appeals court sent the case back for more fact-finding, and the Alaska Supreme Court ultimately held the forfeiture was not excessive, reasoning that even a small amount of alcohol justified it given the harms of alcohol abuse in rural Alaska.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in deciding if a fine or forfeiture is unconstitutionally excessive, courts should judge the seriousness of the offense only in a general, categorical way, or should instead weigh the specific individual's own conduct and culpability.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; it was set for argument in December 2026 and now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Jouppi, who lost in the Alaska Supreme Court, is asking the Court to reverse that ruling. If the Court affirms, the forfeiture of his airplane stands, and Alaska's approach of evaluating an offense's gravity broadly rather than focusing on the individual defendant's conduct would remain intact. If the Court reverses, it would reject that broader approach and send the case back, likely requiring Alaska's courts to reconsider the forfeiture using a standard focused on Jouppi's specific wrongdoing. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Alaska Supreme Court's decision and return the case without resolving whether the forfeiture was excessive.
