## In brief

Kenneth Jouppi asks the Court whether judges must weigh a defendant's actual conduct, not just the offense in the abstract, in excessive-fines cases.

## What happened

Alaska convicted Kenneth Jouppi, an air-taxi pilot, of knowingly flying beer into a village that bans alcohol imports. Under state law, conviction required forfeiting the airplane used in the offense. Jouppi argued forfeiting his $95,000 plane over a six-pack of beer was an unconstitutionally "excessive fine" under the Eighth Amendment. A trial court agreed, but Alaska's intermediate appeals court sent the case back for more fact-finding, and the Alaska Supreme Court ultimately held the forfeiture was constitutional as a matter of law, reasoning that even a small amount of smuggled alcohol justified the penalty given the law's purpose.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in deciding if a fine or forfeiture is excessive, courts may judge the seriousness of the offense only in a general, abstract way—asking what harm this kind of crime typically causes—or whether they must instead focus on how serious this particular defendant's own conduct actually was.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; it was argued on December 1, 2026, and now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Jouppi, who lost in the Alaska Supreme Court, asked the Court to take up the case. If the Court rules for Jouppi, it would hold that courts must assess the gravity of an offense by looking at the specific defendant's own wrongdoing, and the case would likely return to Alaska's courts to reconsider his forfeiture under that standard. If the Court rules for Alaska, it would uphold the more general, abstract approach the Alaska Supreme Court used, leaving the forfeiture of Jouppi's airplane in place. A decision to vacate and send the case back would leave the excessiveness question unresolved for further proceedings without deciding it for either side.
