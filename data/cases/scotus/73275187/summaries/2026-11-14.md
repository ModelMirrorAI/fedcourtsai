## In brief

Ken Jouppi asks the Supreme Court to rule that Alaska excessively punished him by forfeiting his airplane over a passenger's six-pack of beer.

## What happened

Kenneth Jouppi, an Alaska bush pilot, was convicted of a misdemeanor for knowingly flying alcohol into Beaver, a village that bans alcohol. Troopers found beer, some visible, loaded on his plane. Alaska law requires forfeiture of any aircraft used to import alcohol into such a village. Jouppi argued forfeiting his $95,000 plane was an unconstitutionally "excessive fine." A trial court agreed, Alaska's intermediate appeals court sent the case back for more fact-finding, and the Alaska Supreme Court reversed, upholding the forfeiture as not excessive given Jouppi's conduct and the harms of alcohol abuse in rural Alaska.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked how to judge whether a forfeiture punishment fits the crime: should courts weigh how serious the offense was only in a general sense, looking at the statute and the harms the lawmakers were addressing, or should they instead focus on what this particular person actually did and how culpable they were. Separately, the federal government argues that forfeiting property actually used to commit a crime should not require this kind of proportionality comparison at all, given a long history of seizing instruments of crimes like smuggling vessels.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari and the case has been fully briefed, including by the United States as amicus supporting Alaska, and argument was held. The case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Jouppi, who is challenging the ruling below, is asking the Court to reverse the judgment in Alaska's favor. If the Court sides with Jouppi, it would hold that courts must weigh the specific offender's conduct and culpability, not just the offense in the abstract, and send the case back for the forfeiture to be reconsidered under that standard. If the Court sides with Alaska, it would leave the forfeiture of Jouppi's airplane in place, affirming that the punishment did not violate the Constitution's ban on excessive fines.
