## In brief

Kenneth Jouppi asks the Court whether judging an excessive-fine claim requires weighing a defendant's specific conduct rather than the offense's seriousness in the abstract.

## What happened

Jouppi, a bush pilot, was convicted of a misdemeanor for knowingly transporting alcohol into a "dry" Alaska village; the beer belonged to a passenger, and Jouppi says he knew only of one visible six-pack. Alaska law required forfeiture of the plane he used. Alaska courts went back and forth: a trial court found forfeiting the $95,000 plane unconstitutionally excessive, the Alaska Court of Appeals sent the case back for more fact-finding, and the Alaska Supreme Court then held, as a matter of law, that the forfeiture was constitutional, concluding it was not grossly disproportionate to Jouppi's offense.

## What the Court is being asked

The case turns on how courts should measure whether a forfeiture or fine is "excessive" under the Constitution's ban on excessive fines. Jouppi argues that courts must look at the gravity of what the particular defendant actually did, not just classify his conduct under a broadly worded law and ask whether that category of offense, viewed generally, is serious enough to justify the penalty. Alaska responds that its courts did consider Jouppi's specific circumstances and that substantial deference is owed to the legislature's judgment about how serious this kind of offense is and what punishment fits it.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; merits briefs from both sides and numerous outside groups have been filed, and the case was argued on December 1, 2026. The case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Jouppi, the pilot whose airplane was ordered forfeited, is asking the Court to reverse the Alaska Supreme Court's decision. If the Court affirms, Alaska's approach stands and Jouppi's airplane remains subject to forfeiture under the state court's reasoning. If the Court reverses, it would hold that courts must evaluate a defendant's own specific conduct rather than the offense category in the abstract, likely sending the case back for the forfeiture to be reassessed under that standard. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Alaska Supreme Court's judgment without deciding the excessiveness question itself, returning the case for further proceedings.
