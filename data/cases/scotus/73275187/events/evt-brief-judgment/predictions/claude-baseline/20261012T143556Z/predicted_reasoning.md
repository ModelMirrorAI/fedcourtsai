# Forecast — Jouppi v. Alaska (No. 25-246), judgment after merits briefing

**Judgment class: vacated (and remanded).** The Court disturbs the Alaska
Supreme Court's judgment. The most likely form is "vacated and remanded" for
the state courts to apply the correct gravity standard; "reversed and remanded"
is the close second form of the same result. An affirmance is the main
alternative (about one in five). A DIG or an equally divided Court is not live:
the case was granted after a long hold and a supplemental brief, the question
is a clean methodological one, and nothing on the docket suggests a recusal.

**Vote lineup: 8–1, with Justice Alito the most likely dissenter.** Timbs v.
Indiana was unanimous and the Excessive Fines Clause has drawn support across
the Court's usual divides (Gorsuch and Thomas from original meaning; Sotomayor,
Kagan, Jackson from the liberty side). The amicus field is cross-ideological —
Cato, Pacific Legal Foundation, the U.S. Chamber, NACDL, MacArthur Justice
Center, a Republican governor — with no federal amicus supporting Alaska on the
docket at the cutoff. A second dissent (Kavanaugh or Roberts, on deference to
legislative judgments about harm) is the most likely way this becomes 7–2.
Thomas or Gorsuch may write separately on the Clause's original meaning or on
the in rem/in personam forfeiture history while joining the judgment.

**Which question the Court reaches.** It answers the question presented as the
petitioner frames it: gravity is assessed by reference to the defendant's own
offense and culpability, not the offense category in the abstract. It does not
take up the respondent's reformulated question (whether this forfeiture is
excessive), and leaves that application for remand.

**Ground.** Bajakajian's "gross disproportionality to the gravity of a
defendant's offense" is read as asking about *this* defendant: what he did, the
harm he actually caused, any related misconduct. The Alaska Supreme Court went
wrong by measuring harm through legislative history and statewide alcohol harms,
and by treating his falling within the statute's class as settling the matter.
The Court acknowledges that legislative judgments deserve substantial deference,
but holds that such deference cannot replace the defendant-specific inquiry. It
rejects Alaska's position that a mandatory instrumentality forfeiture following
a criminal conviction is effectively exempt.

**Breadth.** The ruling is narrow: a methodological correction and a remand. The
Court does not hold that forfeiting this airplane is excessive, does not lay out
a full multi-factor test, and does not resolve the historical-instrumentality
question beyond rejecting it as a categorical safe harbor. Alaska's argument
about the incomplete record (Jouppi objected to evidence of related misconduct)
goes to the remand, not to whether the judgment is disturbed.

**Dissent's likely theme (if any):** the court below did consider Jouppi's
actual conduct, the legislature's harm judgment deserves deference, and
historical vessel forfeiture shows that forfeiting an instrumentality is not an
excessive fine.
