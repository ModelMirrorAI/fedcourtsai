# Forecast: Jouppi v. Alaska (No. 25-246) — the merits judgment

**Judgment class: `vacated`.** The Court will vacate the Alaska Supreme
Court's judgment and remand. It will hold that the Excessive Fines Clause's
gross-disproportionality inquiry requires courts to evaluate the gravity of
the offense by reference to the specific defendant's own wrongdoing — his
actual conduct, culpability, harm, and sentence — not the offense in the
abstract or the legislature's generalized harm judgment. That is the
petition's own requested relief ("the Alaska Supreme Court's starting-gate
misconception corrected, and the case remanded for further proceedings"),
and it is the disposition that follows from correcting a legal standard
applied "as a matter of law" below: the Court will not itself decide whether
forfeiting the airplane is excessive, because the state courts never applied
the correct standard to the record. An outright `reversed` — holding the
forfeiture unconstitutional without remand — is the main alternative
(perhaps a one-in-five chance given the extreme facts), but the Alaska
courts' unresolved factual disputes (how much beer Jouppi's culpability
covers) make a remand the cleaner and likelier route.

**Vote lineup: unanimous or near-unanimous to disturb, 9-0.** Timbs v.
Indiana — the Court's last Excessive Fines Clause case, argued by the same
litigation ecosystem, with Tyson Timbs himself appearing here as an amicus —
was 9-0. Bajakajian's defendant-specific methodology was written by Justice
Thomas; Justices Gorsuch and Thomas have repeatedly flagged exorbitant
fines-and-forfeiture practice (Toth dissent from denial, Culley concurrence,
Leonard statement), and Justice Sotomayor's Culley dissent (joined by Kagan
and Jackson) shows the other wing's alignment against revenue-driven
forfeiture. The State's abstract standard has no natural champion on the
current Court. I expect the largest defection risk to be a concurrence in
the judgment quibbling with how much of Bajakajian's multi-factor apparatus
to endorse, not a dissent from the judgment. Authorship is unscored; my
guess is Gorsuch, with Thomas and Sotomayor the next most likely.

**Which question the Court decides.** QP as written — the level-of-generality
question only. The Court will not take up the concurring Alaska judge's
theory that in personam forfeitures escape the Clause entirely (foreclosed by
Bajakajian and not pressed by the State), and will not resolve whether this
forfeiture is in fact excessive.

**Ground and breadth.** The majority will ground the holding in Bajakajian's
own application: that decision measured Bajakajian's "culpability relative to
other potential violators," his actual harm, and his actual sentence, and
faulted reliance on theoretical statutory maxima and legislative-history
harms. The rule will be categorical — defendant-specific gravity review in
every excessive-fines case, civil or criminal, fine or forfeiture — but the
Court will decline to canonize any particular multi-factor test, leaving the
circuits' frameworks intact so long as they operate at the right level of
generality.

**Procedural exits.** A DIG is unlikely (~3%): the State's vehicle
objections (fact disputes, criminal-not-civil posture) were fully aired in
the BIO and the Court granted anyway after calling for a response and
holding the petition for months. No recusal is apparent, so an equally
divided affirmance is a remote possibility only via unexpected vacancy.

**Timing** (unscored, stated for discipline): argument December 2026 or
January 2027; decision by late June 2027. A lopsided judgment in a
low-complexity case could come as early as spring 2027.
