## What moves the forecast

**The strongest route to reversal is present injury, not an exemption from Article III.** Petitioners argue both that they are the laws' objects and that the changes already interfere with parenting and participation in their children's care. Their opening brief describes Parents 2A and 2B's child, family conflict, and altered communication, and argues that the Ninth Circuit wrongly reduced all those allegations to speculation about a future runaway episode. Sources: petitioners' merits brief, pp. 15–20, 21–34, 35–49; petition appendix, pp. 14a–25a. These concrete family allegations give the Court a narrower route than recognizing injury whenever anyone dislikes a law's purpose.

**The United States supports disturbing the judgment, but requests vacatur.** Its principal position is that a present reduction in parental authority over access to care is itself an injury, independently of whether the child eventually uses the shelter/referral system. That is a more focused route than petitioners' broad suggestion that object status dispenses with the ordinary inquiry. I follow its identification of a potentially present injury, but discount its categorical claim that every objecting parent can sue immediately whenever that authority changes. Sources: United States merits amicus, pp. 12–26 and conclusion, p. 31. Its requested vacatur is one reason I retain a substantial 0.24 probability on that label instead of equating a petitioner-side win with reversal.

**A close prior decision provides a bridge, not a resolved answer.** In *Mirabelli v. Bonta*, 607 U.S. 492, 496–498 (2026), the Court found parents very likely to have standing to contest school exclusion policies. I verified the March 2 opinion independently through CourtListener and the Court's PDF; both parties also address it. That interlocutory ruling makes it easier for the six Justices on its prevailing side to recognize a present parental-participation injury here. But its school setting and demonstrated interference are important differences. It does not establish that every parent may challenge every child-related policy. Barrett's separate writing also makes her a plausible participant in a careful, rather than categorical, opinion. Sources: that prior decision, pp. 5–7 and Barrett concurrence; respondents' merits brief, pp. 27–28; petitioners' merits brief, pp. 22–23. This prior, not the bare fact of certiorari, is the principal reason for a petitioner-side lineup.

## Why the number is not higher

Respondents present serious legal and vehicle objections. The challenged provisions concern shelter reporting, agency notification and reconciliation efforts, and referrals; they do not themselves authorize surgery or hormone treatment without parental consent. Respondents say the Department must still make a good-faith effort to contact parents, and report that no shelter had invoked the new pregnancy/gender-care reporting route in more than three years. I treat the nonuse figure as respondents' representation, not an independently audited fact or a substitute for the complaint's temporal standing inquiry. Sources: respondents' merits brief, pp. 6–14. I therefore do not premise this prediction on a proved program of secret medical treatment.

The alternative account is that parental communication changes are voluntary responses to a speculative chain of independent choices. *Clapper v. Amnesty International USA*, 568 U.S. 398, 416 (2013), rejects manufacturing standing through precautions against insufficiently imminent harm; I verified that rule through CourtListener. Respondents' application of it here is substantial, especially because notification and access to outpatient counseling are not identical to a current exclusion from a school decision. Sources: respondents' merits brief, pp. 30–48; petition appendix, pp. 14a–25a. An affirmance could attract Roberts or Barrett without requiring either Justice to retreat from parental-rights precedents.

The mootness and preservation objections further limit confidence. Respondents contend that, after abandonment of other claims and children aging out, only Parents 2A and 2B retain a viable individual dispute; those parents did not assert the free-exercise claim. They also contend that the leading new theories were not presented below and that invalidating these laws would not eliminate the remaining family's existing conflict. Petitioners dispute preservation and point to a still-minor child; their proposed future damages amendment does not establish that a live damages claim is already before the Court. Sources: respondents' merits brief, pp. 20–24, 47–49; petitioners' merits brief, pp. 18–19 and n.13. These problems support both the 0.19 affirmance and 0.08 DIG allocations. They also make a ruling on substantive constitutional validity especially unlikely.

Balancing these considerations leaves me only modestly above the 70.36% historical disturbed rate, at 73%. The majority-ground forecast is that concrete present impairment suffices on these pleaded facts; the separate breadth forecast is that the Court does not endorse the government's all-objecting-parents rule. Either proposition can fail independently.
