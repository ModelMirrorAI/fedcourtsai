## In brief

Parents and organizations challenging Washington laws on runaway transgender youth ask the Supreme Court to decide whether they have standing to sue over the laws' effects on their parenting.

## What happened

Washington changed its laws in 2023 so that when a runaway youth seeking "gender-affirming" care arrives at a licensed shelter, the shelter notifies the state's child welfare department instead of the parents; the department must then try to notify parents, offer family reunification services, and offer referrals for behavioral health services. Parents and two organizations sued, arguing the laws violate their constitutional parenting rights, even though none alleged a child had actually run away to a shelter and received such care. The district court dismissed the suit for lack of standing (the legal requirement that a plaintiff show real injury), and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, finding the parents' claimed injuries either self-inflicted or too speculative.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether parents may sue over laws that change how the state handles their runaway children's access to certain health services, even though the parents cannot show their own children have been affected yet, or must instead first show a child was imminently likely to run away and actually obtain such services.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition and has received full briefing, including a brief from the United States supporting the parents. The case was argued and is awaiting the Court's decision.

## What each outcome would mean

International Partners for Ethical Care and the parent petitioners, who lost in the Ninth Circuit, are asking the Court to reverse that ruling. If the Court affirms, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, meaning these parents' lawsuit stays dismissed for lack of standing, though the case could return to lower courts if claims are preserved. If the Court reverses, the parents would be found to have standing, and their case would proceed in the lower courts to address the merits of their constitutional claims against Washington officials. If the Court vacates, the Ninth Circuit's decision would be set aside and the case sent back for further proceedings without a final ruling on standing.
