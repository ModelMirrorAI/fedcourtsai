## In brief

Parents and advocacy groups ask the Supreme Court whether they can sue over Washington laws on runaway transgender youth, after courts found their claimed harms too speculative.

## What happened

Parents and two organizations sued Washington state officials, arguing that 2023 laws governing how shelters and the state's child welfare agency handle runaway youth seeking "gender-affirming" or reproductive health care violate their constitutional parenting, speech, and religious rights. The laws change who notifies parents when a sheltered runaway youth is seeking such care, direct the state to offer voluntary family reconciliation and behavioral health referrals, and allow youth to stay in shelters longer under some circumstances. None of the petitioners' own children were alleged to have actually gone through this process. The district court dismissed the suit for lack of standing, finding the parents' claimed injuries speculative and self-imposed. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and rehearing was denied over dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether these parents have legal standing to sue over laws that do not directly regulate them, where the parents say the laws discourage their involvement in decisions about their children's care and create incentives that could affect their families in the future, even though none of their children have yet gone through the specific chain of events the laws address.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari on June 29, 2026. The case has since been briefed and argued; it was set for argument on December 7, 2026, and the docket shows briefing completed and the appellate record received, with the case now awaiting decision.

## What each outcome would mean

International Partners for Ethical Care and the other petitioners, who lost in the Ninth Circuit, asked the Court to take up the case. If the Court rules for petitioners, it would hold they have standing and send the case back to the lower courts to consider the merits of their constitutional claims for the first time. If the Court rules for Washington officials, the Ninth Circuit's dismissal for lack of standing would stand, ending this suit without any ruling on whether the laws are constitutional. The Court could also vacate and remand without deciding the standing question itself, leaving that issue for further proceedings below.
