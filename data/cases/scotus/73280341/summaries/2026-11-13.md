## In brief

The Supreme Court reviewed whether parents challenging Washington laws on runaway youth seeking gender-related care have standing to sue, after the Ninth Circuit found they did not.

## What happened

Washington amended its laws so that when a runaway minor seeks certain health services at a licensed shelter, the shelter notifies the state child welfare agency instead of the parents directly; the agency must then try to notify parents, offer family reconciliation services, and offer referrals for behavioral health services. A group of parents and two organizations sued, arguing the laws unconstitutionally interfered with their parental rights, free speech, and religious exercise. They did not allege that any of their children had actually used these provisions, instead describing fears that this might someday happen. The district court dismissed the case for lack of standing, finding the harms too speculative, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether parents can sue over a law that changes who is notified and how a state handles runaway children seeking certain services, based on their worry that the law might someday affect their own family, or whether they must instead show a more concrete and immediate injury before they can bring such a challenge.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, received full briefing including from the federal government as amicus supporting the parents, and heard oral argument on December 7, 2026. The case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

The parents and organizations who petitioned are asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling that they lacked standing. If the Court affirms, the lower court's dismissal stands, and these parents' lawsuit ends without any ruling on the underlying constitutional claims. If the Court reverses, the case would return to the lower courts, where the parents could continue pursuing their claims that the laws violate their constitutional rights. A ruling vacating the decision would send the case back without resolving the standing question in either side's favor.
