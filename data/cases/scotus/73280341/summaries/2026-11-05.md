## In brief

Parents and advocacy groups challenged Washington laws on notifying parents about runaway transgender youth; the Court is deciding whether they have standing to sue.

## What happened

In 2023 Washington amended laws governing licensed youth shelters and its child-welfare agency. When a runaway minor seeking "gender-affirming" or reproductive health care arrives at a shelter, the shelter now notifies the state agency rather than parents directly; the agency must then attempt to notify parents, offer family-reconciliation services, and offer referrals for behavioral health services. Parents and two organizations sued, arguing the scheme violates their parental, speech, and religious-exercise rights, based on fears about what might happen if their children ran away. The district court dismissed the suit for lack of standing, finding the alleged harms speculative, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, with dissents from several judges.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether parents who object to this notification and referral scheme have suffered the kind of concrete, actual or likely injury that lets them sue in federal court, even though none of their children has yet run away to a shelter or received the services at issue. The dispute centers on whether being affected indirectly by a law aimed at someone else, or fearing a chain of future events, is enough to get into court, as opposed to requiring proof that the harm has already happened or is about to.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on December 7, 2026; merits briefing, including an amicus brief from the United States supporting the parents, is complete, and the case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

International Partners for Ethical Care and the other parents, who lost in the lower courts, are asking the Court to reverse and hold they have standing. If the Court affirms, the Ninth Circuit's dismissal stands, and these parents' suit ends without any court ruling on whether the Washington laws are constitutional. If the Court reverses, the case would return to the lower courts to decide the merits of the parents' constitutional claims. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Ninth Circuit's decision and send the case back without deciding the standing question for either side.
