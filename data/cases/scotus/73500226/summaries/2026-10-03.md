## In brief

Oklahoma and other states ask the Court to decide whether a federal law letting a private horseracing authority write and enforce rules is unconstitutional.

## What happened

Oklahoma, West Virginia, Louisiana, and several horseracing organizations sued the federal government, the Federal Trade Commission, and the Horseracing Integrity and Safety Authority, a private nonprofit that Congress empowered to regulate horseracing nationwide. They argued the arrangement unlawfully hands government power to a private entity. A federal district court rejected the claim, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed. After the Supreme Court decided a related case on private regulatory power, it sent this case back for another look, but the Sixth Circuit again upheld the law, while a different appeals court, the Fifth Circuit, had ruled the opposite way in a separate case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law can give a private organization the power to enforce federal rules in court, investigate and search regulated businesses, issue subpoenas, and impose penalties, and whether that same organization can effectively write binding federal regulations that a government agency must approve even if the agency disagrees with the policy choices behind them.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including responses from the federal government and the horseracing authority and a reply from Oklahoma, along with amicus briefs supporting the petitioners. It was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, then rescheduled; the docket does not yet show an outcome.

## What each outcome would mean

Oklahoma and the other states and horseracing groups are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the law's delegation of enforcement and rulemaking power to the private authority is constitutional, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's ruling upholding the law stands, leaving the private authority's regulatory powers in place, at least within that circuit's jurisdiction, while a conflicting ruling from the Fifth Circuit remains unresolved nationally.
