## Legal context and assessment

I retrieved and read Brown v. Gilmore, 533 U.S. 1301, 1303 (2001) (Rehnquist, C.J., in chambers), through CourtListener opinion 9195259. It distinguishes an affirmative injunction under the All Writs Act from a stay and explains the exceptional exigency and clarity of entitlement required for such an injunction. I use that procedural principle only, not Brown's unrelated substantive constitutional analysis. The docket calls this an injunction application, but without its contents I cannot establish that the identical All Writs posture applies. Accordingly this is conditional legal context, not a finding that Akerman has failed any particular element.

A high threshold for affirmative emergency relief, a single-applicant posture, and no visible affirmative sign of broader institutional attention support a strong denial forecast. No readable material supplies a reason to predict an unusually clear entitlement or urgent injury that would justify moving toward a grant. That is an evidentiary limitation, not proof that the applicant failed to allege either. I retain a nonzero 3% chance for an unexpectedly compelling application or narrow partial relief hidden by the extraction gap. The missing application makes finer discrimination difficult; reasonable record-limited estimates could differ materially. It also prevents a responsible stakes score, so `big_case_score` is null with a stated rationale.

The inconsistent submission/docketing dates make an exact elapsed-time forecast unreliable. No doctrinal holding or merits vote is predicted from a likely unexplained denial.
