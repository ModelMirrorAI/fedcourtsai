## In brief

Several New York City residents ask the Court to revive their challenge to city and state bans on stun guns and tasers.

## What happened

Nunzio Calce and other individuals, along with two gun-rights groups, sued New York City and its police commissioner, arguing that laws banning possession of stun guns and tasers violate the right to keep and bear arms. One plaintiff, Amanda Kennedy, was herself charged after using a stun gun to fend off an attacker. A federal district court ruled for the city, holding that the plaintiffs had not shown stun guns and tasers are "in common use," a showing it said was required before the challenged bans could even be considered a restriction on constitutionally protected conduct. The Second Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether people challenging a ban on a category of weapon must first prove that the weapon is commonly owned before courts will treat the ban as touching on the right to keep and bear arms at all, or whether that common-use question instead belongs to a later part of the analysis, where the government bears the burden. The petition also asks the Court to decide whether stun guns and tasers are covered at all, and whether the city and state bans on them are unconstitutional.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for a conference, after which the Court asked New York City to respond, a request not yet shown as satisfied since the city sought and received an extension of time to respond. No conference has yet considered the case with the city's response in hand.

## What each outcome would mean

Calce and the other individuals and organizations are asking the Court to take up their case and reverse the lower court's ruling. If the Court grants review and ultimately rules for Calce, the bans could be struck down or sent back for reconsideration under a more favorable legal standard. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's decision upholding the bans stands, and the city may continue enforcing them. A grant would not itself decide who is right; it would only mean the Court will hear full arguments on the question.
