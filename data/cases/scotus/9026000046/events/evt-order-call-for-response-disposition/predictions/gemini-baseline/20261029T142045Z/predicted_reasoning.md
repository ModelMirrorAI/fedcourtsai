# Forecast: *Nunzio Calce, et al. v. City of New York, New York, et al.* (No. 26-46)

## Procedural Trajectory: Distributions, Hold Posture, and CVSG

The docket currently shows **one distribution** (distributed on August 19, 2026, for the September 28, 2026 Long Conference) and no CVSG, followed by the Court's September 8, 2026 call for a response (due October 8, 2026) after Respondent City of New York had waived its right to respond.

- **Further Distributions (`relist-increment`):** Because the Court called for a response prior to the September 28 conference, the petition will be distributed at least once more after the City of New York files its brief in opposition and Petitioners file their reply. Beyond that initial post-response distribution, there is a substantial likelihood that the Court will **hold** the petition pending its decision in *Viramontes v. Cook County*, No. 25-238 (cert. granted June 30, 2026), which presents the closely related question of how *Heller* and *Bruen*'s "in common use" standard operates at the Second Amendment's threshold textual step. If held for *Viramontes*, the petition will be distributed again after *Viramontes* is decided before final disposition; if not held, it is likely to be resolved after one or two post-response distributions.
- **No CVSG (`cvsg-increment`):** The Court will not call for the views of the Solicitor General. The petition challenges state and municipal prohibitions (N.Y. Penal Law § 265.01(1) and N.Y.C. Admin. Code § 10-135) on possessing electronic arms (tasers and stun guns) under the Second and Fourteenth Amendments. No federal statute, agency action, or federal enforcement interest is implicated.

## Disposition and Route (`disposition` and `summary-disposition-route`)

The most likely disposition is **denial of certiorari**, though there is a substantial (~28%) probability of a grant-family disposition—predominantly a **grant, vacate, and remand (GVR)** after *Viramontes v. Cook County* is decided.

- **Why Summary Disposition (GVR) Dominates Plenary Review Conditional on a Grant:** If the Court grants the petition, it is overwhelmingly likely (85% conditional probability) to dispose of the case in the cert order itself via a **GVR in light of *Viramontes v. Cook County*** (or, less likely, a summary per curiam vacatur akin to *Caetano v. Massachusetts*, 577 U.S. 411 (2016)), rather than setting *Calce* for plenary briefing and oral argument. Both the District Court and the Second Circuit resolved this case exclusively at *Bruen*'s threshold plain-text step, holding under *United States v. Gomez*, 159 F.4th 172 (2d Cir. 2025), that a challenger bears the step-one evidentiary burden to prove that a banned class of arms is currently "in common use" for self-defense, and never reached *Bruen*'s historical-tradition step in the alternative. If *Viramontes* holds that bearable arms presumptively satisfy the Second Amendment's plain text at step one and that the "in common use" / "dangerous and unusual" inquiry belongs to the government's burden at step two, a GVR of the Second Circuit's judgment will follow unless the Court concludes that the record deficiencies below independently foreclose relief.
- **Why Denial Remains the Modal Outcome:** Plenary review in *Calce* is unlikely because the Second Circuit decided the appeal by a non-precedential summary order that emphasized case-specific gaps in Petitioners' summary-judgment record (which consisted of four items of evidence—prior testimony from another case, a declaration on Axon Taser sales, an Axon SEC Form 10-K, and judicial notice of two judicial opinions—and included virtually no distinct evidence regarding non-Taser stun guns). Once the City of New York files its brief in opposition highlighting those record-specific evidentiary shortcomings, the Court is more likely than not to deny review rather than grant plenary review or remand a thin summary-judgment record.

## Scope of Review and Conditional Merits Ground

- **Questions Presented:** If the Court were to grant plenary review (rather than GVR), it would take **Question Presented 1** (whether a challenger to a ban on a type of arm must demonstrate that the arm is "in common use" to establish that the ban implicates the Second Amendment's plain text) and **Question Presented 2** (whether the New York State and New York City bans on tasers and stun guns implicate the Second Amendment's plain text), while leaving **Question Presented 3** (the ultimate step-two historical constitutionality of the bans) to be addressed by the lower courts on remand in the first instance, since neither court below reached *Bruen* step two.
- **Conditional Merits Ground:** If granted on the merits or vacated via GVR, the Court would hold that electronic arms (tasers and stun guns) are bearable "Arms" within the plain text of the Second Amendment under *Heller*, *Caetano*, and *Bruen*, and that lower courts may not dismiss a challenge to a categorical ban on bearable arms at the threshold step based on a plaintiff's failure to produce granular nationwide civilian-versus-law-enforcement market-share statistics.

## Separate Writings on Denial (`dissent-from-denial`)

If the Court denies certiorari, most likely no Justice will write separately, particularly if the denial follows a decision in *Viramontes* or rests on the thin summary-judgment record created in the district court. However, there is a meaningful (~12%) conditional probability of a **dissent from or statement respecting the denial of certiorari**, most likely authored by **Justice Thomas** or **Justice Alito** (who authored the concurrence in *Caetano v. Massachusetts*, 577 U.S. at 412–22, joined by Justice Thomas, specifically criticizing categorical bans on stun guns and tasers, including New York's), potentially joined by **Justice Gorsuch**.
