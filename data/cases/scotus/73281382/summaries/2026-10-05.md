## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear Quashaun Reel's challenge to a drug search that followed police knocking at his home during a "knock-and-talk" investigation.

## What happened

Police in High Point, North Carolina, visited Reel's home after receiving tips about drug activity, intending to conduct what officers call a "knock-and-talk." An officer followed a visitor to the front door, smelled marijuana when Reel opened it, and after Reel shut the door, officers forced their way in and found drugs. Reel's motion to suppress the evidence was denied. The North Carolina Court of Appeals affirmed, over a dissent, and the North Carolina Supreme Court affirmed without an opinion.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether officers conduct a Fourth Amendment search when they enter the area immediately around a home to carry out a knock-and-talk investigation with the purpose of gathering evidence against the homeowner, rather than simply to speak with them. Reel's petition says lower courts disagree on how much an officer's purpose matters to this analysis; North Carolina's brief in opposition says there is no such disagreement and that Reel did not raise this purpose argument in the lower courts.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after the state waived and then was asked to respond, and after full briefing including amicus briefs. The Supreme Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Reel, the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case and overturn the North Carolina courts' rulings. Because the Court denied review, the North Carolina Supreme Court's decision stands, leaving in place the denial of Reel's motion to suppress and his resulting conviction. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower courts' reasoning; it simply leaves that outcome undisturbed without deciding the constitutional question Reel raised.
