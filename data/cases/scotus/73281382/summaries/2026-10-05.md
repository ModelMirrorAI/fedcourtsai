## In brief

Quashaun Reel asked the Supreme Court to review whether police searched his home's curtilage during a knock-and-talk visit; the Court denied review.

## What happened

Police went to Reel's North Carolina home after receiving tips about drug activity. An officer followed a visitor to the front door, smelled marijuana when Reel opened it, and after Reel shut the door, officers forced it open and found drugs inside. Reel's motion to suppress the evidence was denied, and he pleaded guilty while reserving his right to appeal. The North Carolina Court of Appeals affirmed, with one judge dissenting, and the North Carolina Supreme Court affirmed without issuing an opinion of its own.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether police conduct a search under the Fourth Amendment, which protects against unreasonable searches, when they enter the area immediately around a home to carry out a knock-and-talk visit while intending to gather evidence against the homeowner. Reel's petition says lower courts disagree about whether an officer's purpose for approaching a home matters to this question; the state's brief in opposition says there is no real disagreement and that Reel never raised this specific argument in the lower courts.

## Where it stands

The Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026, after considering it at two conferences; it also granted a motion allowing supporting groups to file a brief.

## What each outcome would mean

Reel, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves in place the North Carolina Supreme Court's decision affirming the denial of Reel's motion to suppress, meaning the evidence found in his home may be used against him. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the North Carolina courts' reasoning; it simply leaves that outcome standing without deciding the constitutional question Reel raised.
