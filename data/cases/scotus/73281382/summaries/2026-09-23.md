## What happened

Police in High Point, North Carolina, received tips that drugs were being sold from Quashaun Reel's home. Officers went to conduct a "knock-and-talk," an investigative visit that does not require a warrant. Rather than approaching the front door directly, they parked to the side, followed a female visitor to the door, and stood close behind her. When Reel opened the door for her and then shut it after seeing police, officers forced their way in, smelling marijuana, and found drugs inside. Reel's motion to suppress the evidence was denied by the trial court, and North Carolina's appellate courts upheld that decision, applying what they called a "knock-and-talk exception."

## What the Court is being asked

The case concerns limits on how police may approach a home without a warrant to speak with occupants. Everyone can walk up to someone's front door the way an ordinary visitor would, but the Court's precedent says officers cross a line if their real reason for being there is to search rather than simply to talk. The question is whether officers who enter a home's immediate surroundings for a knock-and-talk, but whose purpose is actually to gather evidence against the homeowner, have conducted a search that would normally require a warrant.

## Where it stands

North Carolina waived its right to respond to the petition, but the Court later asked for a response, which the state filed opposing review; Reel then filed a reply. Outside groups have filed briefs supporting Reel's position. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, where the Court will decide whether to take up the case.
