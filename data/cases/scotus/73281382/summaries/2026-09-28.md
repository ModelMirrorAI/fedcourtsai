## In brief

Quashaun Reel asks the Court to rule that police searched his home, without a warrant, when they conducted a "knock-and-talk" visit meant to gather evidence against him.

## What happened

Acting on anonymous tips about drug activity, High Point, North Carolina police went to Reel's home without a warrant. Officers parked to the side, followed a visitor to the front door, and smelled marijuana when Reel opened it. When Reel shut the door, officers forced it open and found drugs inside. Reel moved to suppress the evidence, arguing the officers' entry onto his property was an unconstitutional search rather than a permissible "knock-and-talk." The trial court denied the motion, and Reel pleaded guilty while preserving his right to appeal. North Carolina's Court of Appeals affirmed, over a dissent, and the North Carolina Supreme Court affirmed without an opinion.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether police carry out a search that requires a warrant when they enter the area immediately around a home to conduct a knock-and-talk visit if their actual purpose is to gather incriminating evidence against the homeowner, rather than simply to speak with them.

## Where it stands

North Carolina waived its right to respond but was then asked by the Court to file a response, which it did, opposing review; Reel has since filed a reply. The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, where it awaits a decision on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Reel is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide what limits the Constitution places on knock-and-talk visits; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the North Carolina Supreme Court's decision affirming the denial of Reel's suppression motion stays in place, leaving his conviction intact. North Carolina argues the case is a poor vehicle for review because Reel did not raise this specific argument in the lower courts.
