## In brief

Nielsen asks the Supreme Court to decide whether a federal prisoner's lawsuit over inadequate medical care fits within the Court's limited precedent allowing such damages claims.

## What happened

Kekai Watanabe, a federal inmate, was badly beaten during a prison riot and alleges that Francis Nielsen, a prison nurse, refused to send him to a hospital despite his severe pain, calling him a "cry baby." Watanabe was later found to have a fractured tailbone. He sued Nielsen for damages, relying on a Supreme Court precedent, Carlson v. Green, that allows such suits in limited circumstances against federal officials for deliberate indifference to a prisoner's medical needs. A federal district court dismissed the suit, ruling Watanabe's claim fell outside that precedent. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reversed, holding the claim was not meaningfully different from the precedent and could proceed; one judge dissented, and the full appeals court declined to rehear the case over several dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a prisoner's claim that medical staff ignored a serious but non-fatal injury is similar enough to the Court's past approved case to proceed, or whether differences — such as the severity and immediacy of the harm, and the existence of other complaint procedures prisoners can use — mean the claim cannot go forward absent new authorization from Congress. The Court is also asked more broadly whether it should eliminate this type of damages claim against federal officials altogether.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued and briefed on the merits, with the United States and numerous other groups filing supporting briefs; it now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Nielsen, the prison nurse, asked the Court to take up the case after losing before the Ninth Circuit. If the Court rules for Nielsen, Watanabe's lawsuit would be dismissed and he could not pursue damages for his treatment. If the Court rules for Watanabe, the Ninth Circuit's decision allowing the suit to proceed would stand, and the case would continue in the lower courts toward further proceedings on the merits of his claim. The Court could also rule narrowly on the specific legal question without resolving who ultimately wins the underlying dispute.
