## In brief

Teck Metals asks the Supreme Court to review a Ninth Circuit ruling allowing the Colville Tribes to pursue damages against it for lost cultural uses of a contaminated river.

## What happened

The Colville Tribes sued Teck, a Canadian mining company, claiming decades of discharges from its British Columbia smelter contaminated the Upper Columbia River, harming fish and other organisms. Beyond damages the Tribes sought jointly with Washington State, the Tribes separately sought damages tied to their unique cultural relationship with the river. A federal district court ruled that such cultural-related damages are not recoverable under the federal cleanup law known as CERCLA and granted Teck summary judgment on those claims. The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding the law does not categorically bar recovering lost-use damages that have a cultural dimension, and sent the case back for trial. The appeals court denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the federal law allowing lawsuits over damaged natural resources permits compensation when the harm is described as tribal members' felt loss of cultural connection to those resources, or whether such damages fall outside what the law allows.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference. Teck filed its reply after the Tribes opposed the petition, and briefing is complete. On October 5, 2026, the Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views, so the petition remains pending without further action yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Teck Metals, the company found liable below, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether this kind of cultural-loss damages claim is allowed under the law, resolving nothing until it rules. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, and the Tribes' claims for these damages proceed to trial in the lower courts. Because the Court has asked for the Solicitor General's views first, any decision on whether to hear the case will wait until that brief is filed.
