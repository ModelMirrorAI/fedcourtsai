## In brief

The Court agreed to hear the federal government's bid to reverse a ruling that struck down a presidential proclamation barring asylum for migrants crossing the southern border without authorization.

## What happened

In January 2025, President Trump issued a proclamation suspending entry for migrants crossing the southern border outside ports of entry and barring them from applying for asylum or other protections against removal. Several immigrant-advocacy organizations and individual migrants sued, arguing the proclamation and related agency guidance violated federal immigration law. A district court ruled for the challengers, certifying a broad class and blocking the policy. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit largely affirmed, holding the president lacked authority to eliminate asylum this way, though it narrowed the class definition. The government then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the proclamation and its implementing guidance, which cut off access to asylum for migrants who crossed the border a certain way, are lawful; whether a federal law limiting courts' power to block enforcement of certain removal provisions prevented the lower courts from issuing class-wide relief; and whether the class of affected migrants was properly defined under the rules governing class-action lawsuits.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted the government's petition on November 13, 2026, limited to the first two questions presented (the asylum policy's lawfulness and whether the class-wide relief was barred by the removal-provision law). The case has not yet been briefed or argued on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, represented by Secretary Mullin and other officials, is asking the Court to overturn the appeals court's decision, which favored the migrants and organizations that sued. If the Court ultimately reverses, the asylum restrictions and related removal procedures could go into effect, and the class-wide injunction blocking them would likely be lifted. If the Court affirms, the lower court's decision striking down the asylum bar would stand, keeping the block on the policy in place. The Court could also vacate the decision and send the case back for further proceedings without resolving the dispute for either side.
