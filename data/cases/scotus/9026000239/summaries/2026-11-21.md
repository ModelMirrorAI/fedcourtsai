## In brief

The government asked the Supreme Court to review a ruling striking down a proclamation barring asylum for border crossers, and the Court agreed to hear it.

## What happened

In January 2025, the President issued a proclamation suspending entry of noncitizens crossing the southern border outside legal ports and barring them from applying for asylum, withholding of removal, or related protections. Immigration advocacy groups and individual migrants sued, arguing this exceeded the President's authority under immigration law. A federal district court agreed, certified a class of affected people, and blocked enforcement. The U.S. Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit largely affirmed, holding the asylum bar unlawful while narrowing the class definition. The government then asked the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked three things: whether the proclamation and its enforcement guidance, which cut off access to asylum and related protections, are lawful; whether a federal law limiting courts' power to block immigration enforcement barred the broad court order issued here; and whether the group of people covered by the lawsuit was properly defined under class-action rules and constitutional limits on who can sue.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, then the Supreme Court granted review, but only as to the first two questions concerning the proclamation's lawfulness and the limits on court-ordered relief; it did not grant review of the class-definition question.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, asked the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling, which had sided with the Refugee and Immigrant Center for Education and Legal Services and other challengers. Because the Court granted review, the case will now receive full briefing and argument before a decision. If the Court sides with the government, the proclamation's asylum restrictions and related enforcement procedures could take effect. If it sides with the challengers, the lower court's block on the proclamation would stand. The Court could also send the case back for further proceedings without resolving the issue for either side.
