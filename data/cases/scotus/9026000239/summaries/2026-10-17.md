## In brief

The federal government asks the Supreme Court to review a ruling that blocked a proclamation barring asylum for migrants crossing the southern border illegally.

## What happened

In January 2025, the President issued a proclamation suspending entry for migrants crossing the southern border outside legal ports and barring them from applying for asylum, withholding of removal, or related protections. The Department of Homeland Security issued guidance implementing it. Immigrant advocacy groups and individual migrants sued, arguing the proclamation and guidance violate immigration law. A federal district court agreed, certified a class of affected migrants, and blocked enforcement. The D.C. Circuit affirmed, with one judge dissenting in part. The full appeals court declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the President had authority to bar migrants from applying for asylum through this proclamation, whether a federal law limiting court injunctions in immigration cases prevented the lower courts from blocking enforcement classwide, and whether the class of migrants covered by the order was properly defined under the rules governing class-action lawsuits.

## Where it stands

The petition, opposition brief, several amicus briefs, and the government's reply have all been filed. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, and the docket shows no action since.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the lower court. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions itself, deciding nothing at this stage. If it denies the petition, the D.C. Circuit's ruling stands, leaving in place the block on the asylum ban and the class-wide order favoring the migrant groups and individuals who sued. A grant does not mean the government will ultimately win, and a denial does not mean the appeals court's reasoning was necessarily correct—only that its judgment remains in effect.
