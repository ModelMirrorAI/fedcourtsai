## In brief

Federal officials ask the Supreme Court to review a ruling that blocked their policy of denying border-crossers access to asylum and fast-tracking their removal.

## What happened

In January 2025, President Trump issued a proclamation suspending entry for people crossing the southern border outside official checkpoints and barring them from applying for asylum or related protections, citing an overwhelmed immigration system. Immigrant advocacy groups and thirteen individuals sued, arguing the policy violates immigration law. A federal district court agreed, ruling for a class of affected people and blocking the policy's asylum restrictions and new removal procedures. A divided court of appeals panel largely affirmed, holding that the law requires individualized asylum consideration and that only existing statutory removal procedures may be used. Officials, led by the Secretary of Homeland Security, now ask the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the President had authority to suspend border-crossers' access to asylum and other protections and to remove them through new procedures instead of the ones immigration law otherwise sets out; whether a law limiting courts' power to block enforcement of removal rules on a group-wide basis stops the lower courts' broad order; and whether the group of people covered by that order was defined too broadly to meet the usual legal requirements for class-action lawsuits.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and docketed on August 24, 2026, with the challengers' response due September 23, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on it; the lower court's ruling remains on hold pending the outcome here.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal officials are asking the Court to step in. If the Court agrees to hear the case, it will decide the legal questions itself, without yet favoring either side. If it declines, the appeals court's ruling favoring the advocacy groups and individuals stands, and the challenged asylum restrictions and removal procedures would take effect as blocked by that ruling, subject to further proceedings below. A denial would not mean the Court found the lower court correct—only that its decision remains in place.
