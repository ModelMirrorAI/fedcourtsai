# Predicted judgment and lineup

The Court will **affirm in part and reverse in part**, remanding for proceedings consistent with its opinion. The decisive division will be **6–3**: Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett in the majority; Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson dissenting from the decision to disturb the judgment. The government will obtain meaningful relief on discretionary asylum and the injunction's scope, but not wholesale judicial approval of every component of its repatriation regime. The precise judgment label is less certain than the prediction that the judgment changes.

# Predicted ground

The majority will distinguish permission to seek asylum from entitlement to obtain it. It will read §1158(b)(1)(A) to permit categorical exercises of asylum-denial discretion and reject the proposition that §1158(a)(1) invariably requires individual applications once a lawful categorical discretionary policy forecloses a grant. That will displace the lower court's insistence on individualized consideration in this setting. The majority will characterize the policy as an exercise of discretion rather than an additional eligibility condition requiring promulgation under §1158(b)(2)(C).

This is a prediction of the disputed statutory holding, not an assumption that using the word “discretion” settles it. The dissent will argue that the distinction empties the express application right of meaning and lets the Executive evade Congress's limits on categorical asylum restrictions.

# Predicted breadth and remedy

The Court will confine its favorable holding to discretionary asylum for persons covered by the entry suspension. It will not hold that a presidential invocation of invasion or entry-suspension powers can generally override Congress's mandatory protections against persecution or torture. Restrictions on nonstatutory removal that are necessary to preserve those protections will survive in substance. The government has not defended their wholesale elimination in the merits arguments of its petition.

On Question 2, the Court will apply *Aleman Gonzalez* functionally: a lower court cannot escape §1252(f)(1) by styling an order as an injunction against the Proclamation if the order actually directs the operation of covered removal provisions. It will require narrowing of that part of the classwide injunction. It will not treat collateral effects on removal alone as sufficient, or equate the challenged injunction with the separate vacatur of implementing guidance. The independent agency-vacatur component helps explain the partial rather than total reversal.

The Court will not decide the ungranted Question 3 about the class definition as a separate holding. There is no present record basis to expect recusal, equal division, or a DIG, although a policy change could create a later procedural exit. I expect a signed opinion after briefing and argument, most likely by the end of June 2027.

# Predicted writers

Roberts is my narrow first choice to deliver the opinion, with Kavanaugh nearly as likely and Alito a substantial alternative. A bounded opinion accommodating executive discretion while reserving mandatory statutory protections favors Roberts; the remedial precedent favors Alito. Sotomayor is the likeliest principal dissenting author, with Jackson another plausible writer. Thomas or Gorsuch may write separately about executive authority or the limits of the majority's statutory rationale. These are uncertain writing forecasts, as reflected in the distributions rather than a confident assignment claim.
