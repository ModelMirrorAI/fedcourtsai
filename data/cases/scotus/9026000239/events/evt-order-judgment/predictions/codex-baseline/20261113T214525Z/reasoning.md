# Case-specific reasons

**The government's strongest merits argument is discretionary asylum, not an unlimited expulsion power.** The petition, printed pages 16–23, and cert reply, pages 2–8, distinguish eligibility to apply under 8 U.S.C. §1158(a)(1) from discretion to grant under §1158(b)(1)(A). They argue that a permissible categorical denial policy makes individual applications futile. The petition also distinguishes parole's express case-by-case language and invokes decisions allowing agencies to resolve common issues categorically. This provides a narrower path to a government victory than treating entry and expulsion as constitutionally interchangeable. Judge Walker's partial dissent in the provisioned decision below develops this distinction, while the earlier stay posture described in the filings shows it is not an invented interpretation of a silent record. Neither a lower-court stay nor a dissent is a Supreme Court merits holding.

**The opposition identifies the central weakness.** Its pages 18–23 distinguish lawful categorical criteria from a categorical extinction of the application right. Section 1158(a)(1) expressly permits applications regardless of status and manner of arrival; §1158(b)(2)(C) requires additional eligibility restrictions to be established by regulation and consistently with the statute. Calling a universal exclusion a discretionary policy may not escape those limits. The opposition also raises the mismatch between the instruments' contemporaneous rationale and the litigation theory. These points make an affirmance entirely plausible, particularly if Roberts, Barrett, or Gorsuch treats the application language as independently controlling. The government's response that presidential action is not agency action does not automatically answer every objection to the implementing guidance.

**The remedy question adds a second route, but it is not automatic.** Petition pages 23–25 and reply pages 8–9 characterize the injunction as effectively compelling statutory removal procedures governed by §1252(f)(1). The opposition, pages 28–32, and the majority below, printed pages 49–53, instead describe an injunction against a nonstatutory regime grounded in provisions outside Part IV. I verified the relevant precedent through CourtListener: *Garland v. Aleman Gonzalez*, 596 U.S. 543 (2022), opinion 6349227. It bars classwide orders controlling covered INA provisions, but footnote 4 distinguishes collateral effects from injunctions actually running against those provisions. The precedent supports close functional scrutiny, not a rule that all immigration class relief is forbidden. Its six-Justice majority included the six Justices I forecast in the majority here.

I expect some narrowing of injunction provisions that effectively prescribe covered removal procedures. I do **not** assume that this would erase the agency-guidance vacatur or every injunction protecting asylum and torture claims. The opposition emphasizes that the government has not separately challenged the determination that §1252(f)(1) does not bar vacatur. That surviving remedial component, and the government's failure to defend elimination of mandatory withholding and CAT protections on the merits, favor a mixed result over blanket approval of the entire repatriation regime.
