## Anchor and adjustment

The committed statpack's sal-v4 table matches the frozen band. Pooling every displayed strictly prior Term, 2017 through 2025, gives 143 grant-family outcomes over 203 weighted resolved federal petitions, or 70.44%. I computed this from statpack.json's federal prefix_est_grant_rate and prefix_weighted_resolved fields rather than averaging rounded percentages. The own-Term 2026 row is excluded. This is a relatively small and heterogeneous historical sample, not an 86% empirical rate for asylum cases. The pack version consulted is the committed artifact last changed at ea0a1e70a on October 12, 2026 at 21:45:55 UTC; I did not query or refresh the remote corpus and make no claim about its present freshness. Case-specific facts are those provisioned on October 13, not an independently refreshed docket.

I raise the 70.44% federal-petitioner anchor to **P(any grant) = 86%**. The principal positive adjustment is the importance of the underlying question: a final appellate judgment constraining a nationwide presidential border and asylum policy. That is more likely to command review than a routine government loss. The petition's introduction and pages 30–32 emphasize nationwide consequences and the asserted inability of another circuit to develop a conflict because of the class's breadth. Those are advocacy, not established causal findings about border traffic. The four amicus entries, including Iowa and other states, reinforce salience, but are neither four independent merits signals nor a substitute for the federal baseline. Government sponsorship is already in that baseline and receives no second bonus.

There are substantial offsets. The opposition's introduction and pages 18–22 contend that the discretionary-denial theory was not the agency's actual rationale, invoking Chenery, and that it evades Congress's rulemaking and asylum-application requirements. Its pages 28–36 argue that unchallenged vacatur independently limits the usefulness of reviewing the injunction and that the class objections involve future membership rather than currently uninjured members. The absence of an established square circuit split, partial abandonment of withholding/CAT defenses, and possible narrowing of the grant make near-certainty inappropriate. A persuasive merits defense can coexist with a high likelihood of review; my number does not predict that the government ultimately wins.

The reply's pages 2–10 squarely dispute the eligibility/discretion distinction and defend both remedial questions. Its page 12 identifies DHS v. D.V.D. as a granted companion concerning Section 1252(f)(1), reports a December 9, 2026 argument setting, and expressly suggests a hold. I use this solely as pre-decision information supplied by the October 6 reply, not as independently verified scheduling or as knowledge of either case's outcome. The overlap supports a meaningful hold or later GVR possibility, but it does not resolve the distinct asylum-policy question. That keeps plenary review more likely than summary disposal.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The paid-segment relist and CVSG cuts were read as terminal population descriptions, not forward hazards. For example, the displayed no-relist bucket has about 1.7% grant-family outcomes and the one-relist bucket about 13.3%; those private-and-government mixed terminal buckets cannot replace this federal risk-set anchor. Nor does the CVSG bucket's roughly 34.9% grant-family rate imply a chance of requesting views here.

I assign 60% to at least one additional distribution because of the number of questions, likely grant scrutiny, and the companion-case complication. It is only moderately above one-half: review could be granted at the first conference, and a silent hold need not generate another distribution immediately. I assign only 0.5% to a new CVSG because the Solicitor General already presents the government's position as petitioner.

Conditional on any grant, I assign 18% to disposal in the cert order, chiefly a later companion-driven GVR or narrower remedial resolution rather than immediate summary reversal. The remaining 82% is a plenary route. Conditional on denial, I assign 45% to some recorded dissent or statement: the executive-authority controversy makes a writing plausible, but denial without explanation remains slightly more likely. These latter numbers are judgments, not published conditioned frequencies. I omit per-Justice votes rather than imply that cert votes are publicly observable.

The stakes score is 0.94 because the substantive question reaches nationwide asylum access and the relationship between congressional protections and executive immigration power, with additional consequences for classwide judicial remedies. It is independent of my grant probability and does not depend on adopting either side's disputed account of migration trends.
