## In brief

A former employee asks the Court to review whether dismissing his disability discrimination lawsuit as a discovery sanction violated due process while his accommodations request sat undecided.

## What happened

Ryan Hauber, representing himself, sued his former employer, Honkamp Krueger & Co., P.C., alleging disability and medical-leave violations. After Hauber's attorney withdrew, he filed a motion asking the court for accommodations based on PTSD. While that motion remained pending, the district court granted Honkamp's motion to dismiss the case with prejudice as a sanction for discovery violations, ruling Hauber had willfully failed to disclose medical records, and it dismissed the accommodations motion as moot. The Eighth Circuit affirmed, and the district court later denied Hauber's motions for relief from judgment, acknowledging it had never explicitly ruled on the accommodations request.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's guarantee of a fair hearing process allows a federal court to end a disabled, self-represented litigant's case as punishment for discovery problems before ruling on that litigant's pending, medically supported request for help participating in the case.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conferences on September 28 and November 6, 2026, with no recorded action on it since. Honkamp waived its right to respond, and Hauber has since filed a supplemental brief and a motion asking the Court to delay considering his petition so it can be reviewed alongside two other pending petitions.

## What each outcome would mean

Hauber, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide the constitutional question, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Eighth Circuit's decision affirming dismissal of Hauber's lawsuit stands, and the case remains closed. The Court could also grant Hauber's pending motion to delay consideration until a later conference, which would postpone any decision on review without resolving the case either way.
