I expect the petition to be distributed at least once more—and likely relisted one additional time after the post-BIO distribution as the chambers examine the vehicle and preservation arguments raised by the government—before the Court enters a final order denying certiorari.
2. **Call for the Views of the Solicitor General (`cvsg-increment` = 0.00):** No CVSG will issue because the United States is already the respondent represented by the Solicitor General.
3. **Disposition (`predicted_disposition` = `denied`, `probability` = 0.14):** The Court will most likely deny the petition for a writ of certiorari after reviewing the government's brief in opposition. Although the petition presents the open question reserved in Footnote 5 of *McIntosh v. United States*, 601 U.S. 330, 341 n.5 (2024)—whether Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.2(b)(4)(B)'s requirement that a forfeiture order be included when orally announcing the sentence is a mandatory claim-processing rule or a time-related directive—several vehicle flaws noticed by the First Circuit will persuade the Court to pass on this petition:
   - At the sentencing hearing, the prosecutor orally moved for forfeiture of "approximately $13.2 million" and requested that the district court defer ruling for one week while the Asset Recovery Unit filed a formal motion, and petitioner raised no contemporaneous objection under Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) at sentencing.
   - Petitioner did not specifically request an evidentiary hearing on traceability under Rule 32.2(b)(1)(B) when opposing forfeiture via his post-order motion for reconsideration and stay.
   - Because the case involves a cumulative breakdown across multiple steps of Rule 32.2 (no preliminary order under Rule 32.2(b)(2)(B), oral deferral at sentencing, and premature entry of the written order six days into the local-rule response window) rather than an isolated, preserved Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) oral-pronouncement objection where a preliminary order was properly entered, the Solicitor General will effectively argue that this case is an poor vehicle to isolate Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B).
4. **Question Presented and Merits Ground (Conditional on Grant):** If the Court grants certiorari, it will reformulate the multi-part, fact-bound question presented in the petition into a clean doctrinal question: *Whether a district court's failure to include a final forfeiture order when orally announcing the sentence, as required by Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.2(b)(4)(B), is a violation of a mandatory claim-processing rule or a procedural error subject to harmless-error review.* If granted, the Court would likely hold (consistent with the Sixth Circuit's post-*McIntosh* analysis in *United States v. Harrell*, 180 F.4th 888 (6th Cir. 2026)) that Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) is a time-related directive governing the district court's conduct rather than a mandatory claim-processing rule barring forfeiture altogether, while vacating and remanding because entering a final $13.2 million money judgment without a preliminary order and before the defendant's response time expired was not harmless error.
5. **Summary Disposition Route (`summary-disposition-route` = 0.12 conditional on grant):** Conditional on a grant, plenary review is far more likely than a summary disposition (88% plenary vs. 12% GVR or summary reversal). However, there is a non-trivial chance (~12% of total grant probability) that the Solicitor General confesses error in the brief in opposition regarding the district court's failure to wait for the 14-day response window under D. Mass. Local Rule 7.1(b)(2) before entering the $13.2 million forfeiture order, inviting a GVR to the First Circuit without conceding that Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) is a mandatory claim-processing rule.
6. **Separate Writings on Denial (`dissent-from-denial` = 0.06):** If the Court denies certiorari, it will most likely do so without recorded dissent or statement. There is a modest 6% chance of a statement respecting the denial or dissent from denial—most plausibly from Justice Sotomayor (the author of the unanimous opinion in *McIntosh*), Justice Gorsuch, Justice Thomas, or Justice Jackson—highlighting the due-process concerns when a $13.2 million forfeiture judgment is entered post-sentencing without a preliminary order or pre-deprivation opportunity to respond.
