# Certiorari Disposition Prediction: *Christopher Ponzo v. United States* (No. 26-285)

## I. Executive Summary

- **Event:** `evt-order-call-for-response-disposition` (forecast at the `response-requested` moment following the September 17, 2026 call for a response)
- **Predicted Disposition:** `denied` (`granted: false`)
- **Outcome Probabilities:** `denied`: **0.87** | `granted`: **0.12** | `gvr`: **0.01**

Petitioner Christopher Ponzo asks the Court to decide the question explicitly left open in footnote 5 of *McIntosh v. United States*, 601 U.S. 330, 341 n.5 (2024): whether a district court's failure to orally pronounce a final order of forfeiture at sentencing in violation of Federal Rule of Criminal Procedure 32.2(b)(4)(B) violates a mandatory claim-processing rule requiring automatic vacatur of the forfeiture judgment, or instead violates a time-related directive subject to harmless-error review (and, if the latter, how harmlessness is measured).

Although the petition frames an attractive legal issue tied directly to a question reserved in *McIntosh*—which readily explains why the Court called for a response after the Solicitor General initially waived—a close inspection of the First Circuit's opinion (*United States v. Ponzo*, 171 F.4th 507 (1st Cir. 2026)) and the district court's orders reveals severe vehicle and preservation defects that the Solicitor General's Brief in Opposition will surface:

1. **Failure to Object at Sentencing:** Even if Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) were a mandatory claim-processing rule, *McIntosh* confirms that a party must timely invoke a claim-processing deadline before it passes (601 U.S. at 337). At sentencing, when the prosecutor orally moved for approximately $13.2 million in forfeiture and asked the court to defer ruling for one week while the Asset Recovery Unit filed a formal motion, Ponzo raised no objection to deferring the forfeiture order past sentencing (Pet. App. 22a).
2. **Actual Notice at Sentencing Under Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B)'s Alternative Clause:** Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) requires that the court include the forfeiture when orally announcing the sentence *"or must otherwise ensure that the defendant knows of the forfeiture at sentencing."* Here, the Superseding Indictment and Rule 11 colloquy gave notice of forfeiture of all proceeds ($36 million gross revenue), the Government's pre-sentencing memorandum identified Ponzo's $13.2 million net profit from 2014–2020 right before requesting forfeiture, and the Government stated at the sentencing hearing that it sought approximately $13.2 million in forfeiture (Pet. App. 21a–22a & n.17, 38a–39a).
3. **Post-Sentencing Reconsideration and Waiver of Any Hearing Request:** Ponzo's core procedural complaint is that after the Government filed its post-sentencing forfeiture motion on February 7, 2025, the district court signed the order six days later (February 13, 2025) before his 14-day local-rule response period expired. Yet Ponzo filed his full written opposition with a motion for reconsideration on February 19, 2025, never requested an evidentiary hearing under Rule 32.2(b)(1)(B) (waiving any hearing argument below and on appeal, Pet. App. 22a n.19), and had his substantive causation theory considered and rejected by both the district court (Pet. App. 34a) and the First Circuit *de novo* (Pet. App. 17a–20a).

Because those threshold preservation and vehicle flaws prevent this case from cleanly testing whether a preserved Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) violation requires automatic vacatur, the probability of a certiorari grant is modest (**0.12**), and certiorari denial (**0.87**) is the strongly favored outcome.

## III. Assessment of Certworthiness

### 1. The Legal Question and Circuit Landscape
In *McIntosh v. United States*, 601 U.S. 330 (2024), the Court held that a district court's failure to enter a preliminary forfeiture order before sentencing under Fed. R. Crim. P. 32.2(b)(2)(B) violates a "time-related directive" subject to harmless-error review rather than a mandatory claim-processing rule. In footnote 5, Justice Sotomayor's opinion for the unanimous Court noted:
> "McIntosh's case does not implicate Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B)'s requirement that forfeiture be imposed at sentencing, which the Government acknowledged raises 'a harder set of issues.'" 601 U.S. at 341 n.5.

Petitioner identifies two circuit conflicts regarding Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B):
- **Mandatory Claim-Processing Rule vs. Time-Related Directive:** Prior to *McIntosh*, the Seventh Circuit (*United States v. Lee*, 77 F.4th 565, 582–83 (7th Cir. 2023); *United States v. Skaggs*, 78 F.4th 990, 993 (7th Cir. 2023)) and the Fifth Circuit (*United States v. Marquez*, 685 F.3d 501, 509 (5th Cir. 2012); *United States v. Brooks*, 78 F.4th 138, 141 (5th Cir. 2023)) treated Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B)'s requirement that forfeiture be included in the oral sentence as mandatory when objected to. Following *McIntosh*, however, the only two circuits to decide published cases on Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B)—the First Circuit in *Ponzo*, 171 F.4th at 519, and the Sixth Circuit in *United States v. Harrell*, 180 F.4th 888, 901–03 (6th Cir. 2026)—both concluded that *McIntosh*'s framework renders Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) a time-related directive subject to harmless-error review, joining the Third, Fourth, and Eleventh Circuits' harmless-error approach.
- **Standard for Harmless Error:** Petitioner further argues that the First Circuit split from *Harrell* and other circuits on how harmless error is evaluated, contending that other circuits require pre-sentencing notice of the exact amount and a pre-order opportunity to be heard, whereas the First Circuit relied on the district court's post-order rejection of Ponzo's reconsideration/stay motions and its own *de novo* rejection of his legal theory on traceability.

While the question reserved in *McIntosh* footnote 5 is genuinely certworthy in the abstract and may eventually warrant review in a clean case, the post-*McIntosh* circuit decisions (*Ponzo* and *Harrell*) actually *agree* on the primary threshold question (that Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) is a time-related directive subject to harmless-error review), and the secondary dispute over how harmlessness was applied to Ponzo's particular record is heavily factbound.

### 2. Severe Vehicle and Preservation Defects
The Solicitor General's Brief in Opposition will almost certainly argue that this case is an unsuitable vehicle to resolve the *McIntosh* footnote 5 question for four independent reasons documented in the First Circuit's opinion:

1. **Forfeiture / Acquiescence at the Sentencing Hearing:**
   Under *McIntosh*, the defining feature of a mandatory claim-processing rule is that "[i]f the affected party alerts the court to the deadline and invokes its protection, the relevant action cannot be taken after the deadline has passed." 601 U.S. at 337. Conversely, a party who fails to object when the district court proposes to defer forfeiture past the sentencing hearing forfeits any claim-processing bar and is relegated to plain-error review under Rule 52(b). Here, as the First Circuit explicitly noted, at the February 5, 2025 sentencing hearing the prosecutor moved for forfeiture, stated the Government sought approximately $13.2 million, and asked the court to defer ruling so the Asset Recovery Unit could file a formal motion within one week—and Ponzo **"didn't object when the government orally moved at sentencing for him to forfeit 'approximately [$]13.2 million' or when the judge asked the government to file a formal forfeiture motion '[w]ithin one week.'"** (Pet. App. 22a; see also Pet. 10, 32). Even in the Seventh Circuit (*Lee*, 77 F.4th at 582–83; *Skaggs*, 78 F.4th at 993), a defendant must object to the failure to impose forfeiture at sentencing to trigger mandatory claim-processing relief.

2. **Notice at Sentencing Under Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B):**
   Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) is written in the disjunctive: "The court must include the forfeiture when orally announcing the sentence **or must otherwise ensure that the defendant knows of the forfeiture at sentencing.**" Here, the record shows that Ponzo knew at sentencing that the Government was seeking forfeiture of his ~$13.2 million net profits from the Mass Save scheme:
   - The Superseding Indictment contained a forfeiture allegation for all proceeds traceable to the wire-fraud counts, and at the Rule 11 plea hearing the Government recited that CAP Electric obtained ~$36 million and advised Ponzo of forfeiture (Pet. App. 21a, 37a–38a).
   - The Government's pre-sentencing memorandum (at pp. 26–27 & n.8) specifically stated that CAP Electric's net income from the scheme from 2014 to 2020 was approximately $13.2 million and requested forfeiture immediately after noting the "over $13 million in personal profit" (Pet. App. 21a & n.17, 38a).
   - At the sentencing hearing itself, the Government orally moved for forfeiture of "approximately $13.2 million" and the district court deferred entry of the formal order for one week (Pet. App. 22a, 39a).
   Thus, there is a substantial threshold dispute over whether Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B)'s notice requirement ("otherwise ensure that the defendant knows of the forfeiture at sentencing") was even violated, or whether the district court and parties effectively agreed at sentencing to defer the mechanics of the final order by one week.

3. **Full Consideration of Petitioner's Legal Theory on Reconsideration and Waiver of an Evidentiary Hearing:**
   Petitioner's strongest rhetorical point is that after the Government filed its formal motion on February 7, 2025, the district court entered the forfeiture order six days later (February 13, 2025), prior to the expiration of the 14-day response period under D. Mass. Local Rule 7.1(b)(2). However:
   - On February 19, 2025, Ponzo filed his complete written opposition alongside a motion for reconsideration, followed by a motion to stay forfeiture pending appeal (Pet. App. 22a).
   - As the First Circuit noted in footnote 19 (Pet. App. 22a), Ponzo's opposition/reconsideration motion **never requested an evidentiary hearing** under Rule 32.2(b)(1)(B), and he waived any hearing-denial claim on appeal by failing to raise it in his opening brief.
   - Instead, Ponzo's opposition rested on a pure legal/conceptual contention based on undisputed facts: because he "cold-called" and self-generated 93% of the customers, he argued that his $13.2 million in net profits were not "but-for" proceeds of the >$1 million in bribes he paid to the CLEAResult managers (Darlington and Marra).
   - Both the district court (in its March 19, 2025 Memorandum & Order, Pet. App. 34a) and the First Circuit (reviewing the legal question *de novo*, Pet. App. 17a–20a) squarely addressed and rejected that contention on the merits, holding that because every Mass Save project had to be scoped and approved by CLEAResult project managers—whom Ponzo paid $1,000/week in cash, a John Deere tractor, a MacBook, and other bribes while they approved and inflated contracts by millions of dollars—all net profits from the scheme satisfied the preponderance standard for "but-for" traceability under 18 U.S.C. § 981(a)(1)(C) and § 981(a)(2)(B) (which already deducts direct costs of providing lawful services sold in an illegal manner).

4. **Lack of Prejudice Under Any Circuit's Harmless-Error Standard:**
   Because Ponzo never requested an evidentiary hearing under Rule 32.2(b)(1)(B) and his challenge to the $13.2 million net-profit figure turned on a legal theory ("self-generated customers") that both courts rejected as a matter of law on the undisputed guilty-plea admissions, the First Circuit's harmless-error conclusion does not squarely conflict with *Harrell* or other circuits. Even if the district court had waited eight more days (from February 13 to February 19, 2025) to read Ponzo's written opposition before signing the forfeiture order rather than rejecting that exact filing on reconsideration, the outcome would have been identical.

## IV. Conclusion and Probability Calibration

- **Denied (`0.87`):** Most likely outcome. Once the Solicitor General files the Brief in Opposition detailing Ponzo's failure to object at sentencing when the court deferred the forfeiture motion for one week, the pre-sentencing disclosure of the $13.2 million net-profit figure, his waiver of any request for an evidentiary hearing under Rule 32.2(b)(1)(B), and the district court's post-order consideration of his written opposition on reconsideration, the Court is very likely to deny certiorari and await a cleaner vehicle where a defendant contemporaneously objected to a post-sentencing forfeiture order.
- **Granted (`0.12`):** There remains a non-trivial ~12% chance that four Justices wish to resolve the *McIntosh* footnote 5 question now—given the recurring nature of Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) errors and the recent published opinions in *Ponzo* (1st Cir.) and *Harrell* (6th Cir.)—and view the district court's premature entry of the post-sentencing order six days after the Government's motion as an arresting illustration of lax Rule 32.2 compliance.
- **GVR (`0.01`):** Minimal probability; *McIntosh* was decided in 2024 and already applied by the First Circuit below, and no pending case on the OT 2026 merits docket controls this question.
