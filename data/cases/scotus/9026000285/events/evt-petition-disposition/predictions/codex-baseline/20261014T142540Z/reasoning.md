## Why raise 5% to 18%?

**Positive evidence.** The response request after an express waiver is an actual act of Court attention, beyond the baseline band's simple distribution count. It materially raises my subjective grant odds, although the pack provides no matched conditional response-request rate that would justify a mechanical adjustment. The petition presents a recognizable rule-interpretation issue rather than only disagreement over the forfeiture amount: it distinguishes final forfeiture at sentencing under Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B) from a late preliminary order. McIntosh v. United States, 601 U.S. 330, 340–41 n.5 (2024), independently checked through CourtListener opinion 9960861, expressly left the final-order issue outside its decision and discussed the Seventh Circuit's differing characterization. That reservation makes this a plausible follow-up case. The petition's $13.2 million forfeiture, alleged lack of a pre-order opportunity to respond, and claimed cross-circuit disagreement give the procedural question concrete stakes. See petition pp. 2–4, 13–14, 19–31.

**Reasons not to go much higher.** The appendix is substantially less one-sided than the petition's description. The First Circuit relied on notice in the indictment and plea hearing, the sentencing memorandum's reference to more than $13 million in profit, the government's sentencing request for approximately $13.2 million, the absence of an immediate objection, and later judicial consideration of the defendant's opposition. Its footnote 19 also describes a preservation problem concerning a requested forfeiture hearing. Those are the lower court's stated grounds, not findings I independently establish; they nevertheless suggest a vehicle burden involving notice, preservation, and prejudice. The court's treatment is principally harmless-error reasoning rather than a developed express holding classifying Rule 32.2(b)(4)(B). See petition appendix pp. 19a–23a.

The asserted conflict deserves attention but should not be accepted at the petition's full advertised breadth. Its survey mixes published and unpublished decisions, other Rule 32.2 subdivisions, supervised-release analogies, and pre-McIntosh authorities. I independently read United States v. Skaggs, 78 F.4th 990 (7th Cir. 2023), CourtListener opinion 9658456. Its discussion supports the preliminary/final distinction, but its actual disposition vacated a much later postjudgment preliminary order while leaving the original judgment in force; its discussion of reversing on direct appeal was conditional. It is not an identical procedural vehicle. The petition's account of Harrell and the remaining circuits was not independently verified, and I treat its detailed alignment as advocacy rather than an established five-to-five split.

These considerations warrant a meaningful uplift above the approximately 5% anchor while leaving denial clearly most likely. **18%** is my judgmental synthesis, not a fitted model or an empirically measured response-request conversion rate. The principal uncertainties are the government's forthcoming account of preservation and prejudice, and whether the Court sees a clean categorical conflict or a fact-specific application of McIntosh.

## Other claims and significance

The **94% further-distribution** probability reflects the chronology: a response was requested after the existing distribution, with its deadline after the scheduled conference. Another distribution after briefing can occur even if the Court never seriously considers granting. It is not inconsistent with an 18% grant probability. I infer no completed October 9 consideration from a scheduling entry alone.

The **0.2% CVSG** probability is distinct from that already-issued ordinary response request. With the federal government defending its own criminal judgment, a separate invitation for views adds little. The **20% summary-route probability is conditional on a grant**, not a 20% unconditional chance of summary relief. The open question and vehicle disputes favor full briefing if the Court takes the case; McIntosh is not intervening authority relative to the judgment under review. The **4% denial-writing probability is conditional on denial**; procedural fairness could attract a statement, but there is no specific signal identifying one.

The **0.38 big-case score** measures stakes, not grant odds. A decision could clarify nationally applicable safeguards against criminal forfeiture and involves substantial individual property loss, but the issue remains specialized criminal procedure. I omit cert votes because the record supplies no sound basis for an individual Justice lineup.
