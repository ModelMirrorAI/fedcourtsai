## In brief

Christopher Ponzo asks the Court to review a $13.2 million forfeiture order he says was entered without notice or a chance to be heard.

## What happened

Christopher Ponzo pleaded guilty to fraud charges involving a Massachusetts energy-efficiency program. At his sentencing, the government first specified an amount it wanted forfeited, and the district court later granted a forfeiture motion without giving Ponzo the full response period local rules allowed. Ponzo objected that he had never had a real opportunity to contest the $13.2 million figure. The First Circuit agreed that several procedural steps required by federal forfeiture rules had been skipped, but ruled the errors were harmless and affirmed the forfeiture order.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a court may finalize an order forcing a defendant to give up money or property when the government did not state the amount until sentencing, the forfeiture was not announced aloud at the sentencing hearing, the defendant could not respond before the forfeiture motion was granted, he had no chance to present evidence, and the order was entered when he was not present. The petition says the Court left this exact question open in an earlier case about a related timing rule for forfeiture orders.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, after which the Court asked for a response from the government; the United States had initially waived its right to respond. That response's deadline has since been extended to November 18, 2026, and the docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Ponzo, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether the forfeiture order was lawfully entered, deciding nothing about who wins at this stage. If it denies review, the First Circuit's decision upholding the $13.2 million forfeiture against Ponzo stands, though a denial would not mean the Court agrees that ruling was correct.
