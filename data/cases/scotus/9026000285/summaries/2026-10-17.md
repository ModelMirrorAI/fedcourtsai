## In brief

Christopher Ponzo asks the Court to review a $13.2 million criminal forfeiture order he says was entered without notice or a chance to be heard.

## What happened

Christopher Ponzo pleaded guilty to fraud charges involving bribes paid to employees of a company that managed an energy-efficiency program. At sentencing, the government first specified that it wanted $13.2 million forfeited, and the district court later granted that request without giving Ponzo a chance to respond beforehand or to appear in person. The First Circuit Court of Appeals acknowledged these procedural failures but called them harmless and affirmed the forfeiture. Ponzo now asks the Supreme Court to take up his case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a judge can order a defendant to forfeit property when the government did not say how much it wanted forfeited until sentencing, the forfeiture amount was never announced aloud at the sentencing hearing, the judge approved the forfeiture after sentencing without letting the defendant respond, and the defendant had no chance to present evidence or be present when the forfeiture was ordered. Ponzo's petition argues that federal appeals courts disagree about whether these requirements are strict rules requiring automatic reversal when violated, or more flexible rules subject to review for whether the violation actually mattered.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference. The United States waived its right to respond, but the Court then asked for a response, due October 19, 2026; the docket shows the government has since requested more time to file it. No action on the petition itself has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Ponzo, as petitioner, is asking the Court to hear his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide what the forfeiture rule requires; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the First Circuit's decision upholding the $13.2 million forfeiture against Ponzo stands, and the case ends there. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right—only that its decision remains in place.
