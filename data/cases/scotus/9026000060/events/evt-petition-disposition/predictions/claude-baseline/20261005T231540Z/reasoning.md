# Why these numbers

**P(grant) = 0.30.**

**Anchor.** `record/context.json` freezes `band: baseline` under `sal-v4`, mode `forward`, Term 2026, one distribution, no CVSG. The statpack's "Segment base rate by salience band (sal-v4)" table matches the context's salience version, so I pooled the `baseline` band's bracketed `reached` rate over every rendered Term strictly before 2026 (OT2017 through OT2025, the table's whole shown window): about 637 grants over a weighted risk-set denominator of 12,720, which is 5.0%. That is the yardstick my skill will be scored against. The whole-docket cut for CA4 petitions (grant plus GVR about 2.5%) and the relist-0 bucket (about 1.7% grant family) sit below it and I did not anchor on them.

**Adjustments up, in order of weight.**

1. The Court called for a response on August 26, 2026 after respondents waived. This is the strongest pre-conference signal available to a cert cell short of a relist or CVSG: a Justice or clerk read the petition and wanted the other side before it could be denied. The band does not condition on it. In my experience of the paid docket, called-for-response petitions grant at several times the rate of waived petitions, in the low-to-mid teens of percent unconditionally; combined with the signals below I treat this petition as well above that.
2. Published, divided Fourth Circuit opinion (King, J., joined by Wynn, J.; Quattlebaum, J., dissenting) that expressly adopts the Second Circuit's "predictable effect" reading and rejects the "determinative or coercive effect" reading of Bennett v. Spear. The petition frames a 3-3 split (CA5, CA6, CA11 against CA2, CA4, CA9), with a dissent by Judge Miller in the Ninth Circuit case as well. The split is asserted rather than tested by a BIO yet, but the circuit-level disagreement about what Department of Commerce did to Bennett is real and the Court has repeatedly shown appetite for traceability cases (Murthy, Alliance for Hippocratic Medicine, Diamond Alternative Energy).
3. Amicus support at the petition stage from 22 states, the National Shooting Sports Foundation (counsel Noel Francisco), and the NRA and Second Amendment Foundation. Three petition-stage amicus filings is a high count for a private-party petition and signals the stakes the Court responds to.
4. Elite counsel on both sides (Clement & Murphy for petitioners; ICAP at Georgetown for respondents), which raises the quality of the vehicle presentation and the Court's attention.
5. The subject matter. The Court took Smith & Wesson v. Mexico from an identical posture (reversal of a motion-to-dismiss ruling in favor of firearms manufacturers) and ruled for the industry unanimously, and the current Court is generally receptive to petitions that enforce Article III limits.

**Adjustments down.**

1. Interlocutory posture. The Fourth Circuit reversed a dismissal and remanded; the case will proceed below. The Court often prefers a final judgment, though it has granted threshold-jurisdiction questions from exactly this posture, so this is a moderate discount rather than a heavy one.
2. The question is arguably about pleading, not doctrine. Respondents will say the Fourth Circuit applied the "determinative or coercive effect" standard in the alternative and found it satisfied, so the case is a fact-bound disagreement about plausibility rather than a legal split; the Court frequently declines error-correction framed as standing law.
3. A standing dismissal in federal court would not end the litigation. The claims are Virginia state-law claims brought in diversity; a loss on Article III sends respondents to state court where Article III does not apply. That lowers the practical significance and gives the Court a reason to wait for a case where the traceability rule is dispositive.
4. The split is softer than the petition says. The Fifth and Sixth Circuit cases involved government defendants and jawboning theories, which Murthy largely addressed; the Court may regard the law as settled enough by Murthy to leave the circuits to apply it.
5. Base rates for the band itself: at a 5% anchor, even a very strong petition with a CFR usually ends in denial, and my own history of overweighting elite-counsel petitions with amicus support argues for restraint.

Netting these, I moved from the 5% anchor to roughly a 30% probability. I considered 0.35 to 0.40 on the strength of the CFR plus the state amici, and 0.20 on the interlocutory and state-court-alternative points, and settled in between.

**Disposition and binary.** With P(grant) under one half, `granted` is 0 and `predicted_disposition` is `denied`.

**Claims.**
- `disposition` 0.30, equal to the top-level probability.
- `relist-increment` 0.97. The snapshot shows one distribution (September 28 conference), from which the CFR removed the petition. After the BIO on October 26 the petition must be redistributed, which adds a distribution entry regardless of merit. The residual 0.03 is dismissal, settlement, or withdrawal before redistribution, which is rare in a case with this many defendants and amici. I note in `flags.json` that this increment prices a mechanical redistribution rather than a relist.
- `cvsg-increment` 0.06. No federal party or federal statute in the QP; the CVSG cut in the statpack shows CVSGs on roughly 1.2% of paid petitions, and I raise that modestly because the Court has already shown interest and the SG under the current administration is likely to support petitioners, which occasionally prompts an invitation. Still unlikely.
- `summary-disposition-route` 0.10, conditional on a grant. No intervening decision supports a GVR. A summary reversal of a divided published opinion on a contested standing standard is possible but the Court normally hears argument on a question with three amicus filings and a declared split. The prior-Term share of grants disposed of in the cert order (30% to 59% per the statpack's own caveat) is driven by GVRs, which do not fit this case, so I sit well below it.
- `dissent-from-denial` 0.35, conditional on a denial. The Court itself called for a response, so a denial would come after real consideration; Justice Thomas has a long record of writing on Article III and on firearms-industry cases, and 22 states plus a dissent below make a statement plausible. Most denials are silent, so I stay below one half.

**Big case score 0.6.** The stakes are national (the standard for suing manufacturers over marketing when a criminal third party does the harm, and the traceability rule for every third-party-harm suit in federal court), and the parties and amici guarantee press attention. It is not higher because the question is a jurisdictional threshold and a loss for respondents would move, not end, the litigation.

**What I read.** The snapshot `2026-10-05.json` (13 docket entries through the NRA/SAF amicus brief of September 24), `context.json`, `documents.json`, `questions-presented.txt`, and `petition.txt` (140 pages, flagged truncated; I read the question presented, statement, reasons, and vehicle section in full). No brief in opposition exists yet; the respondents' arguments above are my inference of what the BIO will say, not its text. Beyond the provisioned inputs I used the committed statpack, two `fedcourts query` calls, two CourtListener MCP calls (opinion search and the first chunk of the Fourth Circuit opinion to confirm the panel and vote line), and two web searches, which confirmed the CFR and the October 26 BIO date and surfaced no disposition. See `retrieval.md`.

**Where to discount me.** I have no BIO, so the vehicle objections are guessed. My CFR adjustment is drawn from general knowledge of the paid docket rather than from a committed statpack cut, which does not exist for this signal. I know of no companion case or pending grant on the same question, but my web check was brief. If the BIO shows the Fourth Circuit's alternative holding rests on specific allegations that the shooter encountered the marketing, the split framing weakens and I would drop toward 0.20.
