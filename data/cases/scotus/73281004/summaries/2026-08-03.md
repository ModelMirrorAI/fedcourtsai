## In brief

Oregon asked the Supreme Court to review a prisoners' lawsuit over its pandemic response, then the parties jointly dismissed the petition.

## What happened

Prisoners in Oregon's custody sued the state and corrections officials, arguing that Oregon's overall response to COVID-19 across its prisons between March 2020 and May 2022 violated their constitutional protection against cruel and unusual punishment. A federal district court denied the officials' request for qualified immunity, a legal protection that shields government officials from damages lawsuits unless they violated clearly established rights. A jury, the court said, could decide whether the state's pandemic measures were reasonable overall. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, and later denied rehearing. Oregon then asked the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

Oregon asked the Court to decide two things: first, whether prisoners can bring a single constitutional claim challenging a state's overall, multi-year pandemic response across many facilities, rather than specific actions by specific officials; and second, whether it was already clear, back in 2020, that such a broad response could count as cruel and unusual punishment, especially since a judge had ruled in June 2020 that Oregon's early response was constitutional.

## Where it stands

After the petition was filed, the respondents waived their right to respond, but the Court later asked for a response anyway. Before that response was due, the parties jointly asked to dismiss the petition, and the Court dismissed it in July 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Oregon, the officials who lost below, asked the Court to take up the case. The joint dismissal ends the case at the Supreme Court without any ruling on the questions raised. The Ninth Circuit's decision denying qualified immunity to the Oregon officials remains in place, meaning the prisoners' lawsuit can continue toward a jury trial on whether the state's pandemic response was reasonable. No broader legal rule was decided for other cases.
