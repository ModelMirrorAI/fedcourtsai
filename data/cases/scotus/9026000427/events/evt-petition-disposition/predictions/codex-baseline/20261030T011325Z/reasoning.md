## Why below the anchor

The strongest potential issue is QP2: whether an actual post-disclosure refusal to restore pay or correct a position description is a new failure to take a personnel action, rather than a continuing effect of an earlier decision. That is a conceivable statutory issue reaching beyond this employee. But the petition's argument, pp. 17–21, does not demonstrate competing appellate holdings on that distinction. QPs 1 and 3 are even more closely tied to the signature, position description, and sequence of disclosures in this record.

The attached decision is expressly nonprecedential. Its analysis, App. 6–8, applies the contributing-factor requirement and an established discrete-act/continuing-effects distinction, rather than announcing a new categorical prohibition on relief for every later retaliatory act. The petition asks the Court to recharacterize continued withholding as new decisions, but whether distinct decisions occurred is itself entangled with the facts. The contrast between February disclosures and the later April paperwork does not, by itself, establish that those disclosures caused an independent personnel action; the appendix separately addresses the later-numbered disclosures associated with the reassignment theory. I do not adopt the petition's broader characterization of the lower court's ruling as an uncontested fact.

The original administrative judge also rejected the protected-disclosure showing; the full Board did not reach that alternative ground after finding no contributing factor (App. 4–5). Thus success on the temporal theory would not itself establish an entitlement to whistleblower relief. The signature and hazardous-duty issues add factual layers rather than a clean, developed national conflict. These vehicle and certworthiness weaknesses, combined with a response waiver and no affirmative signal of Court interest yet, justify reducing the roughly 5.1% band anchor to 0.5%. A waiver is not proof that no Justice will request a response, and a nonprecedential decision is not an absolute bar to review.

For legal context I retrieved **Bosley v. Merit Systems Protection Board, 162 F.3d 665 (Fed. Cir. 1998)** through CourtListener, opinion 759750. Its opening and conclusion distinguish continued pay effects from separately alleged reprisals, with a partial remand on the latter. That makes the petition's concern intelligible but does not establish that Coleman presented an overlooked distinct act on a comparable record. Bosley predates Morgan; I do not adopt the petition's suggestion that the 1998 case applied the 2002 decision. An attempted official-rules web search and PDF retrieval yielded no usable returned content, so I rely on neither as verified additional authority.
