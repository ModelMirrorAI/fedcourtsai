# Cert-Stage Disposition Prediction: *Richard W. Coleman, Jr. v. Merit Systems Protection Board* (No. 26-427)

## 1. Case Overview and Procedural Posture

Petitioner Richard W. Coleman, Jr., proceeding *pro se* on a paid petition (No. 26-427, docketed September 29, 2026), seeks review of a June 10, 2026 nonprecedential *per curiam* decision of the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit (Lourie, Schall, and Taranto, JJ.), which affirmed a nonprecedential August 14, 2024 Final Order of the Merit Systems Protection Board (MSPB) dismissing his Individual Right of Action (IRA) appeal for lack of jurisdiction. Rehearing and rehearing *en banc* were denied on August 6, 2026.

According to the provisioned record:
- In 2008, Mr. Coleman was hired as a Supervisory Explosives Specialist in the Pentagon Force Protection Agency's (PFPA) Hazardous Devices Branch ("Bomb Squad"), receiving a 25% hazardous duty pay (HDP) supplement under 5 U.S.C. § 5545(d)(2) and 5 C.F.R. § 550.904.
- Following a fall 2014 anonymous complaint to the Department of Defense Office of Inspector General (DoD OIG) alleging that Bomb Squad employees were erroneously receiving HDP where hazardous duty was already accounted for in the classification of their positions, agency officials informed Bomb Squad employees on January 23, 2015 that the HDP supplement would be discontinued effective January 24, 2015. On April 24, 2015, the agency provided a written memorandum explaining its determination under 5 C.F.R. § 550.904(a) and updated the position descriptions (PDs) to remove references to HDP (reissued in July 2015).
- Beginning on February 2, 2015 and continuing through February 12, 2018, Mr. Coleman made eleven alleged protected disclosures to agency officials, the DoD OIG, the Office of Personnel Management (OPM), the Office of Special Counsel (OSC), and Members of Congress challenging the legality of the HDP discontinuation and alleging defects in the revised position description (including an allegedly duplicated electronic signature on the OF-8 form that he contended violated 18 U.S.C. § 1001(a)(3)).
- After OSC issued a close-out letter on November 18, 2021, Mr. Coleman filed an IRA appeal with the MSPB on December 3, 2021 under 5 U.S.C. §§ 1221 and 2302(b)(8). Both the MSPB (App. 36a–52a) and the Federal Circuit (App. 1a–8a) held that the Board lacked IRA jurisdiction under *Yunus v. Department of Veterans Affairs*, 242 F.3d 1367, 1371 (Fed. Cir. 2001), because all eleven alleged disclosures postdated the discrete January 24, 2015 HDP discontinuation (and the disclosures relied upon for his reassignment claim postdated the April 24, 2015 PD revision), so none could have been a "contributing factor" in the challenged personnel actions. Both tribunals rejected his "continuing reprisal" theory—that each subsequent paycheck omitting HDP or each subsequent refusal to correct the PD constituted a new, independent "failure to take" a personnel action under 5 U.S.C. §§ 2302(a)(2)(A)(ix) and (b)(8)—relying on *Bosley v. Merit Systems Protection Board*, 162 F.3d 665, 667 (Fed. Cir. 1998), and *Hamley v. Department of the Interior*, 122 M.S.P.R. 290, 294 (2015).

On the Supreme Court docket (`snapshots/2026-10-30.json`), the petition was filed September 29, 2026; the Solicitor General waived the right of respondent Merit Systems Protection Board to respond on October 19, 2026; and the petition was distributed on October 21, 2026 for the Conference of November 6, 2026 (`distribution_count = 1`, `band = baseline`, `salience_version = sal-v4`). ## 3. Analysis of Certiorari Criteria (Rule 10)

### A. Absence of a Circuit Conflict
Petitioner presents three questions (Pet. i–ii): (1) whether a post-disclosure refusal to reinstate a discontinued statutory pay differential constitutes an independent "fail[ure] to take" a personnel action under 5 U.S.C. §§ 2302(a)(2)(A)(ix) and (b)(8) rather than a continuing effect of a prior discrete act under *Bosley*, 162 F.3d at 667, and *National Railroad Passenger Corp. v. Morgan*, 536 U.S. 101 (2002); (2) whether an agency's continued reliance on an allegedly inaccurate or improperly signed position description after protected disclosures is a continuing abuse of authority/failure to take a personnel action; and (3) whether the Federal Circuit's panel decision conflicts with *Chambers v. NASCO, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 32 (1991), regarding fraud on the court.

None of these questions implicates a split among the United States courts of appeals:
1. **Discrete-Act vs. Continuing-Effect Doctrine under the WPEA:** Although the All Circuit Review Act (5 U.S.C. § 7703(b)(1)(B)) permits WPEA petitions for review to be filed in regional circuits as well as the Federal Circuit, the petition identifies no decision from any regional court of appeals holding that an agency's failure to reverse a pre-disclosure pay termination transforms every subsequent paycheck or refusal to reconsider into a fresh retaliatory personnel action under § 2302(b)(8). To the contrary, the rule that a discrete personnel decision occurs when made and that subsequent refusals to undo it or continuing paycheck consequences do not reset the causal sequence is standard across federal employment and retaliation law. See *Delaware State College v. Ricks*, 449 U.S. 250, 258 (1980); *Morgan*, 536 U.S. at 110–15; *Bosley*, 162 F.3d at 667.Accepting Petitioner's theory would effectively abolish the statutory requirement in 5 U.S.C. § 1221(e)(1) that a whistleblowing disclosure be a "contributing factor" in the personnel action: any employee aggrieved by a pre-existing pay or classification decision could complain about its illegality afterward and treat the agency's non-reversal as a new retaliatory failure to act.
2. **Nonprecedential Decision Below:** Both the Federal Circuit's 8-page *per curiam* opinion (App. 1a–8a) and the MSPB's Final Order (App. 36a–52a) are designated nonprecedential, applying settled circuit law (*Yunus* and *Bosley*) to the chronology of Petitioner's 2015–2018 disclosures.
3. **Inapplicability of *Chambers v. NASCO*:** Petitioner's invocation of *Chambers v. NASCO, Inc.*, 501 U.S. 32 (1991), raises a fact-bound dispute over an allegedly duplicated electronic signature in Block 21 of a 2015 position description (which the agency and DoD OIG investigated in 2016 and characterized as administrative "sloppy work"). That issue was neither the basis of the Federal Circuit's jurisdictional holding nor a doctrinal question of inherent judicial sanctioning power.
