## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a federal employee's bid to revive his whistleblower retaliation claim over denied hazardous duty pay.

## What happened

Richard W. Coleman, Jr., a former Pentagon Force Protection Agency supervisor, lost his hazardous duty pay supplement in January 2015 after the agency decided he had been receiving it in error. He made a series of disclosures beginning that February challenging the decision and alleging problems with the paperwork justifying it, including a duplicated signature the agency called "sloppy work." He pursued a whistleblower retaliation claim before the Merit Systems Protection Board, which dismissed it for lack of jurisdiction, finding his disclosures came after the decision to cut his pay and so could not have caused it. The Federal Circuit affirmed, rejecting his argument that the agency's continued refusal to restore his pay after his disclosures was a separate, ongoing violation.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether an agency's repeated refusal, after an employee's whistleblower disclosures, to restore pay or fix a disputed personnel document counts as its own new retaliatory act, separate from the original decision that triggered the dispute, for purposes of letting the employee pursue a retaliation claim.

## Where it stands

The Merit Systems Protection Board waived its right to respond to the petition. The Court considered the petition at its November 13, 2026 conference and denied it on November 16, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Coleman, the petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case after losing before the Board and the Federal Circuit. The Court's denial leaves the Federal Circuit's ruling in place, meaning Coleman's retaliation claim remains dismissed for lack of jurisdiction. A grant would instead have brought the case to full briefing and argument, with the Court deciding the legal question itself rather than leaving the lower court's ruling as the final word.
