## In brief

A former substitute teacher asks the Court to revive her free-exercise and free-speech claims against a school district's gender-identity policies.

## What happened

Kimberly Polk, a part-time substitute teacher for Montgomery County Public Schools, objected on religious grounds to policies requiring staff to use students' preferred pronouns and to withhold information about a student's gender identity from parents without the student's consent. When she would not affirm compliance, the district did not retain her as a substitute. She sued, raising free-exercise and free-speech claims. The district court dismissed those claims, and a divided Fourth Circuit panel affirmed, holding that established precedents permitted the policies. After that ruling, the Supreme Court decided a separate case addressing similar parent-notification policies, and the district later revised its guidelines and reinstated Polk with an accommodation.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a long-standing rule letting neutral, generally applicable policies burden religious exercise without heightened court scrutiny should still apply here, or whether this dispute is instead like other cases where such burdens automatically trigger the toughest level of judicial review. The Court is also asked whether requiring a teacher to use certain pronouns and withhold certain information counts as the school's own speech that it can control, or as the teacher's protected speech.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for November 13, 2026, after earlier being rescheduled; the docket does not yet show a decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Polk, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to reverse that ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether the school district's policies violated her constitutional rights, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision rejecting Polk's claims stands, and the school district's win remains in place. A grant would not itself decide the outcome; it would simply mean the case proceeds to briefing and argument on the merits.
