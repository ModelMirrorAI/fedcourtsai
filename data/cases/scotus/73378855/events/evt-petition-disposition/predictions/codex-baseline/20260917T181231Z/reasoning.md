## Why materially below the anchor

The petition's first question asks whether an unexplained state appellate affirmance can satisfy due process when punitive damages are challenged; the second alleges a denial of meaningful review throughout the proceedings. Petition pp. 3–8 argues conflict with established punitive-damages precedents rather than identifying a developed split over a squarely adopted appellate rule. Its strongest assertion is that no court meaningfully reviewed the award.

The opposition supplies unusually concrete contrary evidence. Its Appendix J is the December 10, 2024 hearing transcript, not merely counsel's characterization. At A-9–A-11, defense counsel presents the constitutional guideposts and the trial judge discusses the conduct and rejects excessiveness. At A-13–A-14, opposing counsel corrects the damages calculation and the judge accepts that the ratio is within the permissible range, expressly invoking BMW. This is not an exhaustive written guidepost analysis, and some of the judge's remarks are deferential to the jury. Nevertheless, it substantially undermines a claim that meaningful consideration never occurred. The distinction between inadequate review and no articulated written opinion makes this a poor vehicle for the broad questions posed.

The damages figures reinforce caution rather than an obvious constitutional emergency. Petition p. 7 and BIO pp. 1–3 describe $800,000 punitive damages, $200,000 additional compensatory damages, and $160,696.46 previously awarded compensatory damages. Using the full compensatory recovery yields approximately 2.22:1; even excluding the earlier award yields 4:1. Neither calculation alone establishes constitutionality, but neither supplies an extreme-ratio signal. The remittitur motion and initial hearing argument instead recite $1.3 million and about $167,000, producing 7.8:1; opposing counsel corrects those figures during the hearing. I do not treat the initial 7.8:1 claim as the operative award. The separate judgment in favor of Mark Wilson is not added to Watts's compensatory denominator.

The BIO also invokes *TXO Production Corp. v. Alliance Resources Corp.*, 509 U.S. 443, 464–65 (1993). I verified the relevant passage through CourtListener's opinion text: the opinion rejects the asserted constitutional deficiency of an unexplained trial-court ruling where counsel received an adequate hearing and the judge indicated agreement with the jury's appraisal. That is persuasive context for this factual posture, not proof that TXO conclusively resolves every possible state appellate written-opinion requirement. The absence of a written explanation is not automatically evidence of absent review. I rely on the provisioned parties' presentations of BMW, State Farm, Cooper Industries, and Oberg rather than claiming an independently exhaustive precedent survey.

These concrete vehicle weaknesses justify reducing the matched 5.12% anchor to 0.6%. The residual covers the possibility that the Court sees a recurring appellate-review problem despite the trial hearing. The docket supplies no positive repeated-distribution, CVSG, or amicus signal counterbalancing the weaknesses. This is a merits-of-cert assessment, not an endorsement of the underlying conduct allegations or a prediction that the Court will affirm the damages award on the merits.
