## In brief

Michael Mendenhall asks the Court to overturn a 1960 ruling and require warrant affidavits to rest on firsthand witness testimony rather than hearsay.

## What happened

Police in Denver arrested Mendenhall after a confrontation outside his home and sought a warrant to search it. A detective wrote the warrant affidavit based on what another officer told him a witness had said, not on any firsthand observation. A judge issued the warrant, officers seized a baseball bat, and charges were later dropped. Mendenhall sued Denver, arguing the city's practice of allowing such secondhand information in warrant applications violates the Fourth Amendment. Lower courts ruled against him, holding they were bound by the Supreme Court's 1960 decision permitting hearsay in warrant applications.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it should abandon its longstanding rule allowing police to rely on secondhand information when swearing out a search warrant, and instead require that someone with direct, personal knowledge of the facts swear to them before a judge issues the warrant.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for September 28, 2026, after the Court asked for and received a response from Denver and a reply from Mendenhall. The docket shows no action yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Mendenhall is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether hearsay can support a warrant, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Tenth Circuit's ruling against Mendenhall stands, and the city's practice of allowing secondhand information in warrant affidavits remains in place, at least in that circuit. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right, only that it leaves that decision undisturbed.
