## What happened

Michael Mendenhall got into a confrontation outside his Denver home with a man who called police. An officer relayed the accuser's account to a detective by phone, and the detective, without speaking to the accuser himself, swore an affidavit based on that secondhand information. A judge issued a warrant, and police searched Mendenhall's home and seized a baseball bat, though charges were later dropped. Mendenhall sued Denver, arguing the warrant violated the Fourth Amendment because it rested on hearsay rather than firsthand knowledge. Lower courts ruled against him, saying they were bound by a 1960 Supreme Court decision, Jones v. United States, which allows warrants based on reliable hearsay.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it should abandon its longstanding rule allowing warrants to rest on secondhand information, and instead require that whoever swears to the facts supporting a warrant must have personally witnessed them. Mendenhall argues the Constitution's oath requirement was originally understood to demand firsthand testimony, and that the existing rule invites unreliable and sometimes false information into warrant applications. Denver responds that the Constitution's text does not require firsthand knowledge, that the current approach reflects how investigations actually work, and that changing the rule would slow down warrant applications without meaningfully improving reliability.

## Where it stands
