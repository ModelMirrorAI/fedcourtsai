## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a Denver man's bid to overturn decades-old precedent letting police use secondhand information to get search warrants.

## What happened

Police responded when a man reported being threatened outside Michael Mendenhall's home. An officer relayed the accuser's account to a detective, who wrote a sworn warrant affidavit based entirely on that secondhand information, without speaking to the accuser himself. A judge issued the warrant, and officers seized a baseball bat from Mendenhall's home; charges against Mendenhall were later dropped. Mendenhall sued Denver, arguing the warrant violated the Fourth Amendment because it relied on hearsay rather than firsthand testimony. The district court and the Tenth Circuit both ruled against him, citing a 1960 Supreme Court decision, Jones v. United States, that allows warrants based on reliable hearsay.

## What the Court is being asked

Mendenhall's petition asks the Court to overturn its own 1960 ruling and hold that the Fourth Amendment's requirement that warrants be backed by a sworn "oath or affirmation" means only someone with firsthand knowledge of the facts can supply the information, not an officer repeating what someone else said. Denver answers that the existing rule is well established, consistent with the Constitution's text, and workable, and that Mendenhall's case is a poor vehicle for revisiting it.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court asked Denver to respond before the second conference. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Mendenhall asked the Court to take up his case and overturn the Tenth Circuit's ruling against him. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Tenth Circuit's decision in place, meaning Jones remains good law and Mendenhall's lawsuit against Denver does not go forward on this claim. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning or with Denver's position; it simply leaves that ruling standing.
