## In brief

The Court declined to hear Michael Mendenhall's bid to overturn a decades-old rule letting police use secondhand information in search warrant applications.

## What happened

Police arrested Mendenhall after a man accused him of a threat during a dispute outside his home. A detective sought a warrant to search Mendenhall's house based on an affidavit relaying what another officer had been told, rather than anything the detective personally observed. A judge issued the warrant, and officers seized a baseball bat; charges were later dropped. Mendenhall sued Denver, arguing the warrant violated the Fourth Amendment because it rested on secondhand information rather than firsthand sworn testimony. Lower courts ruled against him, citing a 1960 Supreme Court decision allowing such evidence in warrant applications.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether the Court should abandon its longstanding rule allowing secondhand accounts to support a warrant, and instead require that only someone with direct, personal knowledge of the facts may swear to them before a judge issues a search warrant.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court requested a response from Denver after the first conference. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Mendenhall, who brought this case, asked the Court to take up his appeal. With the petition denied, the Tenth Circuit's ruling against him stands, meaning Denver's warrant practice—and the underlying rule allowing secondhand information in warrant applications—remains in place. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision as the final word in this case, without the Court deciding the underlying constitutional question.
