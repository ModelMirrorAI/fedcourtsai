## Case-specific adjustment to 9%

The petition seeks a major doctrinal change, not correction of a lower court's departure from governing precedent. Its question expressly asks to overrule Jones v. United States, 362 U.S. 257 (1960). The petition's introduction and legal argument frame the oath requirement as a historically categorical safeguard, analogize to the restoration of categorical guarantees in Crawford and Ramos, and argue that contemporary remote-warrant infrastructure could accommodate firsthand testimony. Those are meaningful reasons for interest, especially with the four distinct supporting amicus groups recorded in the snapshot and the Court's affirmative response request.

Against that, Denver's opposition, pp. 1–5 and 16–17, identifies no competing circuit rule and emphasizes reliance on the existing warrant framework. The petition itself acknowledges that Jones controlled below (petition pp. 20 and 35). I independently checked the historical Jones opinion's hearsay holding at 362 U.S. 270–271 through CourtListener. A request to replace this framework nationwide faces a larger review hurdle than a clean conflict over an unsettled rule. This weighs the probability below the elevated-band pool, without treating stare decisis as an absolute bar to review.

The apparent relist signal also deserves a discount. The response was requested before the first scheduled conference, and the second distribution followed receipt of the requested opposition and reply. Two recorded distributions are not proof of two fully briefed substantive conference examinations. The statpack itself warns that distribution counts can include rescheduling before consideration. I therefore do not treat the summer interval as unexplained evidence of a prolonged hold or repeated substantive interest. The response request remains positive evidence, but does not independently establish four votes for review.

Vehicle quality is contested. The petition, pp. 18–20 and 35–36, describes a preserved policy challenge against Denver, simple double-hearsay facts, and a home search, all favorable to presenting the legal question. Denver argues that municipal culpability independently defeats recovery even if Jones is overruled. I give this only a modest adverse adjustment, not dispositive weight: the historical majority in Board of County Commissioners v. Brown, 520 U.S. 397, 404–407 (1997), distinguishes directly unconstitutional municipal action from facially lawful action that causes a later employee violation. The opposition's formulation of deliberate indifference as intending to violate rights should not simply be accepted as a universal rule. The absence of the reply and underlying complaint text limits my assessment of that dispute.

Taken together, the response request, amici, and focused historical theory preserve a real grant chance, while the absence of a demonstrated split, requested doctrinal overhaul, and qualified distribution signal justify a substantial downward adjustment from 17.24% to 9%.
