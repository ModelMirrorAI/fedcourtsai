## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a supplements trade group's challenge to a New York law restricting sales of weight-loss and muscle-building supplements to minors.

## What happened

New York passed a law banning sales to minors of dietary supplements labeled or marketed for weight loss or muscle building. The Council for Responsible Nutrition, a trade association for supplement makers, sued New York's Attorney General, arguing the law unconstitutionally restricts commercial speech. A federal district court denied the Council's request to block the law while the case proceeded, and the Second Circuit Court of Appeals agreed, finding the law likely survives the constitutional test that applies to commercial speech restrictions. The Council then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks how courts should apply the standard governing restrictions on commercial speech. Specifically, it concerns how much evidence a government must produce to show a speech restriction will actually reduce the harm it targets, and how much weight courts should give to a legislature's chosen approach instead of requiring proof that no less speech-restrictive alternative would work. The Council argued the Second Circuit's approach conflicted with how other appeals courts handle these questions; New York disputed that any such conflict exists.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and on October 5, 2026, the Court denied it.

## What each outcome would mean

The Council for Responsible Nutrition asked the Court to take up the case. With the denial, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, meaning New York's law remains in effect and the Council's request to block its enforcement continues to fail, at least for now in the lower courts. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Second Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed. The underlying lawsuit over the law's constitutionality may still continue in the lower courts.
