## Evidence for and against review

**The petition's strongest point is the connection between the harm and the chosen restriction.** Its first question is not merely whether supplements can be harmful, but whether evidence supports using labeling or marketing as the trigger for restricted sales. Its second question attacks deference to that proxy where an ingredients-based approach might regulate conduct without the same speech burden. The arguments at petition pp. 14–23 and 25–32 identify a potentially recurring distinction between proving a harm and proving that a particular speech restriction alleviates it. The opinion's reliance on reasonable legislative judgment gives that concern a concrete target (Pet. App. 12a–15a).

**The asserted split is materially contested.** The BIO, pp. 19–23, argues that the cited circuits agree on the legal framework and differ because their evidence differs. In particular, it distinguishes Junior Sports Magazines as a record without support connecting the advertisement restriction to unlawful gun use, and points to other circuits' acceptance of studies and anecdotes. These are advocacy claims, not a conflict I independently verified through all the underlying decisions. The reproduced Second Circuit opinion does identify scientific evidence of harm and explains why the marketing category tracks the category used in the literature (Pet. App. 12a–14a). Its tailoring discussion expressly considers, rather than wholly ignores, an ingredients-based alternative and explains the administrative objection to it (15a). Thus the petition's characterization of a wholly evidenceless or wholly unexamined restriction is too strong, even though the sufficiency of those explanations remains debatable.

**The procedural obstacles substantially limit the uplift.** The requested review follows denial of a preliminary injunction in ongoing litigation, not final adjudication after a complete record. The BIO, pp. 24–26, describes further discovery and argues the questions are not outcome determinative. The appendix confirms an alternative public-interest assessment even assuming likely merit in the First Amendment claims (30a). I give that issue real weight rather than merely repeating the BIO. But I do not treat every equities issue as an airtight independent ground: the appellate irreparable-harm analysis partly depends on its merits conclusions (28a–29a), and footnote 9 expressly leaves the delay question unresolved. A grant could still address the legal standard and remand; the vehicle is unattractive, not jurisdictionally impossible.
