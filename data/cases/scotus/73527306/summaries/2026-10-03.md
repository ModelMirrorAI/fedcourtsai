## In brief

A Minneapolis police officer asks the Court to review a ruling that let a man he shot with a less-lethal projectile pursue an excessive-force lawsuit against him.

## What happened

During unrest following George Floyd's death, Officer Benjamin Bauer shot Ethan Marks in the eye with a "less-lethal" projectile launcher, causing severe injuries. Marks had struck another officer, Pobuda, moments earlier, but Pobuda had pushed him back and no longer saw him as a threat when Bauer fired. Marks (later succeeded by his mother, Anne Marks, after his death) sued for excessive force. The district court and the Eighth Circuit twice denied Bauer qualified immunity, a legal protection that shields officials from suit unless they violated clearly established law. The Supreme Court had previously sent the case back once for reconsideration under a newer ruling, but the Eighth Circuit reaffirmed its decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it was already clearly established, at the time of the shooting, that using a less-lethal projectile this way was an unreasonable use of force, considering everything that happened in the encounter rather than focusing narrowly on the instant force was used. Bauer argues the appeals court kept applying an overly narrow focus despite being told to reconsider; Marks's mother answers that the court properly weighed the whole encounter and that disputed facts should go to a jury, not to the Supreme Court.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for October 9, 2026, after full briefing including a reply. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Bauer, the officer, asks the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the force used was clearly unlawful at the time, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies review, the Eighth Circuit's ruling stands, meaning Marks's mother could continue pursuing the lawsuit against Bauer in the lower courts without a trial-ending ruling of immunity. A denial would not mean the Court agrees Bauer is liable—only that the lower court's decision remains in place.
