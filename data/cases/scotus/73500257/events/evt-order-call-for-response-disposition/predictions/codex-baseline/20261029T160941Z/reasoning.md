I move substantially above 5.23%, to **30%**, because several specific facts make this a credible summary-correction candidate:

- The Court affirmatively requested a response after waiver. That removes the case from the completely unattended petition population, although it does not establish four votes for review.
- Eight circuit judges joined a detailed rehearing dissent directed at the application of Supreme Court immunity standards, not merely dissatisfaction with the outcome. It supplies a focused error-correction argument and identifies factual distinctions in the panel's controlling precedent.
- Question 2 permits a narrow immunity ruling without resolving the underlying search's constitutionality or adopting question 3's sweeping limitation on sources of clearly established law. The petition expressly seeks summary reversal as an alternative to plenary review.
- The appendix's discussion of Stanton v. Sims and Rivas-Villegas supplies pertinent examples of the asserted error pattern. I use their treatment in the staged judicial opinions, not an independently retrieved opinion or an assumed later development in this case.

The substantial downward constraint is vehicle and merits uncertainty. The amended panel opinion offers a reasoned distinction between immediately tracking someone to a specific property and searching a neighborhood after losing the trail. It assumes rather than decides that the canine alert established probable cause. It describes at least eighteen minutes before the dog even arrived, leaving the actual entry potentially later; the petition's more compressed chronology should not be accepted as undisputed. The search was of an uninvolved person's enclosed backyard, and police did not find the suspect. The governing procedural posture requires the plaintiffs' favorable factual inferences. These points make a summary reversal less straightforward than the petition and dissent suggest. The cited contrary decisions also do not establish a clean, square published circuit split. The panel affirmed the dog-shooting immunity ruling and the city's municipal-liability victory, so this is not a wholesale rejection of immunity or a live municipal-liability conflict. Finally, numerous fact-intensive immunity petitions can be denied even when the alleged error is plausible.

Thirty percent is therefore a judgmental upward adjustment for the combination of active attention, a substantial rehearing dissent, and a narrow summary route—not a measured conditional frequency or a claim that the call for response alone multiplies the odds sixfold. The unpublished future opposition is the principal missing adversarial input.
