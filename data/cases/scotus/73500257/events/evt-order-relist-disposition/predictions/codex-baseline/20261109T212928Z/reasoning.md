## Why move above the anchor

The strongest combination is a real relist after the requested opposition and reply, an eight-judge dissent from denial of rehearing en banc, and a sharply framed qualified-immunity issue for which summary correction is available. The petition expressly requests that alternative in section V, printed page 30; the reply renews it at pages 12-13. The requested response and relist indicate actual attention, not agreement with the officers.

I read the questions presented, the petition's account and qualified-immunity arguments, the opposition's introduction and sections I-IV, the reply's responses, and the relevant opinions in the appendix. The controlling amended opinion and rehearing order are in `appendix.txt`, App. 1a-17a, followed by Judge Collins's dissent, App. 18a-36a. The dissent's eight named judges are a concrete signal of controversy, not a vote count attributable to the Supreme Court. Its critical distinction is between permanently abandoning a suspect's trail and believing that a police perimeter and trained dog's alert have restored it. That distinction offers a narrow basis to find insufficiently specific notice without holding that the entry was constitutional.

The panel states that even assuming the alert supplied probable cause, it could not salvage the broken pursuit (App. 12a). This helps make the notice issue reviewable without resolving every dispute over the dog's reliability: a reviewing Court could assume probable cause and still ask whether pre-2019 precedent clearly foreclosed treating this police activity as continuous pursuit. Johnson involved a suspect lost in woods, a wait for backup, and no comparable new location information. Whether those distinctions ultimately persuade is uncertain, but they fit the specificity objection the dissent develops.

Two retrieved primary-source precedents make the summary route concrete. In **Rivas-Villegas v. Cortesluna, 595 U.S. 1 (2021)**, the Court summarily reversed the Ninth Circuit after distinguishing the circuit precedent relied on to deny qualified immunity; it assumed, rather than decided, that circuit precedent could clearly establish law (CourtListener opinion 5118993, especially printed pages 5-6). In **Zorn v. Linton, No. 25-297 (March 23, 2026)**, the Court again summarily reversed a denial of qualified immunity for insufficiently specific precedent, again reserving that source-of-law question (CourtListener opinion 11280281, printed pages 4-6 and footnote 3). These are route and institutional-behavior analogies, not factual holdings about hot pursuit. Zorn predates this petition and is cited in the provisioned petition; neither precedent supplies retroactive notice to officers acting in 2019.

The upward adjustment from 25.1% to 58% is therefore a substantive judgment about this particular summary-reversal candidate, not a mechanical estimate or a general claim that any relisted police case is likely to be granted. I would be much less optimistic about ordinary plenary review standing alone.

## Why substantial denial risk remains

The opposition makes a serious vehicle objection, not just a generic plea against error correction. Its introduction and pages 9-14 contend that there was never an immediate chase, that the perimeter did not actually contain the suspect, that the dog did not track this suspect's individual scent, and that the lapse before entry exceeded the advertised eighteen minutes. The appendix's amended opinion, App. 5a n.1, itself leaves the entry time uncertain. The reply, pages 2-3, disputes the longer timeline but does not establish an entry exactly eighteen minutes after flight. I do not treat petitioners' short description as undisputed fact or resolve these evidentiary conflicts against the homeowners at summary judgment.

The opposition also offers Johnson, Welsh, and out-of-circuit authority as fair warning that a neighborhood search is not an immediate and continuous chase (printed pages 26-30). That response could convince the Court that ordinary application of settled law, rather than summary correction, is appropriate. There is no demonstrated square circuit split over materially identical perimeter-and-dog facts. The municipal-liability and dog-shooting seizure claims were resolved for defendants below; the live federal dispute concerns the entry, with consequential damages, not a free-standing holding that officers must allow their dog to be attacked (App. 13a-17a).

The broad third question, whether only Supreme Court decisions can clearly establish law, has a particularly weak vehicle: the opposition says it was never argued or decided below (printed page 31), and the reply invokes discretionary review of unpreserved issues rather than showing preservation (printed page 11). Neither Rivas-Villegas nor Zorn adopted that sweeping rule. I therefore assign little probability to taking this case for a wholesale restriction on the sources of clearly established law.

For coherence, an illustrative decomposition of the 58% grant family is 87% summary reversal, 3% GVR or another cert-order judgment, and 10% plenary review, all conditional on a grant. That yields the declared **0.90 conditional summary-disposition-route probability**, with summary reversal the most likely individual outcome. Denial remains roughly two chances in five, with only a small residual for a procedural disposition. I am not equating the probability of ultimate officer success after a hypothetical grant with the probability that the Court intervenes now.
