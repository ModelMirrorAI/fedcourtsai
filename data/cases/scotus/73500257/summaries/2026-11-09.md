## In brief

North Las Vegas asks the Court to revive qualified immunity for officers sued after a warrantless backyard search and dog shooting during a search for a fleeing suspect.

## What happened

Police searching a neighborhood for a domestic-violence suspect who had fled lost track of him for at least eighteen minutes. A K-9 unit then led an officer, Lieutenant Salkoff, to jump a wall into the walled backyard of Genoa Jones and Cornell Tinsley without a warrant. Their dogs attacked the police dog, and Salkoff shot and killed two of them. Jones and Tinsley sued the city and officers. The district court granted the officers qualified immunity. The Ninth Circuit reversed in part, holding that the search was not justified by "hot pursuit" because the officers had lost the suspect's trail for too long, and that this was already clearly established law; it affirmed on other claims, including the shooting itself. Rehearing en banc was denied over a dissent from eight judges.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether police can still invoke the "chasing a fleeing suspect" exception to the warrant requirement when they temporarily lose a suspect's trail but later believe, based on further police work, that they have picked it back up; whether the officers should be shielded from liability because no prior case clearly established that this kind of search was unlawful; and whether only this Court's own decisions, rather than lower federal appeals court rulings, can ever count as clearly establishing a right for purposes of that shield.

## Where it stands

The case was distributed for conference in September 2026 and, after the Court asked for a response and received full briefing, it has since been distributed for conference again, most recently for November 13, 2026. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will take the case.

## What each outcome would mean

North Las Vegas and its officers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision reviving the lawsuit. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, and Jones and Tinsley's lawsuit over the backyard search continues in the lower courts. If the Court grants review, it will decide the legal questions itself after full briefing and argument; affirming would leave the lawsuit alive, reversing would restore qualified immunity and end that claim, and vacating would send the case back without resolving it for either side.
