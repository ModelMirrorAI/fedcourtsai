## In brief

North Las Vegas and two officers ask the Court to revive qualified immunity after the Ninth Circuit let a warrantless-search lawsuit over two dogs' deaths proceed.

## What happened

Searching a neighborhood for a fleeing domestic-violence suspect, a North Las Vegas police lieutenant entered the walled backyard of Genoa Jones and Cornell Tinsley without a warrant, and shot and killed their two dogs after the dogs confronted his police dog. Jones and Tinsley sued the city and officers. A federal district court granted the officers qualified immunity, a legal protection that shields officials from being sued unless they violated clearly established law. The Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed in part, holding that entering the yard violated clearly established Fourth Amendment law, though it let stand the ruling favoring the officers on the dog-shooting itself. The full Ninth Circuit declined to rehear the case, over dissent from eight judges.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether officers searching for a fled suspect can still claim they were in "hot pursuit" — an exception letting police search a home without a warrant — when they temporarily lost track of the suspect but later developed new leads. It is also asked whether the officers' conduct violated law that was clearly established enough to deny them immunity, and more broadly whether only this Court's own rulings, rather than lower-court decisions, should ever count as "clearly established law" for immunity purposes.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court then asked the respondents to respond; after an extension, that response was filed October 5, 2026. The case has not yet been scheduled for a conference to decide whether to grant review.

## What each outcome would mean

North Las Vegas and the officers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to hear the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision reviving Jones and Tinsley's lawsuit over the yard search stands, and the case would continue in the lower courts, though a denial would not mean the Court agrees the Ninth Circuit was right.
