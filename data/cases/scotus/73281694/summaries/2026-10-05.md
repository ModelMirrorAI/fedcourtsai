## In brief

The Court declined to hear Norfolk Southern's bid to revisit Pennsylvania courts' jurisdiction over it under the Commerce Clause.

## What happened

Robert Mallory sued Norfolk Southern Railway in Pennsylvania state court over a workplace injury claim, relying on a Pennsylvania law treating a company's registration to do business there as consent to be sued there on any claim. The Supreme Court previously upheld that law against a due-process challenge but left open whether it violates the Commerce Clause. On remand, the trial court found Norfolk Southern had waived that argument by raising it too late, and denied its objections. Pennsylvania's appellate courts declined to review that ruling. Norfolk Southern then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to take up the Commerce Clause question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether a state can treat a company's registration to do business there as enough, by itself, to let its courts hear any lawsuit against that company, even one with no connection to the state, without that arrangement improperly burdening interstate commerce. Mallory's estate countered that no court had actually ruled on this question, because the trial court had found the argument waived under state procedural rules rather than deciding it.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after briefing that included an opposition and a reply, and the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Norfolk Southern, the company facing suit, asked the Court to take up the case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves in place the Pennsylvania trial court's ruling that Norfolk Southern waived its Commerce Clause argument by raising it too late, meaning that argument was never decided on its merits in this case. Mallory's estate's position, that the courts could proceed without resolving the constitutional question, stands. The denial does not decide whether the Commerce Clause limits this kind of jurisdiction law.
