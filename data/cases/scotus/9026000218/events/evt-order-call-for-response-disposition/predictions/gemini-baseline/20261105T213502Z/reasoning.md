## 1. Procedural Posture and Record Overview

In 1992, Mark Copeland was shot thirteen times in broad daylight on the sidewalk in front of 2615 Valence Street in New Orleans (App. 57a, 71a). Petitioner Juan Matthews (known by the nickname "Beaver") was convicted of second-degree murder in Orleans Parish Criminal District Court and sentenced to life imprisonment (Pet. 6, 8; App. 13a). At trial, the State presented no physical or forensic evidence linking Matthews to the scene (latent fingerprints lifted from paper bags near the body did not match Matthews, and the murder weapon—a .45-caliber automatic handgun—was never recovered, App. 61a–62a, 66a, 92a). Instead, the State relied on:
1. **Three eyewitness identifications:**
   - **Lloyd Martin**, the victim's best friend, who had known Matthews ("Beaver") from the neighborhood since age 13, testified that he saw Matthews walk up and shoot Copeland in the chest and then stand over him shooting him in the head with a chrome .45 automatic (App. 81a–82a). Although Martin later entered into a plea agreement in an unrelated heroin case (disclosed to and cross-examined before the jury, App. 5a), Martin gave his formal statement identifying Matthews on April 1, 1992—the day after the murder and prior to his unrelated drug charge (App. 5a, 80a–88a).
   - **Dominique Gibson**, a 12-year-old boy who had just handed Copeland a bag of chips and a Hawaiian Punch moments before the shooting (App. 81a), identified Matthews at the scene and at trial as the shooter and testified that Matthews frequented the area (App. 5a, 11a, 15a).
   - **Vanessa Henry**, a 10-year-old girl, identified Matthews as the shooter and testified that she witnessed the shooting from across the street outside her residence at 2608 Valence Street (Pet. 7; App. 5a, 176a–178a).
2. **Matthews's own statements and rebutted alibi:** The night of the murder, Matthews voluntarily met with Detective Herman Cade, denied shooting Copeland, "but stated that he (Matthews) and Copeland had been arguing earlier in the day" (App. 75a). At trial, Matthews presented an alibi defense that was inconsistent with his contemporaneous account to police and was rebutted by the State (App. 10a–11a, 15a).

Decades later, after obtaining the New Orleans Police Department homicide file and the District Attorney's file, Matthews filed an application for state post-conviction relief raising a claim under *Brady v. Maryland*, 373 U.S. 83 (1963), based on three categories of undisclosed evidence from Detective Michael Fejka's March 31, 1992 neighborhood canvass notes (App. 98a–102a) and Detective Joe Janssen's April 1, 1992 photographic lineup report (App. 79a, 105a–106a):
1. **Natha Lee Rubin's photographic lineup non-identification:** Rubin was sitting in her car across the street at 2614 Valence Street when she saw a "stout young black male" (about 5'11" to 6', 220 lbs., wearing a multi-colored sweatshirt and black jeans) walk onto the sidewalk; a blue van blocked her view of the shooting itself, and after hearing shots she saw the same man walk calmly back toward Robertson Street (App. 77a–78a, 103a–104a). Rubin was listed on the State's witness list, and her initial description on the scene was in the incident report, but the State did not disclose her April 1, 1992 statement and the fact that she was shown a six-person photographic lineup containing Matthews's photo and was unable to make an identification—though the police report noted that "Miss Rubin stated she did not closely observe the face of the subject she saw walking away from the scene" (App. 4a, 79a, 105a–106a).
2. **Leroy Milton's canvass statement:** Detective Fejka's field notes recorded that Leroy Milton, who lived across the street at 2612 Valence Street, heard gunshots from his front yard, looked across the street, and saw a group of men running toward Claiborne Avenue and a lone Black male running toward South Robertson Street whom Milton described as "about 40 years, stock build, dark complexion wearing a baseball cap," adding that "[h]e didn't see the mans face" (App. 101a–102a). (Matthews was 20 years old at the time of the shooting, Pet. 9.)
3. **Canvass notes for 2608 Valence Street regarding Vanessa Henry:** Detective Fejka's field notes for 2608 Valence Street—the address where 10-year-old Vanessa Henry testified she lived and stood outside during the shooting—listed interviews at 7:10 p.m. and 7:14 p.m. with two adult residents, Elmedia Isadore and Herbert Victor, both of whom stated they were inside the house when they heard shots, with no mention of Vanessa Henry (App. 100a–101a).

On February 25, 2025, the Orleans Parish Criminal District Court denied post-conviction relief after an evidentiary hearing (App. 13a–16a). On August 27, 2025, a divided panel of the Louisiana Fourth Circuit Court of Appeal (Judges Lobrano and Dysart) granted Matthews's supervisory writ application to review the record but denied relief on the merits (App. 3a–7a). Judge Jenkins dissented, reasoning that the district court had failed to articulate the *Bagley*/*Kyles* "undermines confidence in the outcome" standard and had instead "reduced its materiality analysis to a sufficiency of evidence test," and stating that she would remand for the district court to amend its judgment to include findings under the proper standard (App. 7a–12a). On March 18, 2026, the Supreme Court of Louisiana denied Matthews's writ application in a one-word order ("Writ application denied"), with Justice Griffin noting that she "would grant and docket" (App. 1a–2a).

Matthews filed a paid petition for a writ of certiorari on August 17, 2026, represented by Hogan Lovells Cadwalader US LLP (Jo-Ann Tamila Sagar, Counsel of Record) and the Southern Center for Human Rights. On August 27, 2026, Louisiana waived its right to respond. The petition was distributed on September 16, 2026 for the September 28, 2026 Long Conference (`distribution_count: 1`). Four amicus briefs supporting Petitioner were filed on September 21, 2026—by the Innocence Network, the Cato Institute, the Louisiana Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers, and Former State and Federal Prosecutors (`amicus_briefs: 4` in `record/context.json`)—and on September 29, 2026, the Court called for a response (due October 29, 2026).

## 3. Analysis of Case-Specific Signals

### A. Upward Adjustments (Why the Probability Exceeds ~1%–2%)
1. **Call for Response Following Four Amicus Briefs and Specialist Counsel:** At least one Justice requested a response from Louisiana after the filing of four coordinated cert-stage amicus briefs (Innocence Network, Cato Institute, LACDL, and Former State and Federal Prosecutors) and a petition by experienced Supreme Court litigators at Hogan Lovells and the Southern Center for Human Rights.
2. **Direct State Post-Conviction Posture and Lower-Court Dissent:** Because this petition arises directly from state post-conviction review rather than federal habeas under 28 U.S.C. § 2254(d), the Court's review is not constrained by AEDPA deference. Moreover, both Judge Jenkins on the Louisiana Fourth Circuit (who would have remanded for a proper *Kyles* materiality analysis) and Justice Griffin on the Louisiana Supreme Court (who voted to grant and docket) dissented below, and Orleans Parish has a well-documented history of *Brady* reversals in the Supreme Court (*Kyles v. Whitley*, 514 U.S. 419 (1995); *Smith v. Cain*, 565 U.S. 73 (2012); *Wearry v. Cain*, 577 U.S. 385 (2016)).

### B. Downward Adjustments (Why Certiorari and Summary Reversal Are Unlikely)
1. **Purely Factbound Error-Correction Framing:** The petition does not identify a doctrinal circuit split over a question of federal law; instead, it argues that Louisiana courts failed to faithfully apply settled *Brady*/*Kyles* materiality standards to the facts of Matthews's case. Indeed, the petition candidly states: *"The issue presented here is not whether this Court should expand Brady. Nor is it whether this Court should revisit its precedents. The question instead is whether Louisiana courts must follow them"* (Pet. 34), and overtly pitches the case for summary reversal (Pet. 34–36).
2. **The Operative Appellate Opinion Explicitly Cited and Applied *Kyles*, *Bagley*, and *Smith v. Cain*:** While the Orleans Parish Criminal District Court's brief written ruling did not recite the *Kyles*/*Bagley* "undermines confidence in the verdict" formulation verbatim, the Louisiana Fourth Circuit Court of Appeal granted a supervisory writ, reviewed the record, and issued a reasoned majority opinion that **did** expressly cite and apply *Kyles*, *Bagley*, and *Smith v. Cain* (App. 4a–7a). The Fourth Circuit majority stated:
   - That the record showed the district court "weighed the cumulative effect of the suppressed evidence and found it insufficient to undermine confidence in the verdict" (App. 4a);
   - That under *Smith v. Cain*, 565 U.S. at 76, "impeachment evidence may not be material where other evidence of guilt is sufficiently strong" (App. 4a–5a); and
   - That "[u]ltimately, this case does not present a close call under *Kyles* and *Bagley*. The alleged *Brady* evidence is neither singularly compelling nor collectively material when assessed alongside the trial record, which includes multiple consistent identifications of Defendant by witnesses, two of whom were familiar with him" (App. 6a).
3. **Strong Headwinds from *Klein v. Martin*, 607 U.S. ___ (Jan. 26, 2026) (per curiam):** Just nine months before this event, in *Klein v. Martin*, No. 25-51 (`scotus/72187113`), the Supreme Court summarily **reversed** the Fourth Circuit (with only Justice Jackson dissenting) where the Fourth Circuit had accused a state appellate court of applying a forbidden sufficiency-of-the-evidence test despite the state court's invocation of *Kyles* and *Bagley*. In *Klein*, the Court specifically reiterated that under *Strickler v. Greene*, 527 U.S. 263, 290, 292–294 (1999), "even if undisclosed evidence 'entirely discredit[s]' a prosecution witness, the failure to turn over the evidence is not material if 'considerable' other evidence 'link[s]' the defendant to the crime and the record provides 'strong support' that the defendant would have been convicted anyway." Although *Klein* arose under AEDPA, its core holding—that a state appellate court does not commit *Kyles* sufficiency-of-the-evidence error merely by concluding that strong independent evidence of guilt prevents undisclosed impeachment material from undermining confidence in the verdict—directly undercuts Petitioner's characterization of the Louisiana Fourth Circuit's opinion here.
4. **Recent Denials in Stronger Louisiana *Brady* Petitions (*Skinner* and *Brown*):** Corpus priors confirm that the current Court has repeatedly declined to grant certiorari or summarily reverse in Louisiana *Brady* cases raising this exact argument:
   - In *James Skinner v. Louisiana*, No. 25-1 (`scotus/73110505`), a Louisiana prisoner supported by the **same core amicus coalition** (Innocence Network, Cato Institute, Two Louisiana Prisoners, and law professors—cited in Matthews's own petition at Pet. 33–34) and opposed by the same Louisiana counsel of record argued that Louisiana courts systematically apply a sufficiency-of-the-evidence test and refuse to enforce *Brady*. Despite being relisted and distributed **9 times**, *Skinner* was **denied** on March 30, 2026.
   - In *David Brown v. Louisiana*, No. 22-77 (`scotus/67123792`), 143 S. Ct. 886 (2023), a **capital** prisoner (represented by Jeffrey L. Fisher) sought review where the State had suppressed a co-defendant's confession that two other men—not Brown—committed the killing, the state trial court had granted a new penalty phase, and the Louisiana Supreme Court reversed by emphasizing the "overwhelming" and "copious" trial evidence. Even on those far starker facts, after **7 distributions**, the Court **denied** certiorari on April 3, 2023, over a dissent by Justice Jackson joined by Justices Sotomayor and Kagan.
5. **Substantive Weakness of the Withheld Items Relative to *Wearry* and *Smith*:** Once Louisiana files its brief in opposition, it will highlight several record facts that make this a poor candidate for summary reversal or plenary review:
   - Neither Natha Rubin nor Leroy Milton saw the shooting (Rubin's view was blocked by a van; Milton looked over only after hearing shots), and **both** contemporaneously told police they did not get a good look at the face of the person they saw leaving the area (App. 4a, 79a, 102a). Moreover, Rubin and Milton gave conflicting descriptions of the person they saw (a 19–20-year-old heavyset man walking calmly vs. a 40-year-old man in a baseball cap running), and Rubin's 5'11", 220–240 lb., 19–20-year-old description actually resembled Matthews's general physical profile (20 years old, 5'11", heavy build, App. 69a, 74a, 77a).
   - As for Vanessa Henry, the fact that Detective Fejka's canvass notes from 7:10–7:14 p.m. listed two adults at 2608 Valence Street without mentioning a 10-year-old child is peripheral impeachment at best, and two other eyewitnesses who knew Matthews beforehand (Lloyd Martin and Dominique Gibson) independently identified him as the shooter, alongside Matthews's own admission to Detective Cade that he had argued with the victim earlier that day.
