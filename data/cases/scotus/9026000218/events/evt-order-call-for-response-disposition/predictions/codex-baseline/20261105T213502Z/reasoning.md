## Why the appendix matters

The petition's strongest argument is that Louisiana preserved a life sentence despite suppressed police material, in an eyewitness case without physical evidence connecting Matthews to the crime. It identifies an alternative description, a nonidentification, and notes potentially undermining the vantage point of a child eyewitness. It argues that the courts substituted evidence sufficiency for cumulative materiality. App. 7a–12a records Judge Jenkins's dissent seeking a remand for explicit findings under Brady, Bagley, and Kyles; App. 1a–2a records Justice Griffin's willingness to grant and docket. The direct route from state postconviction proceedings avoids the separate federal habeas deference question, and the decisions address the federal issue rather than imposing an express state procedural bar. Those features make intervention conceivable.

But App. 4a–7a is materially less stark than the petition's presentation. The appellate majority explicitly refers to cumulative effect and confidence in the verdict, distinguishes the proposed suppressed information from stronger impeachment in other cases, and relies on multiple identifications, including familiarity with Matthews. It says the jury already heard about Martin's plea deal and the conflicting shooting time. App. 13a–15a also shows that the trial judge called the issue materiality, addressed the nonidentifying witnesses' failure to see the shooting, and explained why a discrepancy in residence did not necessarily negate presence. That reasoning may be contestable or insufficiently cumulative, but it is not a facial announcement that sufficient inculpatory evidence always defeats Brady.

The dissent below sought better findings while leaving the conviction intact pending remand; it did not itself hold that Matthews was entitled to a new trial. The petition's other-court comparisons concern application of an accepted standard to different facts, not a clean disagreement over the governing rule. Thus the Court can treat this as a request for record-intensive error correction rather than a vehicle for resolving a legal split. The missing opposition is a meaningful uncertainty, not permission to assume the State concedes the petition's framing.

As a historical comparator, I verified **Wearry v. Cain, 577 U.S. 385 (2016)** through CourtListener, opinion 3183080. The opinion's opening and disposition establish a summary reversal from Louisiana state postconviction proceedings over suppressed material evidence; the separate writing identifies Justice Alito's dissent, joined by Justice Thomas. This confirms that the petition's proposed route is real, but a selected successful comparator is not a frequency estimate. Its sharply damaging impeachment is not interchangeable with the disputed inference about a child's residence here. I do not extrapolate its vote alignment mechanically to the present bench.

## Other probabilities and stakes

`relist-increment=0.99` predicts an additional distribution beyond the one already on the docket, normally once the requested response arrives or its time expires. It is not a 99% probability of a true post-conference relist; this moment masks the claim. My modal path is that administrative redistribution followed by denial without a true relist; if the petition attracts more attention, one or two genuine relists are a plausible alternative. `cvsg-increment=0.005` reflects the absence of a distinctive federal-government interest.

`summary-disposition-route=0.75` is conditional on some grant, not an unconditional 75% prediction. Illustratively, the 16% grant probability can be divided into 9.5 points of summary reversal, 2.5 of another cert-order corrective remand, and 4 of plenary review. No identified intervening decision makes a routine hold-and-GVR the principal route. The established-rule character and the petition's express request for summary reversal make cert-order relief more plausible than plenary review if the Court intervenes.

`dissent-from-denial=0.22` is conditional on denial and includes a statement respecting denial. Silence remains the modal outcome. The independent, unconditional order-writer probabilities give the greatest individual chances to Sotomayor and Jackson, but distinguish authorship from merely joining a writing and leave room for writing on a rare grant. They are not predictions of disclosed cert votes.

The **0.48 stakes score** reflects a life-without-parole sentence, alleged long-concealed evidence, and potential consequences for enforcement of disclosure obligations beyond this defendant. It is not a grant-likelihood score. Application of established doctrine to a noncapital individual record makes it less broadly consequential than a case changing the governing constitutional test.
