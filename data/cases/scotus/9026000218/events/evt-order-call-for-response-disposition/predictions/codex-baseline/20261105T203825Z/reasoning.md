## Petition versus the decisions below

The single question asks whether Louisiana rejected Matthews's Brady claim using evidentiary sufficiency instead of materiality. The petition presents a potentially serious example of recurring misapplication of settled disclosure law: withheld nonidentification evidence, an alternative description, and possible impeachment of a child eyewitness in an eyewitness-dependent murder case. The appellate dissent and one state supreme court vote to grant review support taking the complaint seriously. Sources: questions-presented.txt; petition.txt, introduction and pp. 9–14, 26–30, 34–36; appendix.txt, App. 1a–16a.

The appendix nevertheless substantially qualifies the petition's framing. The appellate majority expressly invokes cumulative effect, confidence in the verdict, and Kyles/Bagley, and finds the evidence neither individually nor collectively material. It distinguishes a witness who failed to identify anyone after a poor view from an eyewitness recanting an identification. It also explains that three witnesses identified Matthews, that some impeachment was already before the jury, and that uncertainty about a child's residence does not establish she was absent. Thus, this is not a clean refusal to acknowledge the governing standard. The trial ruling's segmented reasoning and emphasis on guilt remain contestable, but the appellate court supplies a materiality rationale. Judge Jenkins would require additional findings while leaving the conviction intact, not immediately order acquittal or a new trial. Sources: appendix.txt, App. 4a–12a, 14a–16a.

These features pull the forecast below what the call, amici, and petition's characterization alone might suggest. The alleged disagreement with other jurisdictions looks predominantly like different applications of an accepted rule, rather than a developed disagreement about the rule itself. There is no provisioned same-question match or linked lead case supplying a hold/GVR rationale. The direct state postconviction posture avoids a federal habeas statutory-deference layer, but does not remove the fact-bound nature of materiality. I assess jurisdiction and vehicle quality from the petition and appendix, not from an assumed future opposition.

Wearry v. Cain, 577 U.S. 385 (2016), independently retrieved through CourtListener (opinion 3183080), demonstrates an available summary-reversal route for Louisiana postconviction Brady errors and stresses cumulative review. Its much stronger attacks on the prosecution's core witnesses limit the analogy. It makes a grant plausible, not probable. I settle at 14%: meaningfully above 5.15%, but still a clear denial forecast.
