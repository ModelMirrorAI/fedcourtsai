## Why this is more than routine, but still likely denied

The question asks whether Louisiana courts improperly rejected Matthews's Brady claim by testing evidentiary sufficiency. The petition, printed pages 9–12 and 27–30, identifies withheld information about a nonidentifying photographic-lineup witness, a different witness's description of a person fleeing, and police notes allegedly weakening a child eyewitness's claimed vantage point. The trial judgment acknowledges nondisclosure. An eyewitness-dependent conviction without forensic evidence makes cumulative impeachment potentially important. Judge Jenkins's dissent would require proper findings under Brady, Bagley, and Kyles. State postconviction review avoids the added federal habeas deference hurdle; the petition also represents that no independent state procedural bar prevents review (petition, printed pages 29–30).

But the appendix materially qualifies the petition's framing. At **App. 4a–7a**, the appellate majority expressly discusses cumulative effect and confidence in the verdict, cites Kyles and Bagley, and distinguishes Smith and Wearry. It treats the withheld witnesses as people who did not see the shooting, and the address evidence as insufficient to establish that the child could not have witnessed it. It relies on other identifications and impeachment the jury already heard. At **App. 14a–16a**, the trial court's itemized treatment is vulnerable, but its final discussion does invoke materiality in light of the evidence. Thus a reviewing Justice could reasonably read the case as a dispute over the significance of modestly exculpatory information rather than adoption of an openly forbidden legal rule. The appellate court's explicit materiality analysis also makes correcting the trial court's missing formulation less consequential.

The petition alleges recurring Louisiana departures and contrary results elsewhere, but it does not establish a clean division over the verbal legal rule: the dispute concerns whether the courts actually honored an agreed rule. This is a better candidate for exceptional error correction than for a new plenary doctrinal holding. The appellate dissent seeks a limited remand, not an immediate new trial, which further tempers the claim that the record unmistakably compels reversal.

For historical context I verified **Wearry v. Cain, 577 U.S. 385 (2016)** through CourtListener, opinion 3183080, opening text. That opinion reverses a Louisiana state postconviction judgment for suppressed material evidence, establishing that this posture can support intervention. It does not establish that Matthews's weaker and differently situated suppressed evidence must produce the same result. The corpus citation lookup for Wearry returned no rows with an express citation-coverage warning; three historical GVR rows returned by a broader structured query were procedurally illustrative, not Brady analogues, and did not move the forecast.
