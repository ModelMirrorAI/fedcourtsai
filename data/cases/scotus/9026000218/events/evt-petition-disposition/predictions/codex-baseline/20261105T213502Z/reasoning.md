## Why denial still leads

The question is whether the Louisiana courts improperly substituted sufficiency of the trial evidence for Brady materiality. Matthews argues that withheld police notes supplied alternative descriptions and a failed identification, and undermined a child eyewitness's claimed vantage point. In a case dependent on eyewitnesses rather than physical evidence, such evidence can matter even when enough trial evidence remains to support conviction. Appendix B's dissent gives the complaint institutional credibility, but it would have remanded for adequate findings rather than itself ordered a new trial. Appendix A records another judge's willingness to hear the case; neither disagreement establishes entitlement to Supreme Court review.

The strongest counterweight is Appendix B's majority, pages 4a-7a. It expressly invokes cumulative consideration and confidence in the verdict, distinguishes the withheld material from the stronger impeachment evidence in other cases, and explains that the nonidentifying witnesses did not see the shooting or adequately see the person's face. It also reasons that nonresidence does not preclude presence, that other witnesses identified Matthews, and that some credibility problems were already exposed at trial. Appendix C, pages 13a-16a, acknowledges nondisclosure but disputes the material significance of the descriptions and residency information. Thus nondisclosure should not be conflated with an adjudicated concession of every element of a Brady violation.

The petitioner may be right that these explanations conceal an improperly fragmented or sufficiency-driven assessment. But the appellate court did not openly reject the governing constitutional rule. The dispute therefore remains heavily factual: the credibility and corroboration of several witnesses and how the withheld notes would have changed the defense. The petition's comparisons with other jurisdictions show allegedly inconsistent applications on similar facts, not a clearly established disagreement over the legal rule. That makes denial substantially likelier than plenary review or summary reversal, particularly without the State's forthcoming answer to the petition's characterization of the record.

For general context I retrieved *Wearry v. Cain*, 577 U.S. 385 (2016), through CourtListener: search and the opening two text chunks of opinion 3183080. Its opening states that the Court reversed the Louisiana postconviction judgment for withholding material evidence; the retrieved analysis confirms that materiality concerns confidence in the verdict, not whether acquittal is more likely than not. It establishes an available correction route, not a matched probability estimate or a conclusion that Matthews has the same evidentiary showing. Corpus lookup by Wearry's citation returned no rows and expressly warned of sparse citation coverage. Other retrieval attempts and their results are recorded in `retrieval.md`.
