## In brief

Randy and Evgenia Quaid ask the Court to revive privacy claims that lower courts dismissed as barred by California's litigation privilege.

## What happened

The Quaids sued several people and entities over a long-running property dispute in Santa Barbara, California, alleging that a sheriff's employee accessed their criminal history and arrest records and that the records were passed along and eventually used in related state-court litigation. The district court dismissed their privacy-related claims against the private parties and lawyers involved, ruling that California's litigation privilege—which shields communications made in court proceedings—barred the claims, and refused to let the Quaids amend their complaint. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. It also affirmed dismissal of other claims, including one against the county for failing to meet a government-claims filing requirement, and upheld an order declaring the Quaids vexatious litigants in connection with separate claims against another defendant.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a court may treat a legal shield for courtroom communications as covering not just the filing of records in court, but also the earlier, separate act of obtaining and routing those records in the first place, when deciding that no amended complaint could ever get around that shield. The Quaids argue courts must separately examine whether the initial acquisition of the records was itself wrongful before applying the privilege so broadly.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed: one group of respondents filed a brief opposing review, the Quaids filed a reply, and the case was distributed for the justices' conference on November 6, 2026. The docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The Quaids are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision, which upheld dismissal of their claims. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the privilege question itself, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, leaving the dismissal of the Quaids' claims against Fidelity, Hepworth, and other private respondents in place, with no further amendment allowed.
