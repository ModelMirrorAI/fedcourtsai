## In brief

Randy and Evgenia Quaid ask the Court to review a Ninth Circuit ruling that their privacy claims over leaked criminal records were barred by California's litigation privilege.

## What happened

The Quaids sued several parties connected to a California property dispute, alleging that a sheriff's employee accessed their criminal history records and passed them along a chain of officials and private attorneys, who used the records in related state court litigation. A federal district court dismissed their claims, ruling California's litigation privilege shields communications made in judicial proceedings, and found amendment would be futile. The court also dismissed other claims on separate grounds and declared the Quaids vexatious litigants. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding the privacy claims all stemmed from obtaining and disclosing the records in a judicial proceeding.

## What the Court is being asked

The Quaids argue courts below wrongly treated the later courtroom use of their records as covering the earlier, separate act of accessing and passing along the records in the first place. They say California law distinguishes unlawful evidence-gathering from protected litigation communications, and the lower courts should have let them amend their complaint to target the earlier conduct. The respondents counter that the ruling simply applied settled state privilege law to the specific facts pleaded, raising no issue suited to further review.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed: respondents filed briefs opposing review, and the Quaids filed a reply. The docket does not show the petition has been distributed for a conference or acted on yet.

## What each outcome would mean

The Quaids are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants the petition, it would agree to hear the case and decide the privilege question itself, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's ruling affirming dismissal of the Quaids' claims stays in place, and the case ends there. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower courts were right, only that their decision stands.
