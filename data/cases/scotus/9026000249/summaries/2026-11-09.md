## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a couple's bid to revive privacy claims over criminal-record disclosures made during a property dispute.

## What happened

Randy and Evgenia Quaid sued several people and entities connected to a long-running dispute over Santa Barbara property, after a sheriff's employee allegedly accessed their criminal history records and the records were passed along to a prosecutor and then to private lawyers involved in related state litigation. A federal district court dismissed their claims, mainly on the ground that California's litigation privilege shields communications made in connection with judicial proceedings, and it also dismissed other claims on separate grounds, declared the Quaids vexatious litigants, and awarded costs. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that the claims "all stem" from obtaining and disclosing the records in court and that amendment would be futile.

## What the Court is being asked

The Quaids ask the Court to decide whether a court may treat a legal privilege that shields litigation-related communications as also covering the earlier, separate act of improperly obtaining and routing restricted law-enforcement records, simply because those records were later used in a court filing, without separately examining whether that earlier conduct was unlawful on its own.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference, and on November 9, 2026 the Court denied it.

## What each outcome would mean

The Quaids, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up and overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Ninth Circuit's decision in place: the dismissal of the Quaids' privacy-related claims against the private parties and law firm respondents stands, and they may not amend their complaint to pursue those claims further. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed.
