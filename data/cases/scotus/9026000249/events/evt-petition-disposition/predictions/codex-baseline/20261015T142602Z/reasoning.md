I reduce that approximately 5% anchor to **0.5% for any grant**, including GVR and summary reversal. The reduction is a judgment about this petition, not a fitted numerical model:

- **The requested correction is predominantly state-law and case-specific.** The question presented concerns application of California's litigation privilege to antecedent acquisition of restricted records. The petition invokes federal pleading and amendment rules but identifies no competing federal appellate rule or unsettled federal statutory issue. The opposition's Rule 10 discussion, pp. 5–7, is persuasive as a cert-selection argument without requiring acceptance of its view of the underlying merits.
- **The decision has limited precedential reach.** The provisioned Ninth Circuit memorandum expressly identifies itself as unpublished and nonprecedential except under its cited circuit rule (App. 1). It applies the privilege and futility doctrines to these allegations rather than announcing a broad new federal rule (App. 3–4). The petition's claimed conflict with California decisions is not a demonstrated inter-circuit conflict on an important federal question.
- **The vehicle cannot cleanly test the broadest privacy concern.** Petition p. 4 excludes the Government Claims Act ruling concerning the County defendants and the service dismissal of the employee alleged to have accessed the database. The appendix confirms those separate procedural grounds. The remaining dispute concerns private litigants and counsel, with a contested distinction between procuring records through communications and independently accessing the database. Fidelity's opposition, pp. 9–12, presses that distinction. The separate dismissals do not automatically defeat claims against every private defendant, but they complicate a vehicle aimed at unauthorized government-database access.
- **The petition has a genuine, but limited, doctrinal point.** Petition pp. 5–12 distinguishes wrongful evidence acquisition from subsequent use in litigation and asks for amendment rather than an immediate liability ruling. Kimmel v. Goland, 51 Cal. 3d 202, 205, 211 (1990), retrieved through CourtListener, supports the existence of a communicative/noncommunicative distinction while expressly limiting its holding to illegal recording. That makes the petition more substantial than a bare complaint about an adverse result. However, the district court directly addressed and distinguished Kimmel (App. 33), so the record does not support treating the issue as wholly ignored below. Disagreement with that distinction remains much stronger as an argument for case-specific correction than as a reason for Supreme Court review.

The privacy importance and nonfrivolous amendment argument keep the grant probability above zero. They do not overcome the absence of an identified federal conflict, unpublished disposition, and vehicle limitations. I have not inferred a low probability merely from pro se status, the petitioners' identities, or the existence of unreviewed vexatious-litigant findings.
