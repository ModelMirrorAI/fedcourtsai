## In brief

A victim's family asks the Court to review a ruling that the FBI lacked constitutional authority to seize a second gun and clothing during its investigation of a fatal shooting.

## What happened

Todd Murray, a member of the Ute Indian Tribe, died from a gunshot in 2007 on the Uintah and Ouray Reservation during an encounter with an off-duty local police officer. His family sued the federal government, claiming the officer, not Murray, fired the fatal shot, and seeking damages under an 1868 treaty. After years of litigation over whether the FBI improperly failed to preserve evidence, the Court of Federal Claims ruled that the FBI could not have seized the officer's gun and clothing because, once a local police chief took possession of the gun, the Fourth Amendment barred federal agents from taking it without probable cause against that officer. The Federal Circuit affirmed, and later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether investigators at a homicide scene may take possession of significant physical evidence generally, or whether they may seize only evidence connected to a specific suspect they already have reason to believe committed a crime, and whether someone who takes an item of evidence into their own hands at a crime scene thereby gains a privacy interest that blocks investigators from taking it.

## Where it stands

The government has waived its right to respond to the petition. The petition was distributed for the Court's December 11, 2026 conference, and the docket shows no action on it since.

## What each outcome would mean

The Murray family, as petitioners, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Federal Circuit's ruling, which went in the government's favor on this issue. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the evidence-seizure question itself, without deciding in advance who should win. If the Court denies review, the Federal Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the ruling that the FBI lacked authority to seize the gun and clothing, a result that favored the government's position in this dispute.
