## 2. Case-Specific Adjustments Driving `P(grant) = 0.008`
Starting from the **5.18%** `baseline` reached anchor and **2.69%** zero-amicus reference rate, multiple severe procedural and substantive defects push the grant probability sharply downward to **0.8% (`0.008`)**:

1. **Solicitor General Waiver at First Distribution (`2026-11-25.json`):** Respondent United States filed a waiver of its right to respond on November 13, 2026, and the petition was distributed on November 24, 2026 for the December 11, 2026 Conference without a Brief in Opposition or a prior call for a response (CFR). Under modern Court practice, a petition with an SG waiver is almost never granted without the Court first calling for a response, receiving the government's brief, and redistributing the case.
2. **Severe Vehicle Defect — The Federal Circuit Did Not Pass Upon the Questions Presented:** Both Questions Presented challenge the Court of Federal Claims' March 29, 2023 spoliation ruling (*Jones VI*, 2023 WL 2681819), which reasoned that FBI Agent Ashdown lacked legal "control" over Vernal Police Officer Vance Norton's .40 Glock (then held by Vernal Police Chief Jensen) and Norton's clothing on April 1, 2007 because Chief Jensen had a possessory/privacy interest under *Rakas v. Illinois*, 439 U.S. 128, 143 n.12 (1978), and Agent Ashdown lacked probable cause under the plain-view doctrine (*Arizona v. Hicks*; *Soldal*; *Texas v. Brown*) to believe Norton had committed a crime. Yet **the Petition explicitly concedes (Pet. 13)** — and Judge Taranto's unanimous Federal Circuit opinion confirms (*Jones VIII*, App. 13a–14a) — that **the Federal Circuit did not directly address that Fourth Amendment / *Rakas* issue**. Instead, the Federal Circuit affirmed on the factbound evidentiary record that onsite evidence (photographs of Norton's clean clothing, Chief Jensen's inspection of the Glock, and officer attestations) corroborated Agent Ashdown's account and did not sufficiently indicate criminal activity rather than a self-inflicted wound (App. 14a). The Supreme Court is "a court of review, not of first view," and routinely denies petitions asking it to review trial-court constitutional reasoning the court of appeals bypassed.
3. **Nonprecedential Decision Below and Absence of Any Circuit Split:** The Federal Circuit designated *Jones VIII* **`THIS ORDER IS NONPRECEDENTIAL`** (App. 1a), and *Jones VI* was an unreported Court of Federal Claims discovery-sanctions ruling. That completely undercuts Petitioners' central Rule 10 pitch (Pet. 14–16) that the government "created binding Fourth Amendment precedent" hamstringing federal homicide investigations nationwide: neither ruling binds any court (let alone any regional circuit with criminal jurisdiction). Moreover, Petitioners identify zero conflict among the federal courts of appeals or state high courts, arguing only fact-specific error under settled Supreme Court plain-view and privacy cases (*Warden v. Hayden*, *Coolidge*, *Texas v. Brown*, *Horton v. California*, *Katz*, *Rakas*).
4. **Independent Post-Trial Non-Liability Findings (*Jones VII*, App. 14a–16a):** Even after imposing a spoliation sanction against the government for the destroyed .380 Hi-Point handgun, the Court of Federal Claims conducted a full merits trial (*Jones VII*, 171 Fed. Cl. 576 (2024)) on Petitioners' 1868 Ute Treaty "Bad Men" damages claim and found that the forensic, medical/autopsy (contact wound to the left temple of a left-handed decedent), and scene evidence failed to show by a preponderance of the evidence that Officer Norton shot Todd Murray. The Federal Circuit affirmed that ultimate non-liability finding for lack of clear error (App. 14a–16a). Reopening a 19-year-old civil damages suit (shooting in April 2007; three Federal Circuit appeals) over an ancillary civil-discovery "control" predicate in the face of comprehensive post-trial causation findings is an exceptionally poor vehicle.
5. **Small Tail Risk Preserved (`0.008` rather than `0.001`):** I keep `0.8%` rather than rounding to near-zero because the underlying facts — the 2007 fatal shooting of an enrolled Ute tribal member on the Uintah and Ouray Reservation, the 1868 Ute Treaty's "Bad Men" clause, and documented federal spoliation of shooting evidence — touch recurring concerns of Justice Gorsuch (who authored the Tenth Circuit's landmark opinion in the related reservation-jurisdiction litigation, *Ute Indian Tribe v.
