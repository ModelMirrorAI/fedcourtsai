# Rationale

## Record and baseline

I assign P(any grant) = **0.82**, with a limited grant on question one as the point prediction. This is a cert-stage petition event, not a forecast of the eventual judgment. I used the provisioned `2026-10-26.json` snapshot and `context.json`: forward mode, no cutoff, docket-number Term 2025, sal-v4 band `high`, two distributions, and a June 1 CVSG. I did not retrieve the petition's disposition or subsequent history and do not independently know its outcome.

The committed statpack's matching sal-v4 `high` risk-set rates provide the anchor. Pooling every displayed Term strictly before 2025, namely 2017–2024, gives **314 grant-family outcomes / 898 weighted resolved petitions = 34.97%**, using `prefix_est_grant_rate` and `prefix_weighted_resolved` in `metrics/statpack.json`. This agrees with the bracketed `reached` column in `metrics/statpack.md`; I exclude the 2025 and 2026 rows. These are the committed pack's estimates, not a fresh live-corpus measurement, and I did not establish the underlying blob's current vintage.

The modern discretionary-cert table gives a whole-population grant-family rate of approximately 2.8%, and the Ninth Circuit cut approximately 3.2%, but those unconditioned rates are not the relevant anchor for this selected, CVSG-bearing petition. The paid-segment CVSG cut has 164 weighted resolved petitions, with 29.3% granted and 5.5% GVR, approximately 34.8% combined. That pooled descriptive cut does not condition on the government's recommendation. Nor do I treat the terminal relist buckets as forward transition probabilities: their grant-family rates are about 1.7%, 13.4%, 40.6%, and 36.8% for zero, one, two, and three-plus relists. This petition's post-CVSG redistribution has a different procedural explanation from an unexplained repeat conference.

## Why move substantially above the anchor

The decisive additional evidence is the **October 7 invited brief for the United States**, particularly its Discussion, sections A.2–A.3 and B, and Conclusion. The government recommends granting only question one, with a reformulation that makes the disputed need for an additional misleading-effect inquiry explicit. It identifies conflicting approaches in other circuits, treats the issue as a recurring legal standard rather than a fact-specific disagreement, and expressly addresses the interlocutory posture and continued litigation of the Item 303 claim. That combination makes a limited grant considerably more likely than the unconditioned CVSG rate. No supplied statistical table measures grant probability conditional on a favorable SG recommendation, so the adjustment to 0.82 is judgment, not a claimed measured frequency.

I compared that brief with the petition's actual opening questions and introduction, the opposition's introductory argument and vehicle section, and the reply's vehicle discussion. The petition alleges that the Ninth Circuit substitutes materiality for the distinct misleading-effect inquiry. The opposition disputes that characterization, argues that the two inquiries converge on these facts, and emphasizes remand at the pleading stage and the danger of taking another factbound securities case. Those are meaningful reasons not to assign near-certainty. The government directly confronts the apparent agreement on the petition's original wording and offers a narrower formulation addressing the live dispute.

Question two is appreciably weaker. The government opposes review of that question, disputes the claimed conflicts over Item 303, points to regulatory reconsideration, and identifies a materiality issue not properly presented for review. The surviving required-statements theory also means that success on question one would not necessarily end the litigation. These points account for much of the remaining 18% non-grant probability, rather than supporting a forecast that both questions will be taken.

The 0.60 relist-increment probability reflects a modest preference for one additional conference before a grant, not a lookup from the terminal buckets. The CVSG is already on the record, so its increment is vacuous. The 0.04 summary-route probability is conditional on a grant: the older Macquarie precedent discussed in the filings supplies an analogy, not an identified newly intervening ruling requiring a GVR. The 0.08 dissent-from-denial probability is conditional on denial; the much smaller per-Justice writing probabilities are unconditional and include the unlikely possibility of writing on a grant order. No empirical baseline for these increments or writings was supplied.

## Input limitations and stakes

`questions-presented.txt` is mis-extracted: it begins with a later importance argument rather than the petition's actual questions. I recovered both questions from the petition's opening page I and cross-checked them against page I of the SG brief. This is flagged durably. `documents.json` marks the petition truncated, but not the opposition, reply, or SG brief; none is marked empty or OCR-derived. I relied on the petition's available opening material rather than treating its truncated remainder as silence.

The snapshot records supplemental briefs filed October 20 and 21, but their text is not among the provisioned documents. I do not infer their contents. No related-docket record was provisioned. Two general-precedent web searches and an attempted official Macquarie PDF open returned no usable content; they supplied no evidence. All substantive legal context here is attributed to the available filings, not independently verified precedent. No CourtListener or live-corpus query was needed.

The stakes score of 0.64 reflects national consequences for offering disclosures, issuer compliance, and private securities liability. It is distinct from the much higher grant probability: a strong vehicle for an important commercial-law issue is not necessarily a broadly transformative case.
