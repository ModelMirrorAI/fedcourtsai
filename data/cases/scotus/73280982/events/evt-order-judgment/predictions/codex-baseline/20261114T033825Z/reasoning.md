## Why this case moves upward

First, the statutory distinction is concrete. Section 11(a)'s misleading-omissions branch concerns information needed to prevent affirmative statements from misleading investors. A fact can affect an investment decision without contradicting the reasonable meaning of any statement already made. The separate branch covering facts required to be stated makes it especially difficult to treat this branch as a freestanding obligation to publish all material information. This does not mean Section 11 as a whole excludes pure omissions.

Second, the lower court's actual instructions, not just the petition's characterization, create the problem. In the provisioned decision, printed pages 27–33, the panel acknowledges a distinction between materiality and misleading effect but treats it as collapsing in the interim-results setting. It directs the district court to find a disclosure duty if the omitted information was adequately alleged to be material, while expressly declining to decide the omissions' actual sufficiency. That makes a correction of the governing legal standard more plausible than an affirmance based on a factual finding the panel never made.

Third, two verified precedents favor correction. **Omnicare, Inc. v. Laborers District Council Construction Industry Pension Fund, 575 U.S. 175, 189–194 (2015)** requires a contextual account of why an omission renders a published statement misleading. **Macquarie Infrastructure Corp. v. Moab Partners, L.P., 601 U.S. 257, 264–266 (2024)** distinguishes half-truths from pure omissions under Rule 10b-5(b), while expressly recognizing Section 11(a)'s additional required-statements branch. Macquarie is therefore a close textual analogy, not a holding that abolishes Section 11's independent disclosure duties. Its unanimous opinion by Sotomayor also makes this look less like a conventional ideological securities case. I retrieved those precedents, not any decision in this case.

Fourth, the government's invited brief, printed pages 9–19, supports Robinhood on the granted issue and explains why a further misleading-effect inquiry is indispensable. It opposes review of the separate Item 303 question. The grant adopts its reformulated first question, expressly asking about an additional inquiry. That choice sharpens the legal disagreement; I do not treat adoption of the question, or the government's presence, as a promised merits vote.

## Counterarguments and uncertainty

The respondents' strongest argument, developed in their opposition at printed pages 14–16 and October supplemental brief at pages 2–8, is that these are genuine half-truth allegations, not silence: Robinhood allegedly highlighted favorable growth and selected Q2 results while withholding contrary contemporaneous information. They maintain that the lower court assessed the relevant context rather than eliminating an element. That argument could persuade the Court to affirm the judgment, or to conclude on fuller examination that the supposed dispute is unsuitable for resolution.

There is also an unreviewed Item 303 route. The grant excludes the petition's second question; correcting the misleading-omissions standard need not end the litigation. This both weakens the value of Robinhood's requested relief and makes a narrow remand more likely than final dismissal. I allow roughly nine percentage points for an undisturbed affirmance and five for a DIG; these possibilities account for the 14% complement, rather than being silently counted as disturbed.
