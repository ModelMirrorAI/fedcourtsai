## Why the adjustment is large, but not conclusive

The United States and SEC identify an important, recurring legal issue under Section 11(a)'s misleading-omissions prong: materiality alone should not substitute for asking whether an omission makes an affirmative statement misleading. The SG identifies conflicts with the Third, Tenth, and Eleventh Circuits, treats the Second Circuit's alignment more cautiously, and explains why the First Circuit's extreme-departure approach also does not resolve the statutory distinction (printed pages 10–17). The alleged defect is a categorical standard governing registration statements, not simply the size of Robinhood's interim financial decline. The Court's invitation and the ensuing considered recommendation support taking that question seriously; they are not two statistically independent multipliers.

I checked the older precedent Macquarie Infrastructure Corp. v. Moab Partners, L.P., 601 U.S. 257, 263–266 (2024), through CourtListener MCP. Its Rule 10b-5(b) holding distinguishes actionable half-truths from pure omissions, while expressly recognizing Section 11(a)'s separate required-disclosure language. It supplies a close doctrinal analogy, not a holding that disposes of this petition or eliminates its Item 303 theory. The provisioned SG brief also relies on Omnicare's treatment of misleading registration statements. I do not equate a favorable merits analogy with automatic certworthiness.

The main discount is vehicle quality. The BIO argues that selective Q1 and Q2 disclosures already present a conventional half-truth case, that the claimed split rests on misreading the opinion, and that review would be interlocutory (printed pages 14–16, 22–23, 30–32). The reply responds that the Ninth Circuit did not identify an actual misleading statement and treated materiality as sufficient (printed pages 4–7, 12). The SG independently recognizes the adversity problem and proposes a precise reformulation: whether materiality can establish liability without an additional inquiry into misleading effect (printed pages 18–19). That is a plausible cure, not proof the Court will accept the vehicle.

There is also a surviving Item 303 required-statements theory. The SG explains that the second question lacks a demonstrated conflict warranting review, involves an ongoing regulatory review, and does not properly present a distinct materiality issue the government considers potentially important (printed pages 19–23). A win on Question 1 therefore need not terminate the lawsuit. That incomplete-relief concern helps keep my estimate below certainty, but the SG explicitly concludes that it does not defeat review of the first question (page 23). I assign most remaining probability to denial rather than to a broad two-question grant or an extraordinary procedural exit.
