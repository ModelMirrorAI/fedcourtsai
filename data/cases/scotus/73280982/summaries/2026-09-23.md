## What happened

Robinhood went public in July 2021, disclosing first-quarter 2021 financial results while providing only estimated, partial figures for the still-closing second quarter. After the stock later declined, investors Vinod Sodha and Amee Sodha sued Robinhood, its officers, and its IPO underwriters, arguing the offering documents omitted material interim financial data and misled investors. A federal district court dismissed the suit, applying a heightened standard requiring an "extraordinary" departure from historical performance. The Ninth Circuit reversed, rejecting that heightened standard and sending the case back for the district court to apply ordinary materiality and disclosure rules. One judge dissented. Robinhood then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The case involves two questions about what public companies must disclose when they sell stock to the public. First, whether a company can be held liable for leaving out recent, not-yet-finalized financial data simply because investors might find it important, or whether courts must also separately ask whether leaving it out made the company's other statements misleading. Second, whether a securities-disclosure regulation requiring companies to describe known business trends means a company must disclose and quantify partial data from a quarter that has not yet officially closed.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including amicus briefs supporting review and a reply from Robinhood, and the case was distributed for the Court's conference in late May 2026. On June 1, 2026, the Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief expressing the federal government's views, a step known as a CVSG, meaning the Court has not yet decided whether to take the case.
