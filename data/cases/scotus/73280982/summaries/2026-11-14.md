## In brief

The Court agreed to hear Robinhood's challenge to a ruling that let investors pursue claims over financial information left out of its 2021 IPO documents.

## What happened

Investors sued Robinhood and its IPO underwriters, claiming the company's offering documents omitted interim second-quarter financial data showing a business slowdown, making earlier statements about strong growth misleading. A district court dismissed the suit, applying a rule requiring the omitted data to show an "extreme departure" from historical results before disclosure was required. The Ninth Circuit reversed, holding that ordinary materiality—not an extreme-departure showing—governs whether such omissions must be disclosed, and separately ruled on related disclosure requirements under SEC regulations. Robinhood asked the Supreme Court to review both rulings.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether a company can be held responsible for a misleading registration statement just by showing that left-out financial information was significant to investors, or whether courts must also separately ask whether leaving it out actually made some specific statement in the documents misleading.

## Where it stands

The Court asked the federal government for its views; the government recommended granting review on a reformulated version of the question and denying review of a second question Robinhood raised about separate disclosure rules. The Court granted the petition, limited to the question about materiality and misleading omissions.

## What each outcome would mean

Robinhood, which lost on this issue in the lower court, asked the Court to take up the case and is seeking to overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling. If the Court ultimately sides with Robinhood, investors would need to separately prove that any omitted information made a specific statement misleading, not just that the information was significant, making the investors' claims harder to sustain. If the Court instead sides with the investors, the Ninth Circuit's more investor-friendly standard would stand, and the case would continue under that rule. The case has not yet been argued or decided on the merits.
