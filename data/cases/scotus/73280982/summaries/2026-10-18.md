## In brief

Robinhood asks the Supreme Court to review a Ninth Circuit ruling that revived shareholder claims over disclosures in Robinhood's 2021 public stock offering.

## What happened

Robinhood went public in July 2021, shortly after a surge in trading activity from the "meme stock" and Dogecoin trends. Its offering documents reported first-quarter results and estimated second-quarter revenue, while warning that recent growth might not continue. Investors sued, claiming Robinhood should have disclosed additional interim financial data showing a business downturn before the offering. A district court dismissed the claims, applying a standard requiring the omitted data to reflect an "extreme departure" from historical results. The Ninth Circuit reversed in part, rejecting that standard and adopting a different test, then sent the case back for further review. Robinhood asked the full Ninth Circuit to reconsider; that request was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions about when a company offering stock must disclose financial information from the period just before the offering. The first is whether a company can be held responsible for leaving out recent financial data simply because investors might find it important, without separately asking whether the omission made the company's actual statements misleading. The second is whether a securities regulation requiring disclosure of known business trends requires companies to disclose and calculate the effects of recent, not-yet-finalized financial data before their regular reporting deadlines.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, after which the Court invited the Solicitor General to submit the federal government's views. The government's brief recommends that the Court grant review on the first question, in a reformulated form, but not the second. The docket does not yet show the Court acting on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Robinhood, joined by the underwriters, asked the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the disclosure questions itself, which would not by itself determine who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision favoring the investors, Vinod and Amee Sodha, would stand, and the case would continue in the lower courts under that court's standards. The government has suggested the Court take up only the first question, in revised form, while leaving the second question's resolution for the lower courts to address on remand.
