## In brief

Robinhood asks the Court to review a ruling that revived shareholder claims over its 2021 IPO disclosures.

## What happened

Investors sued Robinhood and its IPO underwriters, claiming the company's offering documents omitted interim second-quarter financial data showing a business slowdown, in violation of securities-law disclosure rules. A district court dismissed the claims, applying a rule that such interim data need only be disclosed if it showed an "extraordinary" departure from historical results. The Ninth Circuit reversed in part, holding that the usual test for whether information is significant to investors can itself establish a duty to disclose it, and that a separate disclosure regulation does not require proof that omitted data reflected a long-lasting pattern. Robinhood asked the full Ninth Circuit to rehear the case; that request was denied. Robinhood then asked the Supreme Court to review both rulings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a company can be held liable for leaving out interim financial data without a separate finding that the omission made some specific statement in its offering documents misleading, and whether a regulation requiring disclosure of known business trends applies only to patterns that are sufficiently long-lasting.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief from the federal government filed after the Court invited the Solicitor General's views, and supplemental briefs from both sides. The case was distributed for the Court's November 13, 2026 conference, where the justices will consider whether to take it up.

## What each outcome would mean

Robinhood, which lost the relevant rulings in the Ninth Circuit, is asking the Court to take the case and reverse. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on one or both questions and decide the legal standards itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's rulings stand, and the investors' lawsuit continues in the lower courts under the standards that court announced. The federal government has urged the Court to grant review of the first question but not the second, while reformulating how that question is worded; the investors oppose review of both.
