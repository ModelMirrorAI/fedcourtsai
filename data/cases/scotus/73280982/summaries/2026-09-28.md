## In brief

Robinhood asks the Court to review a ruling that revived shareholder claims over what it disclosed about its finances before its 2021 stock offering.

## What happened

Investors sued Robinhood and its IPO underwriters, claiming Robinhood's offering documents were misleading because they omitted interim second-quarter 2021 financial data showing a sharp business slowdown after early-2021 trading surges tied to "meme stocks" and Dogecoin. A federal district court dismissed the claims, applying a rule that intra-quarter declines must be disclosed only if "extraordinary." A divided Ninth Circuit panel reversed, rejecting that heightened standard and sending the case back for the district court to reconsider under ordinary materiality and disclosure rules. Robinhood then asked the Supreme Court to take up the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two questions about securities-offering disclosures: first, whether a company can be held liable for leaving out interim financial data simply because investors might find it material, without separately asking whether that omission made some other statement in the offering documents misleading; and second, whether a regulation requiring disclosure of known business trends and uncertainties requires companies to disclose and quantify interim financial data before their regular quarterly reporting deadline.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including amicus briefs and a reply from Robinhood, and was distributed for the Court's May 28, 2026 conference. On June 1, 2026, the Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views on the case; that brief is still pending, and the docket shows no further action since.

## What each outcome would mean

Robinhood and the other petitioners are asking the Court to hear the case. If the Court grants review and later rules for Robinhood, it would narrow when omitted interim financial data can support liability, cutting off the shareholders' revived claims. If it rules for the shareholders (respondents), the Ninth Circuit's standard would stand and their lawsuit would continue in the lower courts. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision remains in effect and the case returns to the district court for further proceedings, without the Supreme Court deciding who is right.
