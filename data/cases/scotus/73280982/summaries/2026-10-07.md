## In brief

Robinhood asks the Court to narrow when IPO documents must disclose interim financial data under securities disclosure rules, after the Ninth Circuit revived shareholders' lawsuit over its 2021 IPO.

## What happened

Shareholders sued Robinhood and its IPO underwriters, claiming the company's offering documents misleadingly omitted unfavorable interim second-quarter 2021 financial data while touting strong first-quarter results. A district court dismissed the suit, applying a standard requiring the omitted data to reflect an "extreme departure" from historical performance. The Ninth Circuit reversed, rejecting that heightened standard and reviving the claims under the ordinary materiality test, while also ruling on related disclosure requirements known as Item 303. Robinhood asked for rehearing before the full Ninth Circuit, which was denied, and then petitioned the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide, first, whether a company can be held liable for failing to disclose material interim financial data without separately asking whether that omission actually made some specific statement in its offering documents misleading. Second, the Court is asked whether a separate disclosure regulation requires companies to disclose and quantify interim financial data before their regular quarterly reporting deadlines, and if so, under what circumstances.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court then invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views on whether the case should be heard; that brief has now been filed. The docket shows no further action since.

## What each outcome would mean

Robinhood, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide the disclosure questions, with no side guaranteed to win. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision reviving the shareholders' lawsuit stands, and the case returns to the district court to apply that court's standards to the claims brought by Sodha and the other investors. Either way, the Court's action at this stage would not itself resolve whether Robinhood's disclosures were adequate.
