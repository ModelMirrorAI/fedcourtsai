## Reasons to move above the anchor

The question presented is concrete: whether the Sixth or Eighth Amendment requires unanimity when state law makes a jury's determination that death is appropriate a prerequisite to a death sentence. Jackson's resentencing produced **8–4 death recommendations**, the new statute's minimum, after unanimous aggravator findings. That squarely separates disputed sentencing unanimity from undisputed jury factfinding. This is direct review, not collateral review constrained by retroactivity or habeas deference, and the provided record does not place it on an imminent execution timetable. The appendix confirms that the Florida Supreme Court rejected both relevant constitutional theories; the Sixth Amendment historical-unanimity alternative was expressly addressed at pp. 14a–15a, not merely assumed preserved from counsel's characterization.

The petition's strongest cert argument is the conjunction of a newly operative sentencing rule, a sharply presented vehicle, and an originalist jury-right argument that is not confined to evolving standards of decency. The petition and reply connect Andres, Ramos, and Apprendi to the jury's historical capacity to withhold death. That argument could attract interest across the Court's usual capital-punishment divisions. Experienced Supreme Court counsel and three organized amicus filings reinforce the petition's presentation and importance, but do not themselves supply four votes.

The provisioned related-case files identify **Hunt v. Florida, No. 26-78**, through one shared amicus brief. That record shows Florida's opposition filed on October 29, 2026, following extensions. The Jackson petition's footnote 5 says Hunt presents the same unanimity issue plus a separate ex post facto question. This makes coordinated consideration a plausible explanation for Jackson's pre-conference rescheduling. It is **not** a docketed hold order, a grant in Hunt, or evidence that either petition will be granted. Jackson's cleaner single constitutional question also makes it a plausible lead vehicle if the Court takes the issue.

## Reasons denial remains more likely

Florida's principal answer is substantial: jury findings that establish death eligibility are different from the ultimate selection of punishment. The opposition relies on **McKinney v. Arizona, 589 U.S. 139, 144–45 (2020)**, for the proposition that a jury need not perform the final weighing or sentencing selection once it has found the necessary aggravator. I independently verified that passage through CourtListener's McKinney opinion, opinion ID **4510103**, using a targeted document search. It does not decide the exact statutory-choice/unanimity argument here, as the reply emphasizes, but extending jury protections past that eligibility boundary is a real doctrinal step. Florida also invokes the surviving jury-sentencing aspect of Spaziano; the reply's claim that Andres supplies an already controlling answer is disputed, not an uncontested premise.

The asserted state-high-court conflict is also weaker than a current conflict between jurisdictions administering competing rules. The petition identifies Delaware's Rauf and Connecticut's Daniels, but both jurisdictions have abandoned capital punishment, and Florida disputes whether Daniels addressed the same determination. The competing decisions predate McKinney. This reduces the practical uniformity rationale even though it does not eliminate the constitutional importance. The BIO's list of earlier denied petitions is evidence of prior unsuccessful attempts as represented in the filed record, not binding precedent; the reply fairly distinguishes their statutes and questions. I do not treat those denials as adjudications of this new law.

Florida's prediction that its legislature might remove the jury's selection role after a loss is speculative and does not defeat Jackson's present interest. It receives little weight. More important is uncertainty over whether four Justices wish to revisit capital sentencing in this posture and whether the historical theory attracts enough interest beyond the Justices generally receptive to capital defendants. Those considerations leave denial at roughly three in four despite an unusually substantial petition.
