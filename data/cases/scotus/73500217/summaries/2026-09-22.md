## What happened

Dr. Melanie Crites-Bachert, a surgeon with operating privileges at Providence Health & Services – Oregon, was suspended and lost access to Providence's facilities after she refused a COVID-19 vaccine, which was authorized only for emergency use. She sued Providence, arguing federal law gave her a right to informed consent before taking the vaccine and a right to sue when that consent was denied. A federal district court dismissed her case, and the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that federal drug law reserves enforcement to the government rather than allowing private lawsuits. The Ninth Circuit denied further review in December 2025. Crites-Bachert has now asked the Supreme Court to take up the case.

## What the Court is being asked

Federal law lets the government authorize experimental drugs for emergency use, but requires that patients be told they have the choice to accept or refuse the product. A separate law lets courts recognize that members of the armed forces can sue to enforce that same choice. The petition asks whether an ordinary citizen, not in the military, should likewise be allowed to sue when someone denies her that right to refuse an emergency-use drug, or whether — as the Ninth Circuit held — only the federal government can enforce that protection.

## Where it stands

Providence has waived its right to respond to the petition, meaning it has chosen not to file a brief opposing review unless the Court asks for one. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, where the Justices will decide whether to grant or deny review.
