## In brief

A Texas death-row inmate asks the Supreme Court to hear his claim that prosecutors withheld evidence and used false testimony about drugs in the victim's system.

## What happened

Richard Vasquez was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas for killing four-year-old Miranda Lopez. Decades later, Vasquez says he discovered that prosecutors knew before trial that a toxicology report used against him was internally inconsistent, and that trial testimony about cocaine in the victim's blood was false. He has raised these claims repeatedly in state and federal courts. Most recently, he filed a new federal habeas petition without first getting permission from the court of appeals, as a rule governing repeat petitions requires; a district court dismissed it, and the Fifth Circuit denied him permission to appeal that dismissal. The state, represented by the prison system's director, opposes review, arguing Vasquez's case is a poor vehicle for deciding anything and that his claims lack merit.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether claims like Vasquez's—that prosecutors hid evidence and knowingly used false testimony—count as part of a "second or successive" habeas petition when the inmate did not know about the suppressed evidence or falsity at the time of his first petition. If such claims are treated as second or successive, an inmate must clear extra procedural hurdles before a court will even consider them.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for a December 4, 2026 conference, then rescheduled, and Vasquez filed a reply brief on November 17, 2026. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will take the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Vasquez, the inmate, is asking the Court to hear his case and rule that his claims are not "second or successive." If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide the legal question, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision denying Vasquez permission to appeal stands, leaving his claims blocked under the current rule, which the state's filings say matches the approach most other circuits have taken.
