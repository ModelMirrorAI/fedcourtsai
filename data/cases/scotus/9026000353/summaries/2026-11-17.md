## In brief

A death-row inmate asks the Court to decide whether certain new claims about suppressed evidence count as a barred "successive" habeas filing.

## What happened

Richard Vasquez was convicted of capital murder and sentenced to death in Texas for the killing of four-year-old Miranda Lopez. Years later, Vasquez says he discovered that prosecutors knew before trial that a toxicology report used against him was unreliable, and that testimony about cocaine in the victim's system was false. After losing earlier rounds in state and federal court, Vasquez filed a new federal habeas petition raising these claims without first getting permission from the court of appeals, as a rule requires for repeat petitions. The district court dismissed it, and the Fifth Circuit denied him a certificate allowing an appeal. Texas's director of the prison system, the respondent, argues the claims were rightly treated as repeat filings and that Vasquez's case has multiple problems that would prevent the Court from deciding the question anyway.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a claim that the government hid evidence or used false testimony, when the prisoner did not know about it at the time of his first habeas filing, should be treated the same as an ordinary repeat petition — meaning the prisoner would need advance court permission before a lower court could even consider it.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's December 4, 2026 conference and was then rescheduled; the docket does not show that any conference has yet taken place or that the Court has acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Vasquez, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case and rule that his claims should not have been treated as a barred repeat filing. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision denying him a certificate of appeal stands, and his claims remain blocked. If the Court grants review, it would hear full briefing and argument and decide the legal question itself; a grant alone would not determine who wins. Texas, the respondent, opposes review, arguing the case has procedural problems and that Vasquez's underlying claims lack merit regardless of how the legal question is resolved.
