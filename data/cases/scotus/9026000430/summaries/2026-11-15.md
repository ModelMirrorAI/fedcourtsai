## In brief

A horsemen's association asks the Court to decide whether letting a private racing authority draft binding rules violates limits on delegating government power to private entities.

## What happened

Congress created the Horseracing Integrity and Safety Authority, a private nonprofit, to write and enforce nationwide rules for thoroughbred racing, subject to review by the Federal Trade Commission. The National Horsemen's Benevolent and Protective Association and others sued, arguing this arrangement unconstitutionally hands government power to a private body. After earlier rulings and a congressional amendment giving the FTC power to change the Authority's rules, the case returned to the Fifth Circuit, which held that the Authority's rulemaking role is constitutional but that its enforcement powers (investigating, sanctioning, and suing people) are not, because the FTC's oversight comes only after enforcement actions occur. Both sides separately asked the Supreme Court to review the decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The horsemen's association asks the Court to decide whether it violates the rule against delegating government power to private entities for a private organization's rules to take legal effect before the government agency overseeing it can revise or block them, even if the agency can change the rules later.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference on December 4, 2026, after briefing that included the federal government's and the Authority's opposition, an amicus brief, and the horsemen's association's reply. No order on the petition has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The National Horsemen's Benevolent and Protective Association, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up this additional question alongside related cases. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether the timing of the FTC's authority over the Authority's rules matters constitutionally, without yet deciding who is right. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling upholding the rulemaking arrangement as constitutional stays in place, leaving that part of the case over, though related petitions on other questions would still proceed separately.
