## 3. Case-Specific Analysis under the *Barefoot* / *Nken* Stay Standard

To obtain a stay of execution pending certiorari under *Barefoot v. Estelle*, 463 U.S. 880, 895 (1983), and *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010), an applicant must demonstrate (1) a reasonable probability that four Justices will vote to grant certiorari, (2) a significant possibility of reversal (a fair prospect that five Justices will reverse the judgment below), and (3) a likelihood of irreparable harm absent a stay, with the balance of equities weighed against the State's strong interest in the timely enforcement of its criminal judgments (*Hill v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 573, 584 (2006)). While irreparable harm from execution is undisputed, Guardado cannot establish a reasonable probability of certiorari or a significant possibility of reversal on any of his claims:

1. **Challenge to Florida's Executive Clemency Process:**
   Guardado's application (`application.txt`, pp. 2–3) focuses almost exclusively on a Fourteenth Amendment Due Process challenge to Florida's clemency process. Relying on Justice O'Connor's concurring opinion in *Ohio Adult Parole Authority v. Woodard*, 523 U.S. 272, 288–90 (1998), Guardado argues that Florida's clemency process is an arbitrary "hollow proceeding" (likened to a two-headed coin flip) because no death-sentenced prisoner has received clemency in Florida since 1983 (across 138 executions) and Governor DeSantis has signed 50 death warrants since 2019 while stating he will not grant clemency to murderers or felony sex offenders.
   As the Florida Supreme Court explained in its unanimous October 29, 2026 opinion below (*Guardado v. State*, No. SC2026-1605, slip op. at 9–12, retrieved via CourtListener opinion ID `11516852`), Guardado was not denied access to Florida's clemency process: he was notified in March 2026 of a clemency interview scheduled for July 2026, and his October 5, 2026 death warrant expressly states that executive clemency was considered pursuant to the Rules of Executive Clemency and found inappropriate. Under *Woodard*, clemency is a matter of executive grace rather than an adjudicatory right, and Justice O'Connor's concurrence contemplated judicial intervention only where a state official literally decides by chance or arbitrarily denies a prisoner any access to the clemency process—not where a governor exercises executive discretion against commuting sentences for aggravated capital murder. Both the Florida Supreme Court and the U.S. Supreme Court have repeatedly rejected this exact Florida clemency argument, including in *Zakrzewski v. State*, 415 So. 3d 203, 211 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 146 S. Ct. 57 (2025).

2. **Underlying Successive Postconviction Claims (Untimely/Procedurally Barred IAC and Warrant-Period Claims):**
   In the Florida Supreme Court decision below (No. SC2026-1605, slip op. at 12–17), Guardado also raised two additional claims alongside his clemency challenge: (a) that trial and initial postconviction counsel were ineffective in investigating penalty-phase mitigation (including his stay at the Dozier School for Boys, childhood abuse, and substance abuse), rendering his death sentence unreliable under the Sixth, Eighth, and Fourteenth Amendments; and (b) that Florida's 31-day post-warrant litigation schedule violated due process.
   First, the Florida Supreme Court held that Guardado's ineffective-assistance/mitigation claims were both untimely under Fla. R. Crim. P. 3.851(d)(1)–(2) and procedurally barred because the factual basis was available nearly two decades ago and could have been raised in prior collateral proceedings. That holding rests on an adequate and independent state procedural bar that precludes U.S. Supreme Court review on certiorari. Second, the Florida Supreme Court rejected the challenge to the 31-day warrant period because Guardado received full notice and an opportunity to be heard (a *Huff* hearing and appellate review), consistent with settled precedent in which the U.S. Supreme Court has repeatedly denied certiorari and stays (*Reaves v. Florida*, No. 26A524 / No. 26-5865, denied Oct. 20, 2026; *Jennings v. State*, 422 So. 3d 107 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 146 S. Ct. 402 (2025); *Bates v. State*, 416 So. 3d 312 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 146 S. Ct. 66 (2025); *Jones v. State*, 419 So. 3d 619 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 146 S. Ct. 79 (2025); *Hutchinson v. State*, 416 So. 3d 273 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 145 S. Ct. 1914 (2025); *Tanzi v. State*, 407 So. 3d 385 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 145 S. Ct. 1914 (2025); *Bell v. State*, 415 So. 3d 85 (Fla.), *cert. denied*, 145 S. Ct. 2872 (2025)).
