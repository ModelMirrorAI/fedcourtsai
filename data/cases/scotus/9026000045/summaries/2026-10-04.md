## In brief

A former credit union CEO asks the Court to let him sue in federal district court over constitutional problems with an agency proceeding against him.

## What happened

Jeffrey Moats was CEO of a Texas credit union placed into conservatorship, after which the National Credit Union Administration Board (NCUA) fired him and later brought charges seeking restitution, penalties, and a lifetime industry bar. Moats sued in federal district court, arguing the administrative judge assigned to his case was unconstitutionally shielded from removal and that he was entitled to a jury trial, among other claims, and asked the court to block the NCUA proceeding. The district court dismissed his suit, ruling that a federal statute stripped it of jurisdiction to hear such claims. The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, and rehearing was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the law governing credit unions cuts off federal district courts from hearing constitutional challenges to an agency's structure and procedures before the agency proceeding runs its course, and, separately, whether Congress is even allowed to withhold district-court jurisdiction over such challenges consistent with the Constitution's separation of powers.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference on October 9, 2026, after both sides filed briefs and Moats filed a reply; the docket does not yet show any action taken at or after that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Moats, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the jurisdictional question, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling stands, meaning Moats's suit stays dismissed and he must pursue his constitutional claims, if at all, only after the NCUA proceeding concludes and through the normal appellate route. The government has also asked the Court to instead hold the case pending a related case, Johnson v. United States Congress.
