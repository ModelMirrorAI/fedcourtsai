## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a former credit union executive's challenge to a law that blocked his lawsuit against a federal banking regulator's enforcement proceedings.

## What happened

Jeffrey Moats led a small Texas credit union for 25 years before regulators placed it into conservatorship and fired him. The National Credit Union Administration later brought administrative charges against him seeking millions in penalties and a lifetime industry ban. Moats sued in federal district court, arguing the administrative judge handling his case was unconstitutionally insulated from removal and that he was being denied his right to a jury trial. The district court dismissed his suit, finding that a federal banking statute stripped it of jurisdiction to hear such claims while the agency proceeding was ongoing. The Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals agreed and affirmed that dismissal.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a law that bars courts from interfering with notices or orders issued by the credit union regulator also prevents a district court from hearing a claim that the regulator's whole process is unconstitutionally structured, before the agency proceeding is even finished. It also raises whether Congress can constitutionally cut off court review of such structural challenges at all, even if the law tries to do so.

## Where it stands

The petition asking the Court to take up the case was distributed for the Court's conference on October 9, 2026, and the Court denied it on October 13, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Moats, who brought this case, asked the Court to hear his challenge. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Fifth Circuit's ruling in place: Moats's lawsuit remains dismissed, and he cannot pursue his constitutional claims in district court while the NCUA's enforcement case proceeds. He may still raise those arguments later, after the agency completes its process, by seeking review in a federal appeals court. The denial does not mean the Court agreed the lower court was right.
