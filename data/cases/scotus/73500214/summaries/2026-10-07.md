## In brief

Mortgage borrowers ask the Court to review a ruling that federal banking law bars New York's requirement that banks pay interest on mortgage-escrow accounts.

## What happened

Alex Cantero and other New York homeowners sued Bank of America, saying it should have paid them interest on their mortgage-escrow accounts as New York law requires, but the bank refused, arguing federal law preempts that requirement for national banks. The Supreme Court already heard this case once and sent it back in 2024, telling the Second Circuit to use a more careful, comparative analysis. On remand, a divided Second Circuit panel again ruled the New York law preempted. The petitioners say that decision conflicts with a First Circuit ruling reaching the opposite result on a similar Rhode Island law.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal law governing national banks overrides, or preempts, a state law requiring those banks to pay a minimum interest rate on funds held in mortgage-escrow accounts. The question also touches on how closely lower courts must follow the Court's 2024 instructions for weighing when a state law interferes too much with national banks' powers.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, and the Court has invited the Solicitor General, the federal government's top Supreme Court lawyer, to file a brief giving the government's views before deciding whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Cantero and the other homeowners are asking the Court to hear the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether New York's interest-on-escrow law applies to national banks, with no outcome assumed in advance. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling that the law is preempted stands, meaning Bank of America would not have to pay the interest in this case. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the law was correctly decided, only that the lower ruling remains in place.
