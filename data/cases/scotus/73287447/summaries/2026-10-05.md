## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review Nexstar's bid to throw out DirecTV's antitrust lawsuit over blacked-out TV channels during a pricing dispute.

## What happened

DirecTV accused Nexstar and two affiliated broadcasters, Mission and White Knight, of conspiring to demand inflated fees to carry their stations, causing negotiations to collapse and DirecTV subscribers to lose access to those channels. DirecTV sued under federal antitrust law. A district court dismissed the case, ruling DirecTV could not sue because it never paid the allegedly inflated prices. A divided Second Circuit appeals court reversed, holding DirecTV could proceed because it was the sole target of the alleged scheme and had a long history of dealing with the broadcasters. One judge dissented, saying DirecTV's claimed losses were too speculative.

## What the Court is being asked

The broadcasters asked the Court to decide whether a company that walks away from a deal, rather than paying an allegedly inflated price, can still sue under antitrust law for the business losses that followed. They argued other appeals courts require someone to have actually paid the inflated price before suing. DirecTV countered that no court has adopted such a strict rule and that the lower court's decision rested on facts specific to this dispute.

## Where it stands

The Court considered the petition at conferences in June and September 2026, and after full briefing, denied it on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Nexstar, Mission, and White Knight asked the Court to take up the case and reverse the appeals court's decision. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Second Circuit's ruling in place, meaning DirecTV's lawsuit can continue in the lower courts, where discovery was already underway. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Second Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision standing without the Court deciding the underlying legal question itself.
