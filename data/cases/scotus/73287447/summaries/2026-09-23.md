## What happened

Nexstar and two smaller broadcasters, Mission and White Knight, negotiated with DirecTV to renew agreements letting DirecTV carry their television stations. DirecTV alleges the three broadcasters illegally coordinated to demand inflated fees. When DirecTV refused to pay and negotiations collapsed, Mission's and White Knight's stations went dark for DirecTV subscribers, and DirecTV says it lost customers and profits as a result. DirecTV sued under federal antitrust law. A district court dismissed the case, ruling DirecTV could not sue because it never actually paid the higher prices. The Second Circuit reversed, holding DirecTV could proceed given its long history of dealing with the broadcasters and the fact that it was the sole target of the alleged scheme. One judge dissented. The full appeals court declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The broadcasters argue that a company which walks away from a deal, rather than paying an allegedly inflated price, should not generally be allowed to sue for antitrust damages, because its claimed losses are too indirect and speculative. DirecTV counters that no fixed rule bars such lawsuits, and that courts should look at the specific facts, including whether the parties had a past pattern of doing business together and whether anyone else was more directly harmed. The Court is asked to decide which approach governs when someone who refused to pay a price they say was illegally fixed can sue over the resulting losses.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, with DirecTV opposing review and the broadcasters filing a reply, and the case has been distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, where the Justices will decide whether to take up the case.
