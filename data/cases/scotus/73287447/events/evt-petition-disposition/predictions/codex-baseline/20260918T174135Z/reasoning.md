## Why 13%, below the 17.2% anchor

**The petition has real cert-worthy features.** It presents a published, divided Second Circuit ruling reversing dismissal, identifies Ninth and Tenth Circuit authorities, and raises a threshold antitrust question with effects beyond broadcasting. The dissent argues that the asserted causal chain and damages are too speculative. The Court's actual request for a response after a waiver is affirmative attention, unlike a voluntarily filed opposition. These features keep the forecast materially above an ordinary unselected private petition. Sources: petition, printed pp. 9–20 and 24–29; snapshot entries June 1–23.

**The opposition substantially weakens the claimed square split.** The petition's strongest formulation is that the Second Circuit permits a nonpurchaser's downstream-loss action where the other circuits would reject it. The opposition responds that neither comparator establishes a categorical prohibition and that prior competitive dealings and the lack of more direct victims distinguish this case. That is not merely an unsupported denial of a split: targeted MCP retrieval confirms that City of Oakland v. Oakland Raiders, 20 F.4th 441, 459 n.11, 460 (9th Cir. 2021), declines a bright-line rule and discusses why Oakland's previous dealings in an allegedly anticompetitive market failed to establish a useful benchmark. Montreal Trading Ltd. v. Amax Inc., 661 F.2d 864, 868 (10th Cir. 1981), expressly recognizes that established dealings can reduce speculation. Sources: opposition, printed pp. 11–18; CourtListener opinion 5131036, searches for bright-line and regular course; opinion 395374, search for regular course.

Those precedents do not prove the Second Circuit applied the common standard correctly. Petitioners fairly argue that Oakland also had a long relationship and that DirecTV's alleged history does not solve subscriber-causation problems. The more persuasive cert-stage inference, however, is that the dispute can be characterized as fact-sensitive error correction rather than incompatible legal rules. The distinction is important: a potentially erroneous application is not automatically a strong candidate for discretionary review. Sources: petition, printed pp. 18–24; opposition, printed pp. 14–27; City of Oakland, 20 F.4th at 460.

**The posture makes waiting plausible.** The briefs describe a reversal of pleading-stage dismissal and continuing district-court proceedings, not a final antitrust liability or damages judgment. Whether a longstanding relationship supplies a reliable benchmark, whether customers left because of the blackout, and whether saved retransmission fees offset losses remain disputed. This posture offers the Court a reason to let the factual record develop. It does not create a jurisdictional bar, and the petition's point that antitrust standing drove the appellate reversal retains force. Sources: petition, printed pp. 9–12, 20–24, 29; opposition, printed pp. 7 n.2, 21–27.

**The two distribution entries overstate the strength of a classic relist signal.** The first scheduled conference was preceded by a response request, and the second distribution comes after the resulting briefing. I retain elevated as the harness requires, but modestly discount its generic reached rate rather than treating this as repeated consideration of a fully briefed petition. The statpack itself warns that rescheduling can add distribution entries. Source: snapshot and the paid-segment relist-table scope note.

Together these considerations support **P(any grant) = 0.13**, with denial the modal disposition. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted empirical model. The most important uncertainties are whether the Justices see a functional split despite the comparators' qualifications, what the unreviewed reply adds, and whether federal antitrust-policy concerns prompt a CVSG.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The **0.30** further-distribution forecast leaves substantial room for one real relist after completed briefing, but predicts no increment as the modal path. The **0.12** CVSG forecast reflects a concrete federal interest: the opposition describes the United States' appellate amicus supporting reversal and opposing a categorical nonpurchaser bar (printed pp. 7–8, 19–20). I did not independently retrieve that brief, and participation below is neither a Supreme Court CVSG nor a current recommendation on certiorari. These incremental probabilities are subjective; the statpack provides no state-conditioned forward hazards for them.

The **0.06 summary-route probability is conditional on a grant**, not the unconditional chance of summary relief. There is no identified intervening authority requiring reconsideration, and the disputed multi-factor inquiry is poorly suited to summary correction. The **0.05 writing probability is conditional on denial**, not an individual-Justice forecast. Neither conditional number is presented as a published base rate.

The **0.52 big-case score** measures significance if the question is decided, not grant likelihood. Restricting private damages claims by buyers excluded from purchases could matter across industries, and the existing dispute concerns commercially important television rights. The specific history of dealing, single-target allegation, and pleading posture nevertheless provide avenues for a narrower decision.
