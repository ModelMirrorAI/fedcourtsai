## In brief

Plumbing trade groups and unions ask the Court to decide whether New York's bans on gas appliances in new buildings are overridden by a federal energy law.

## What happened

New York City and New York State passed laws effectively barring gas-burning appliances from most new buildings. Trade associations, unions, and businesses sued, arguing that a federal law, the Energy Policy and Conservation Act, overrides these laws because it bars state and local rules concerning covered appliances' energy use. The federal trial courts ruled for the city and state. The Second Circuit Court of Appeals agreed, holding that the bans regulate which fuel appliances use, not how much energy they use, so the federal law does not override them. In doing so, the Second Circuit said it was knowingly departing from a Ninth Circuit decision that reached the opposite conclusion about a similar ban in Berkeley, California.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state or local law that effectively stops gas-burning appliances from using any energy at all counts as a rule "concerning" those appliances' energy use, a category of rules that the federal energy law overrides. The petitioners say yes, because such a ban functions the same as directly limiting an appliance's energy use to zero; the lower court said no, because the law reads "energy use" as a fixed, lab-tested number set before an appliance reaches consumers.

## Where it stands

The petitioners filed their petition asking the Court to take the case on November 13, 2026. The city and state have until December 17, 2026 to respond, and the Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The plumbing associations and allied unions and businesses, who lost in the Second Circuit, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments and decide whether the federal energy law overrides the gas bans, resolving a disagreement between circuit courts. If it denies the petition, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving the New York City and State bans in effect and upholding the city and state's win below.
