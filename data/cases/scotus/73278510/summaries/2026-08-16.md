## In brief

Michael Salazar's privacy lawsuit against Paramount over a sports website's data sharing with Facebook is before the Supreme Court, which has heard argument and awaits a decision.

## What happened

Salazar signed up for a free email newsletter on Paramount's 247Sports website and later watched video content there while logged into Facebook. He sued under a 1988 federal video privacy law, claiming Paramount improperly shared his Facebook ID and viewing history with Facebook without consent. A federal district court dismissed his lawsuit, ruling he did not count as a "consumer" protected by the law because his newsletter signup was not a subscription to video content. The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals agreed and affirmed the dismissal, splitting from two other appeals courts that had read the law more broadly. The Supreme Court agreed to hear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The law protects "consumers" of a "video tape service provider" from having their viewing history disclosed without consent. The Court is asked whether a person becomes such a protected consumer by buying, renting, or subscribing to anything at all from a business that also provides video content, or only by buying, renting, or subscribing to video-related goods or services specifically.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed, including numerous supporting briefs from outside groups. It was set for argument on October 14, 2026, and now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Salazar, who lost in the lower courts, asked the Supreme Court to take up the case. If the Court reverses, it would side with Salazar, holding that signing up for the free newsletter made him a protected "consumer," and his lawsuit could go forward. If the Court affirms, Paramount's win stands, and Salazar's case remains dismissed because he did not subscribe to video-related goods or services. If the Court vacates, it would send the case back without deciding which side is right.
