## In brief

Michael Salazar asks the Supreme Court to decide whether a 1988 video privacy law covers a sports website's sharing of his newsletter-subscriber data with Facebook.

## What happened

Michael Salazar signed up for a free email newsletter from 247Sports, a sports news website owned by Paramount Global. He alleges that while logged into Facebook, he watched videos on the site, and that tracking code installed on the site sent his Facebook ID and viewing history to Facebook without his consent. He sued under the Video Privacy Protection Act, a 1988 law restricting disclosure of a person's video-rental history. Paramount argued Salazar was not a "consumer" under the law because he never rented, bought, or subscribed to video materials themselves—only to a non-video newsletter. The district court and, on appeal, the Sixth Circuit agreed with Paramount and dismissed the suit, while two other appeals courts had reached the opposite conclusion on similar facts.

## What the Court is being asked

The law protects people who rent, buy, or subscribe to "goods or services" from a company that rents, sells, or delivers videos. The Court is asked whether that phrase covers only audiovisual goods or services, or instead covers anything the company offers, including unrelated items or a free newsletter unconnected to video content.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review on January 26, 2026, after the case was distributed for more than one conference. The case has been fully briefed and was set for argument on October 14, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Salazar, who lost in the Sixth Circuit, is asking the Court to reverse that ruling. If the Court affirms, Paramount's win stands, and Salazar would remain unable to sue because signing up for a non-video newsletter would not make him a "consumer" under the law. If the Court reverses, it would rule that any goods or services from such a company count, which would let Salazar's claim proceed. If the Court vacates, it would send the case back without deciding whether Salazar is a "consumer," leaving that question for further proceedings.
