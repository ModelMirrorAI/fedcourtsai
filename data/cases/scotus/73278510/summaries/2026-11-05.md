## In brief

The Supreme Court heard arguments over whether a man who signed up for a sports website's free newsletter counts as a "consumer" under a 1988 video privacy law.

## What happened

Michael Salazar signed up for a free email newsletter from 247Sports, a sports news website owned by Paramount Global. He watched free videos on the site while logged into Facebook, and the site shared his Facebook ID and viewing activity with Facebook without his consent. Salazar sued under the Video Privacy Protection Act, which bars a "video tape service provider" from disclosing a "consumer's" viewing history without consent. A federal district court dismissed his case, and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that Salazar was not a "consumer" because the law only covers people who rent, buy, or subscribe to audiovisual goods or services, and his newsletter subscription did not qualify. Salazar appealed to the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The case turns on how broadly to read the law's definition of "consumer." The law defines a consumer as someone who rents, buys, or subscribes to "goods or services" from a company that also deals in video materials. The Court must decide whether that phrase covers only audiovisual goods or services, or whether it includes anything the company offers, including things unrelated to video, such as a free email newsletter.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review on January 26, 2026, and the case was argued on October 14, 2026. It now awaits a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Salazar, the person who sued and lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to reverse the Sixth Circuit's ruling. If the Court rules for Salazar, it would find that he does count as a "consumer" under the law, reviving his lawsuit and sending the case back for further proceedings on his privacy claim. If the Court rules for Paramount instead, it would uphold the Sixth Circuit's narrower reading, leaving Salazar's lawsuit dismissed and confirming that only subscriptions to video-related offerings can make someone a "consumer" under the statute.
