# Forecast of the Court's judgment and reasoning

The Court will vacate the Sixth Circuit's judgment and remand. A subscription to a qualifying provider's non-audiovisual goods or services can make someone a consumer under the Video Privacy Protection Act. Salazar's complaint will therefore not fail merely because his subscription concerned a newsletter rather than audiovisual material. This will not establish that Paramount ultimately owes damages.

The predicted division is 6–3. Roberts, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Jackson will support the judgment; Thomas, Alito, and Barrett will dissent. Kagan is the leading authorship forecast. Alito will write the principal dissent, emphasizing ordinary meaning in context, the statute's video-transaction focus, and the consequences of importing modern advertising disputes into that scheme. Barrett is the most uncertain member of the predicted minority; a larger majority is a substantial alternative.

The majority will distinguish the relationship that makes a person a consumer from the information that the statute protects. Section 2710(a)(1) uses goods or services without an audiovisual modifier. The personally identifiable information definition separately requires information identifying a person's request for, or receipt of, specific video materials or services. Reading those limitations as separate requirements gives effect to the different language. The phrase identifying the video tape service provider specifies the source of the goods or services; it does not silently replace their description with the narrower description used elsewhere.

The Court will reject the contention that consumer status must arise from the very transaction that generates protected video information. Its reading will not protect information about every ordinary purchase: the disclosure still must concern qualifying video information, and the defendant still must be a covered provider. Concerns about old purchases, free browsing, and targeted advertising will not justify adding an audiovisual restriction to the consumer definition. The consent provisions supply a lawful route for covered disclosures rather than a reason to narrow the definition.

The holding will be categorical on the question presented, not an exception dependent on whether this newsletter contained video links. It will remain bounded on the other liability elements. The Court will not decide whether every business displaying videos is a provider, whether the particular Facebook identifiers and viewing data qualify as personally identifiable information, or whether all remaining elements are pleaded adequately. Vacatur rather than a final liability judgment will preserve those questions on remand.

Article III standing is the principal procedural complication raised at argument. The Court will proceed to the consumer question rather than dispose of the case on that ground. A DIG remains a minority possibility, not the central forecast; there is no identified recusal supporting an equally divided disposition. A concurrence emphasizing reserved issues is possible, particularly from Gorsuch, but is not part of the modal lineup.
