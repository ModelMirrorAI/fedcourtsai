## Why above the baseline

The strongest point for Salazar is not simply that goods or services sounds broad in isolation. Congress used a video-specific modifier in the adjacent information definition, but not in the consumer definition, while using the same provider-source phrase in both. His opening brief's treatment of the contrasting definitions and advertising exception (printed pp. 22–26, 30–34), reinforced by the reply, makes a contextual case for separate who-is-protected and what-is-protected requirements. The official text of 18 U.S.C. § 2710(a)(1), (a)(3), and (b)(1) confirms that contrast. The opening brief expressly requests vacatur and remand (p. 52); the reply renews that request (p. 24).

Paramount's strongest answer is that the whole definition identifies consumers acting in a provider's audiovisual capacity, not purchasers of whatever else that entity sells. The respondent's integrated-transaction argument (printed pp. 19–26) gives real force to its contextual examples and temporal mismatch objection. I do not dismiss that reading as absurd or assume the Court must choose the broadest possible text. It is the principal merits reason for the 0.22 undisturbed probability.

The argument shifts me moderately above the 0.704 reference, not to near certainty. Kagan expressly characterized Salazar's interpretation as the most natural result of combining the definitions and repeatedly pressed the availability of consent. Jackson's book-purchaser hypothetical and separate consumer/information analysis, and Sotomayor's invocation of provisions protecting information independently of consumer status, support relatively strong petitioner leans. Those are substantive questions, not votes, but they expose respondent's specific structural vulnerability.

Kavanaugh is especially important to the cross-bloc forecast. He pressed Salazar hard about business coverage and modern data sharing, then asked Clement why the Court should not follow the consumer definition as written while leaving provider and information issues for future cases. His insistence on an answer about practical consent also weakens the claim that policy consequences necessarily decide this definition. I treat the later exchanges as a lean toward a bounded statutory ruling, not a promise to reject every defense.

Roberts initially supplied a purposive hypothetical favorable to Paramount but later challenged reliance on extreme examples rather than difficult mixed-business cases. That is a modest, uncertain majority lean. Gorsuch said little; his question about limiting similar audiovisual materials shows interest in a different limiting provision. I put him in the textual majority with substantially less confidence than Kagan or Jackson, and do not pretend that his sparse questioning independently supplies a vote. Thomas and Alito were more persistently concerned with video-era context, ordinary phrasing, and modern breadth. Barrett explored provider coverage and, importantly, standing; her challenge to Clement's temporal distinction cuts the other way. Placing her in dissent is my least secure lineup choice. A 5–4 or 7–2 decision is plausible even with the same holding.
