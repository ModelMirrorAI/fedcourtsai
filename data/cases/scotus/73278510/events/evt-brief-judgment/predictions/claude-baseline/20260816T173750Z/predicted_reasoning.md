# Forecast: Salazar v. Paramount Global, No. 25-459 (merits judgment, briefed moment)

The Court will **affirm** the Sixth Circuit's judgment. The case is set for
argument October 14, 2026; expect a decision between February and June 2027,
with the later half of that window more likely for a case argued in the
October sitting that draws separate writings.

## Judgment class

Affirmed — the Court adopts the Sixth Circuit and D.C. Circuit's reading:
a "consumer" under 18 U.S.C. §2710(a)(1) is a renter, purchaser, or
subscriber of a video tape service provider's *audiovisual* goods or
services, and a free online-newsletter subscription does not qualify. This
resolves the acknowledged 2–2 circuit split (CA2 and CA7 broad; CA6 and CADC
narrow) in favor of the narrow side. I put the chance the judgment below is
disturbed in any form — reversal, vacatur, or partial reversal — at 45%.
Within the disturbed branch, outright reversal adopting the Second Circuit's
unqualified reading is most likely; a narrower vacatur-and-remand keyed to
Salazar's unaddressed allegation that he used the newsletter subscription to
view videos is the secondary route. A dismissal as improvidently granted is
very unlikely — petitioner sold this vehicle on its clean final judgment and
single assumed-premises question, and nothing on the docket has undermined
that. An equally divided affirmance is near-nil: no recusal signal appears
on the docket.

## Vote lineup

6–3 to affirm. Majority: Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett.
Dissent: Gorsuch, Sotomayor, Jackson — the plain-text/pro-privacy axis,
pressing that "goods or services" is unqualified and that the (a)(3) PII
definition already supplies the video-specific limit. A unanimous or 7–2
affirmance on Duguid's model is the nearest alternative lineup; the nearest
reversal world is a Bostock-shaped textualist coalition, which I judge less
likely because the contextual reading here is far stronger than it was in
Bostock. Authorship is not scored, but I expect Kagan or Barrett to write —
both have authored this Court's recent context-over-literalism statutory
opinions — with a dissent by Gorsuch or Jackson.

## Ground and breadth

The majority rests on the interlocking-definitions argument: the parallel
between "renter, purchaser, or subscriber" in (a)(1) and "rental, sale, or
delivery" in (a)(4), the video-specific PII definition in (a)(3), and the
consent provisions that make little sense for consumers with no video
transactions — situating the case in the Duguid/Fischer/Dubin line that
declines to read targeted statutes at their literal semantic limits when
that would convert them into general-purpose damages regimes. The holding
will be categorical (any non-audiovisual transaction fails the definition
nationwide), while expressly reserving the "video tape service provider"
and PII questions both courts below assumed away.

## Separate writings

Expect at least one dissent (Gorsuch or Jackson) on plain-meaning grounds,
echoing Judge Bloomekatz's dissent below and Judge Easterbrook's Gardner
opinion. A concurrence splitting rationale from result — e.g., resting on
the "subscriber of" verb rather than the audiovisual gloss — is plausible
but not expected to fracture the majority's ground.
