## In brief

A man convicted of health care fraud asks the Court to decide whether a federal workers' compensation program counts as a "health care benefit program" under the fraud statute.

## What happened

Christopher Rydberg was convicted of conspiring to commit health care fraud, money laundering, and defrauding the United States, based on a scheme that billed a federal workers' compensation program (FECA) and Blue Cross Blue Shield for unnecessary compound drug prescriptions. He argued that FECA, a no-fault workers' compensation system, is not a "health care benefit program" under the fraud statute because it is not a "plan" or "contract." The district court rejected this argument, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed, citing its own precedent that the term should be read broadly. The Fifth Circuit also denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a workers' compensation program like FECA fits the legal definition used in the health care fraud statute, which covers any "plan or contract" providing medical benefits. Rydberg's petition also asks the Court to reject the Fifth Circuit's practice of reading that definition broadly, and to clarify how a rule favoring defendants when a criminal law is unclear should apply.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond to the petition. The petition has been distributed for the Court's December 4, 2026 conference, where the justices will decide whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Rydberg, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments and decide whether FECA counts as a health care benefit program, a ruling that could affect his convictions and sentence. If the Court denies the petition, the Fifth Circuit's decision affirming his convictions stands, and the case ends without the question being resolved.
