# Predicted Reasoning: *City of Santa Maria, California, et al. v. Adolfo Cardenas* (No. 26-94)

## Procedural Trajectory: Further Distributions and CVSG

The docket currently records one conference distribution (on September 2, 2026, for the September 28, 2026 Long Conference), followed the next day (September 3, 2026) by the Court's call for a response due October 5, 2026, and no call for the views of the Solicitor General (CVSG).

From this posture, at least one additional distribution is virtually certain (`relist-increment` = 0.98): once the respondent files a brief in opposition (or the time to respond expires) and the petitioners file their reply, the Clerk will redistribute the petition for a future conference. After that post-response distribution, the petition is likely either to be disposed of after one or two conferences or to be held pending the Ninth Circuit's en banc decision in *King v. Villegas*, No. 23-1713 (argued en banc June 2, 2026).

The Court will not call for the views of the Solicitor General (`cvsg-increment` = 0.02). Although the question involves Federal Rule of Evidence 410(a) and 42 U.S.C. § 1983, the dispute arises from a municipal police pursuit and a California state-court *nolo contendere* plea, implicates no distinctively federal programmatic or treaty interest, and Primarily asks for a procedural hold or grant, vacate, and remand (GVR) in light of pending Ninth Circuit en banc proceedings.

## Disposition and Summary-Disposition Route

The most likely ultimate disposition is a denial of certiorari (`predicted_disposition` = `denied`, `probability` of any grant = 0.38), though the probability of a grant—specifically a GVR—is far above the baseline for a paid cert petition.

Once the brief in opposition is filed, the respondent will highlight two substantial threshold obstacles to Supreme Court intervention in this interlocutory appeal:
1. **Lack of interlocutory appellate jurisdiction over the *Heck* issue and the municipal defendant:** The case comes to the Court on an interlocutory appeal from the denial of summary judgment on qualified immunity. Under *Swint v. Chambers County Commission*, 514 U.S. 35 (1995), and Ninth Circuit precedent (*Cunningham v. Gates*, 229 F.3d 1271, 1284 (9th Cir. 2000)), the denial of summary judgment under *Heck v. Humphrey*, 512 U.S. 477 (1994), is not itself an immediately appealable collateral order, and the panel below explicitly noted (Pet. App. 2a n.1) that it bypassed the motion to dismiss the City's interlocutory appeal only because it affirmed.
2. **Independent, fact-bound ground in the district court:** The district court did not rely on Rule 410(a) or *King v. Villegas*; instead, it denied summary judgment on *Heck* because it found a genuine factual dispute over whether the officers' use of deadly force was temporally and factually distinct from the conduct supporting Cardenas's no-contest convictions (Pet. App. 18a–21a). Because the case is interlocutory and returns to the district court for further proceedings—where the three-judge panel decision in *King* has already been vacated en banc, 171 F.4th 1163 (9th Cir. 2026)—the Court is modestly more likely than not to deny review and leave any post-*King* *Heck* arguments to be addressed in the lower courts on remand or after final judgment.

Conditional on the Court granting review, however, a **summary disposition via GVR** in the cert order itself is overwhelmingly the likelier route over plenary merits review (`summary-disposition-route` = 0.85). The Ninth Circuit panel's unpublished memorandum disposition rejected the petitioners' *Heck* defense on the sole ground that *King v. Villegas*, 156 F.4th 979 (9th Cir. 2025), barred admission of a *nolo contendere* plea under Rule 410(a) (Pet. App. 3a–4a). Because the Ninth Circuit subsequently granted rehearing en banc in *King* and ordered that the panel opinion not be cited as precedent, the single legal pillar of the Ninth Circuit's *Heck* holding has already been vacated. If the Court grants the petition (either immediately or after holding it for the Ninth Circuit's en banc decision in *King*), it will almost certainly enter a brief order granting certiorari, vacating the judgment, and remanding for further consideration in light of the en banc proceedings in *King v. Villegas*.

## Question Presented and Conditional Merits Ground

If the Court were instead to grant plenary review (conditional probability 0.15 given a grant, primarily if the en banc Ninth Circuit were to reinstate the *King* panel's Rule 410 holding and the Court overlooked the interlocutory vehicle problems), the Court would take the single question presented as framed in the petition: whether a court may refuse to apply the *Heck v. Humphrey* bar when the plaintiff's underlying conviction rests on a *nolo contendere* plea by invoking Federal Rule of Evidence 410(a). If decided on the merits, the Court would likely reverse or vacate on the ground that *Heck* turns on the legal existence of an outstanding criminal judgment rather than the evidentiary admission of a plea under Rule 410(a), joining the Fifth, Sixth, and Tenth Circuits.

## Separate Writings on Denial

If the Court denies the petition, a published dissent from denial or statement respecting the denial is unlikely (`dissent-from-denial` = 0.04). While Justice Thomas or Justice Alito could conceivably write briefly to criticize the Ninth Circuit panel's refusal to vacate its reliance on *King* after *King* was taken en banc, the interlocutory posture and jurisdictional wrinkles make a separate writing improbable.
