## In brief

Santa Maria asks the Court to review a Ninth Circuit ruling that let a man's excessive-force lawsuit proceed despite his earlier no-contest plea to resisting arrest.

## What happened

Adolfo Cardenas fled from police after a crash, and Officer Brice tased him after a foot chase. Cardenas pleaded no contest to resisting an officer and other charges, then sued the city and Brice, claiming the tasing was excessive force. A federal doctrine called Heck bars such lawsuits when winning would undermine a valid conviction. The district court ruled Cardenas's suit was barred because the conviction's record covered the same conduct. A divided Ninth Circuit panel reversed, holding the record did not clearly show which acts of resistance supported the conviction, and separately noted a now-vacated circuit ruling about plea evidence. A judge who agreed with the result disagreed with that reasoning.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the appeals court was wrong to let the lawsuit continue despite the earlier conviction, particularly whether it improperly disregarded Cardenas's own statements about what his plea covered, and whether a separate, now-withdrawn evidence ruling the panel also relied on should affect the outcome.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief opposing review, a reply, and a supporting brief from local-government groups. It was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, after an earlier distribution and request for a response.

## What each outcome would mean

Santa Maria and Officer Brice, the petitioners, are asking the Court to overturn the Ninth Circuit's decision, which favored Cardenas by letting his lawsuit go forward. If the Court grants review and ultimately reverses or vacates that decision, Cardenas's lawsuit could be barred or sent back for further review under the Heck doctrine. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, and Cardenas's excessive-force claim against the city and Brice continues in the lower courts.
