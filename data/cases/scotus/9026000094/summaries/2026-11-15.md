## In brief

A city asks the Court to reverse a lower-court ruling that let a man's excessive-force lawsuit proceed despite his earlier no-contest plea to resisting arrest.

## What happened

Adolfo Cardenas led police on a chase that ended when Officer Andy Brice used a taser to stop him. Cardenas later pleaded no contest to resisting arrest and other charges, then sued the City of Santa Maria and Brice, claiming the taser use was excessive force. A federal district court ruled that a legal doctrine barring civil suits that would undercut a valid criminal conviction blocked his claim, since his plea's factual basis seemed to cover the same conduct. A divided Ninth Circuit panel reversed, holding the record did not clearly show which acts underlay the conviction, and alternatively citing a now-vacated circuit decision about an evidence rule. The city appealed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the appeals court was wrong to let the lawsuit continue, given that Cardenas's own lawyer had repeatedly stated in earlier proceedings that the police report covering all his actions, including the tasing incident, formed the basis for his plea. The city argues the panel ignored those statements and relied on a legal theory from a separate case that has since been withdrawn.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a response and reply, and has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently rescheduled after conferences on November 6 and November 13, 2026. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The City of Santa Maria and Officer Brice, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision, which favored Cardenas. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the lawsuit should have been allowed to proceed. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, and Cardenas's lawsuit against the city and officer continues in the lower courts. A denial would not mean the Court agreed with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning, only that it left that decision in place.
