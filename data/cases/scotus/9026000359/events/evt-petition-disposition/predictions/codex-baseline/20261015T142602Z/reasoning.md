I reduce the approximately 5.0% anchor to 1.2% for the following case-specific reasons:

- The five questions are framed as whether review is warranted because the decision denies property rights or conflicts with established principles, rather than as a sharply identified disagreement among courts. The petition cites other circuits on constructive delay, but does not establish opposing holdings on the same legal question and materially comparable facts. Its principal submission is case-specific error correction.
- The property-interest issue turns on the content of Pontiac's approvals. The petition emphasizes ownership and completed conditional rezoning (petition pp. 8-14). The Sixth Circuit expressly recognizes that a completed discretionary approval can sometimes create an entitlement, but finds that further discretionary permits remained and were not eliminated by the agreement (App. A19-A24). That narrower ground makes a categorical claim that the court disregarded ownership less persuasive as a reason to take this vehicle.
- The takings issue is the strongest candidate for review because deliberate obstruction can be more serious than ordinary administrative delay. Nevertheless, the court assumed a sufficient property interest and addressed delay, bad faith, causation, application deficiencies, and alternative use of the land (App. A25-A31). It did not categorically exclude all delay-based takings. The petition's competing twenty-one-month account and demand for jury resolution largely contest how those principles apply to this record (petition pp. 19-23).
- The First Amendment allegation is troubling if true, but the appellate court assumed a viable retaliation claim for its municipal-liability analysis and held that the mayor lacked final zoning authority, with no evidence that the council adopted the act (App. A38-A42). The petition's apparent-authority and agency arguments, including reliance on a dissent, do not supply a clean conflicting rule governing municipal liability. A potential constitutional wrong and liability of this municipal defendant are distinct questions.
- The record has one distribution, a waiver, no CVSG, and no recorded amicus filings or separate appellate writing. None proves a lack of interest, and a waiver is not a merits concession. Together they supply no affirmative institutional signal strong enough to offset the vehicle problems.

The published appellate opinion and the genuinely consequential property-rights and retaliation allegations keep the grant probability above zero. The principal uncertainty is whether the Court views the delay or entitlement issue as a recurring constitutional problem despite the petition's presentation. The separate 0.30 significance score reflects those potential implications, while recognizing that the immediate dispute concerns a particular local development. It is not the grant probability.
