## Why 1.2%, below 5.0157%

The five questions presented ask whether certiorari is warranted because the decision denies constitutional rights or conflicts with established principles. They do not isolate a concrete division between courts on a single dispositive rule. The petition argues several errors in applying existing doctrine to a specific redevelopment dispute. That is appreciably weaker evidence for review than a developed, outcome-determinative conflict.

- **Entitlement and delay:** The strongest point is the distinction between the owner's approved conditional rezoning and the tenants' outstanding licensing requirements. The petition says the Sixth Circuit found no protected entitlement because discretionary approvals remained, while petitioners argue the rezoning agreement and mandatory permit language already supplied one (petition pp. 8–10). Resolving that contention appears to require close attention to the particular agreement, ordinance, and remaining conditions. The constructive-denial discussion cites several circuits as recognizing related delay principles; it does not establish opposing holdings on the same entitlement question (pp. 10–14).
- **Substantive due process:** The petition contends that the courts applied the conscience-shocking standard too rigidly and failed to appreciate allegedly deliberate obstruction (pp. 15–18). Its cited disagreements about the standard's application do not, on this presentation, amount to a cleanly framed and developed split. This theory also depends on contested characterization of official conduct.
- **Takings:** The petition argues that approximately 21 months to conditional approval was extraordinary and that the issue should have gone to a jury (pp. 18–24). That is a fact-sensitive challenge rather than a clearly separated rule conflict. Its own reproduction of Agins's footnote refers to the absence of extraordinary delay, despite the petition's argument that the footnote supplies no such limitation (pp. 20–21). I treat that internal tension as weakening the claimed direct conflict, without purporting to decide the takings merits.
- **Retaliation and municipal liability:** The alleged threat to a third party because Brown's company sued the city is serious. But the petition acknowledges that the mayor lacked actual final development-approval authority and relies substantially on apparent authority, general agency analogies, and a Praprotnik dissent (pp. 24–28). The retrieved discussion in Pembaur v. City of Cincinnati, 475 U.S. 469, 481–483 (1986), distinguishes discretionary action from final municipal policymaking authority. That makes this presentation less like an obvious disregard of controlling precedent and more like a request to extend municipal liability. I have not independently determined the mayor's authority under local law.

I retain nonzero grant probability because prolonged obstruction following affirmative rezoning and alleged retaliation for seeking judicial relief are consequential constitutional allegations, and the property-entitlement framing could attract interest if later briefing sharpens it. I do not treat the petition's account as established facts or infer the absence of a genuine conflict from the missing opposition alone. The numerical reduction to 1.2% is judgmental, not a fitted estimate from a matched sample.
