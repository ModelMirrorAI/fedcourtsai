# Why this petition is above baseline but still a denial

The petition's introduction and reasons for review, particularly printed pages 1–2, 15–18, and 35–36, identify a substantial structural question: whether corporate form can remove significant federal functions from the Appointments Clause. The petition reports that Justice Gorsuch, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito, expressly identified an unpresented appointments issue in his Consumers' Research dissent. That is meaningful evidence of interest, but a statement in another case is not a commitment to grant this one, nor does three-Justice interest establish a fourth vote. I rely on the supplied filings for that account; attempted official-source web verification returned no usable content.

The most important positive signal is the litigation package. Both the federal memorandum, printed page 2, and the Authority's opposition, printed pages 33–35, affirmatively support review of the separate enforcement challenge because the Fifth Circuit invalidated part of a federal statute and disagreed with the Sixth Circuit. This creates a plausible opportunity to add the preserved appointments question, or eventually to remand this petition in light of a lead decision. The cert reply, printed pages 4–7, persuasively distinguishes this petition as the only vehicle preserving that issue and argues that governmental status is logically antecedent to private nondelegation.

But the **specific issue this cross-petition adds has no identified live appellate split**. The federal opposition, printed pages 3–4, emphasizes that the Sixth Circuit case did not present an appointments claim. The Authority's opposition, printed pages 28–34, similarly distinguishes a split over enforcement from agreement over rulemaking and the absence of an appointments conflict. The Court can decide how far private enforcement may go without deciding that the Authority must be reclassified as governmental. A highly grantworthy lead petition therefore does not make every accompanying cross-petition highly grantworthy.

There is also a real vehicle dispute. The Authority argues that Texas tracks conduct no covered races and thus cannot establish injury traceable to the alleged appointments defect (opposition, printed pages 27–28). The reply, printed pages 2–4, answers with forgone interstate-simulcasting revenue, alleged enforcement threats, and pre-enforcement standing, while noting that the government does not advance the objection. I do not assume the objection is correct or that it was waived; I treat it as added complexity in an otherwise legal question. The government also describes the Authority's functions as recommendatory under FTC supervision. The reply contests that account through the Lucia/PCAOB analogy. Those merits arguments show importance but do not independently establish a need for immediate review.

Balancing the unique preserved question and strong lead-case prospects against no direct split, contested standing, and the availability of a narrower enforcement case moves me from 5.15% to **18%**, not to an even-odds grant. This adjustment is judgment, not a fitted statistical estimate. Most probability remains on denial, potentially coordinated with action on the lead petitions. The main uncertainty is whether four Justices want to expand the lead litigation to governmental status, rather than whether the overall controversy matters.
