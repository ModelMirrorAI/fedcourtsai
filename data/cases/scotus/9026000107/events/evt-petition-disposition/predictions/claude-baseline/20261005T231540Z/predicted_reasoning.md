# Forecast: what the Court will do with No. 26-107, Walls v. Prince George's County

**Bottom line.** The petition is more likely denied than granted, but it sits well above the ordinary paid petition: P(any grant) = 0.22.

## Relists and timing

The docket shows **one distribution** (for the Conference of September 28, 2026), entered the same day the Court **called for a response** after the County had waived. A petition pulled for a response is not considered at that conference, so this one has not yet been voted on. The brief in opposition arrived October 1; the reply is due around October 15. I expect the petition to be **redistributed at least once more** (near-certain) and to accrue **one to three further distributions** in total, because the Court called for a response in the companion Pacific Legal Foundation petition, Tedford's Tenancy, LLC v. City of New York (No. 26-110, same September 28 distribution, same amicus slate), whose response is now due November 10. The natural course is to hold Walls and consider the two together, which points to a decision at a December 2026 or January 2027 conference rather than at the first October redistribution. A plain re-distribution and prompt denial in late October or early November is the main alternative.

## CVSG

No CVSG is on the docket. I expect **none** (P = 0.07). The respondent is a county, no federal program is implicated, and the Court has decided its recent takings-finality cases (Pakdel, Knick) without inviting the Solicitor General. The one path to a CVSG is the Court treating the jurisdictional-versus-prudential question as one touching the Federal Circuit's takings docket against the United States, which the petition flags but does not develop.

## Which question, if granted

If the Court grants, it will most plausibly take **Question 1 as written** (whether an owner must exhaust legislative remedies to ripen an as-applied regulatory takings claim), because the Fourth Circuit expressly rested on the legislative-amendment requirement and did not demand a further waiver application, so the question is cleanly presented. Question 2 (whether a court may dismiss on prudential ripeness grounds a claim that satisfies Article III) is the question shared with Tedford's Tenancy; if the Court wants that broader doctrinal question, Walls, which comes from a federal court of appeals, is the more conventional vehicle than a New York Appellate Division rent-stabilization case, so a grant on both questions, or a grant limited to Question 2 with Tedford's held, is possible. A reformulation folding both into a single question about Pakdel's "de facto finality" standard is the third possibility.

## Summary disposition versus plenary review

Conditional on a grant, I put the chance of disposition in the cert order itself at **0.35**. Two summary routes are live: a Pakdel-style per curiam reversal of an unpublished court-of-appeals ruling that over-reads the finality requirement (the Court did exactly that to the Ninth Circuit in 2021 on a Pacific Legal Foundation petition), and a hold-and-GVR if the Court grants Tedford's Tenancy as the plenary vehicle and decides it first. Plenary review remains the likelier form of any grant, because Question 2 asks the Court to resolve a doctrinal tension it has so far only noted in dicta.

## If denied

Expect a **silent denial** more often than not (P(any dissent or statement respecting denial | denial) = 0.15). The plausible separate writing is a short statement or dissent from Justice Thomas, who has repeatedly criticized the Williamson County line and its "prudential" recasting, possibly joined by Justice Gorsuch; a denial of both companion petitions on the same day would be the more likely occasion for such a writing.

## Merits, conditionally

If granted and argued, the likelier outcome is reversal or vacatur: the Court's recent takings-finality decisions have uniformly rejected lower-court requirements beyond a definitive government position, and a rule that an owner must petition the legislature to change the law before suing fits poorly with Pakdel and with First English's treatment of temporary takings. The jurisdictional-versus-prudential question is the harder one, and a majority could decide the case on Question 1 and leave the status of prudential ripeness unresolved, as it did in Knick and Pakdel.
