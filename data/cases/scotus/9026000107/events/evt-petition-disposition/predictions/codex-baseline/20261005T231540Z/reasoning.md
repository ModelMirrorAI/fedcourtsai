## Why 22%, rather than 5% or a likely grant

**Reasons to move upward.** The Court affirmatively requested an opposition after waiver, and the snapshot lists six amicus filing entries supporting attention to the dispute. That combination is more informative than a routine distribution alone, though it does not reveal how many Justices favor review. I read the filing entries, not the amici's substantive briefs. The petition presents a concrete access-to-court problem with consequences beyond one parcel: whether an owner must seek a political change in governing law before challenging its application. Its account of the Fourth Circuit's reasoning makes the legislative-amendment requirement central even regardless of the status of the waiver request (petition, pp. 10–13). The alleged contrary authorities include state high-court decisions and a Ninth Circuit decision, not merely generalized disagreement with the outcome (petition, pp. 13–18).

**The precedent cuts both ways.** *Pakdel v. City and County of San Francisco*, 594 U.S. 474, 478–80 (2021), distinguishes modest finality from administrative exhaustion: once the government's position is conclusive, procedural missteps do not defeat ripeness; but available procedures can still matter when they could clarify the government's position. That distinction makes the petition legally substantial without establishing that this particular agency letter satisfies finality. I verified the Court's opinion through CourtListener opinion 4699044 rather than accepting either brief's characterization of it.

**Reasons not to make grant the modal outcome.** The Fourth Circuit decision is unpublished (petition, p. 6). The County characterizes its letter as preliminary advice and says Walls never obtained a decision through the available waiver or category-amendment process (opposition, pp. 1–5, 9–11). The petition counters that the agency lacked authority and that the proposed amendment would not actually permit the requested interim system (petition, pp. 8–13). Sorting out those competing accounts could make the case a fact-specific application of existing finality doctrine rather than a clean vehicle for a categorical rule. The disagreement over whether water and sewer access is unavailable or simply delayed adds factual complexity without deciding the cert question.

The County also points out that several split authorities concern older legal frameworks or intermediate state decisions (opposition, pp. 12–13). That objection reduces confidence in the breadth and current sharpness of the asserted split, but does not erase the state high-court or Ninth Circuit authorities the petition identifies. Conversely, the opposition's observation that some courts agree with the Fourth Circuit is not by itself a rebuttal to a split. Question 2 mixes terminology across different ripeness contexts; the petition's extensive jurisdictional-versus-prudential catalogue does not establish that all those cases would reach different results on this record (petition, pp. 21–34). A narrower finality question could be addressed without resolving the entire classification debate.

Balancing those considerations, I assign **P(any grant) = 0.22** and predict **denied**. This is a judgmental upward adjustment, not a fitted response-request/amicus coefficient. Its main uncertainty is whether the Justices see an impermissible legislative-exhaustion rule or an incomplete local permitting process.
