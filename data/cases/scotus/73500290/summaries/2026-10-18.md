## In brief

A medical-supply company asks the Court to revive its antitrust suit against insurers and a rival medical group it says conspired to drive it from the market.

## What happened

United Biologics, doing business as United Allergy Services, supplied equipment and technicians to primary-care doctors offering allergy treatment. It sued Amerigroup Tennessee and a rival allergy practice, claiming they conspired to push it out of the Tennessee market by pressuring the doctors who worked with it to stop. A federal district court dismissed the antitrust claims, finding United Allergy could not sue because it was not a direct competitor, and later ruled against its remaining state-law claims after discovery. The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that a rule limiting who may sue over a shared markup in a supply chain barred United Allergy's claims because the doctors it worked with were harmed more directly.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a long-standing rule that blocks lawsuits by parties who are only indirectly affected by anticompetitive conduct should also block a company that says it was the deliberate target of a boycott by competitors working together to eliminate it, where it seeks to recover its own lost profits rather than a share of an overcharge paid by others.

## Where it stands

## What each outcome would mean

United Biologics, as petitioner, is asking the Court to hear its case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal question itself, which would not by itself determine who wins. If it denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the dismissal of United Biologics' antitrust claims and the judgment for Amerigroup Tennessee and the other defendants. The Solicitor General's views, once filed, may influence whether the Court takes the case, but the request itself decides nothing.
