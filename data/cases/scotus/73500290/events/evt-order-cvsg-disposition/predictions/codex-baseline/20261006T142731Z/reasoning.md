## Case-specific adjustments

**Reasons to raise the roughly 35% anchor:** The question is a concrete boundary of a recurring federal antitrust doctrine, not merely a request to revisit a damages calculation. The petition, pp. 13–17, argues that the Sixth Circuit extends Illinois Brick beyond passed-on overcharges to the intended target's distinct lost profits. It contrasts Mosaic Health and Crimpers in the Second Circuit. The petition also reports criticism of the panel's rule in separate en banc-stage writings. The panel text actually provisioned at App. 29–32 applies a broad indirect-injury rule to both completed transactions and lost sales. That supplies a substantial legal issue even after discounting the petition's advocacy. The CVSG shows that the Court has elected to investigate the federal enforcement implications rather than dispose of the petition at its first consideration; that signal is already largely priced into the anchor, so I do not count it twice.

**Reasons not to move above 50%:** The opposition identifies substantial vehicle problems rather than simply denying the issue's importance. At BIO pp. 13–16 it characterizes the panel's separate proximate-cause discussion as an unchallenged alternative holding. The actual appendix, App. 30–31, does discuss speculative profits from unidentified prospective physician customers independently of Illinois Brick. That is a real obstacle. But the excerpt focuses on prospective customers whereas the complaint also alleges lost business through existing customers; I cannot conclude from the opposition's characterization alone that this alternative rationale necessarily defeats every recoverable category. This is a meaningful downward adjustment, not a dispositive jurisdictional conclusion.

The BIO, pp. 16–24, also challenges the petition's characterization of UAS as a direct competitor or joint venture and distinguishes Mosaic's 340B-related injury from UAS's upstream supplier losses. The petition's claimed split is therefore plausible, but not established merely by different outcomes in different business relationships. Finally, BIO pp. 32–33 argues that percolation and the state-law summary-judgment record make this a poor vehicle. I give that argument some weight without equating the disposition of the separate state-law claims with adjudication of the federal question presented.

My net adjustment is modestly upward, to **P(any grant) = 0.42**. The issue and the potentially overbroad categorical rule justify being above the high-band average; the alternative-ground dispute, split distinctions, and unavailable reply keep the forecast below even odds. The future Solicitor General recommendation is the largest unresolved information item. I do not presume that the invitation predicts a recommendation to grant.
