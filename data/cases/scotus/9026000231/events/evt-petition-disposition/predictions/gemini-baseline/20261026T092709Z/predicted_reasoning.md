# Predicted Court Reasoning and Procedural Trajectory: *Mario Delaine v. United States* (No. 26-231)

## 1. Procedural Trajectory: Further Distributions (`relist-increment`) and CVSG (`cvsg-increment`)

The docket as of the October 26, 2026 snapshot records **one distribution** (`distribution_count = 1`): on September 30, 2026, following the Solicitor General's September 18, 2026 waiver of the United States' right to respond and the September 24, 2026 amicus brief of the National Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers (NACDL), the petition was distributed for the Conference of October 16, 2026. Before that conference convened, however, the Court on October 14, 2026 requested a response from the United States (originally due November 13, 2026, and extended on October 26, 2026 to December 14, 2026).

- **Further distributions (`relist-increment = 0.98`)**: Because a Call for a Response (CFR) is already on the docket after the initial September 30, 2026 distribution, the petition will necessarily be distributed at least once more (`distribution_count >= 2`) after the Solicitor General files the Brief in Opposition (due December 14, 2026) and Petitioner's reply window runs, barring only an extraordinary pre-distribution Rule 46 dismissal or mootness event (~2%). Looking past that guaranteed first post-BIO distribution (anticipated for a January 2027 conference), the most likely outcome (~75% conditional probability) is that the Court denies certiorari after that single post-BIO conference (`distribution_count = 2` at resolution), with roughly a 20–25% chance of one or more post-BIO conference relists (`distribution_count >= 3`) if a chambers circulates a draft statement or scrutinizes the circuit cases cited in the briefs.
- **No CVSG (`cvsg-increment = 0.001`)**: The United States is the party respondent represented by the Solicitor General, and the Court has already called for a Brief in Opposition from the Solicitor General. The Court will not issue an amicus Call for the Views of the Solicitor General (CVSG).

## 2. Expected Disposition: Denial of Certiorari (`disposition = 0.11`)

After the Solicitor General files the Brief in Opposition and the petition is redistributed for conference in early 2027, the Court will most likely **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari without comment:

1. **Absence of a Genuine Circuit Conflict on Intentional Battery Causing Great Bodily Harm**: Every court of appeals to consider Florida felony battery under Fla. Stat. § 784.041(1)—or a materially identical state battery statute requiring an intentional non-consensual touching or striking of another person that causes great bodily harm—has held that the offense qualifies as a violent felony under ACCA's elements clause (18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i)) or the parallel career-offender Guidelines definition. Both the Sixth Circuit below (*United States v. Delaine*, 171 F.4th 880 (6th Cir. 2026)) and the Eleventh Circuit (*United States v. Vail-Bailon*, 868 F.3d 1293 (11th Cir. 2017) (en banc), reaffirmed post-*Borden* in *United States v. Sheely*, No. 24-13967, 2026 WL 2349505 (11th Cir. Aug. 13, 2026)) agree on Fla. Stat. § 784.041(1), and the Seventh Circuit reached the same conclusion for Indiana felony battery in *Douglas v. United States*, 858 F.3d 1069 (7th Cir. 2017). The decisions invoked by Petitioner from the Ninth Circuit (*United States v. Gomez*, 165 F.4th 1199 (9th Cir. 2026) (en banc), *cert. denied*, No. 25-7193 (U.S. May 18, 2026); *United States v. Lopez*, 2026 WL 2165840 (9th Cir. July 28, 2026)), Tenth Circuit (*United States v. Sjodin*, 161 F.4th 1245 (10th Cir. 2025)), and Fourth Circuit (*United States v. Redd*, 85 F.4th 153 (4th Cir. 2023)) addressed assault or mayhem statutes that can be violated through reckless or negligent conduct without an intentional touch or strike directed against another person.
2. **Non-Dispositive Posture of the *Marks* Question**: Although Petitioner frames the case as an opportunity to resolve how *Marks v. United States*, 430 U.S. 188 (1977), applies to the 4-1-4 decision in *Borden v. United States*, 593 U.S. 420 (2021), the Sixth Circuit expressly held that it did not need to take sides in the *Marks* debate because Florida felony battery satisfies ACCA's elements clause under either Justice Kagan's four-Justice plurality opinion in *Borden* or a narrower anti-recklessness reading. That makes *Delaine* an unsuitable vehicle to resolve any circuit disagreement over *Marks*.

## 3. Conditional Merits Scope, Summary Disposition Route, and Separate Writings

- **Question Presented if Granted**: If the Court were to grant certiorari, it would take the single Question Presented in the petition as written—whether an offense that requires an intentional act (here, intentionally touching or striking another person against their will), but no separate mens rea as to the resulting physical pain or injury (great bodily harm, permanent disability, or permanent disfigurement), qualifies as a "violent felony" under ACCA's elements clause, 18 U.S.C. § 924(e)(2)(B)(i). If granted and decided on the merits, the Court would likely **affirm** the Sixth Circuit's judgment, holding that when a statute requires a purposeful or knowing application of physical contact consciously directed "against the person of another" (*Borden*, 593 U.S. at 431 (plurality opinion)) and requires that the contact actually cause great bodily harm (*Delligatti v. United States*, 604 U.S. 423, 432 (2025); *Stokeling v. United States*, 586 U.S. 73, 84–85 (2019)), the elements clause does not demand an additional specific-intent or knowing mens rea element attached to the severity of the resulting injury.
- **Summary Disposition Route (`summary-disposition-route = 0.09`)**: Conditional on a grant of review, plenary briefing and oral argument is the overwhelming path (~91% conditional probability). There is no pending Supreme Court merits case on ACCA's elements clause or *Borden* mens rea for which *Delaine* would be held and GVR'd, and the Solicitor General is highly unlikely to confess error given uniform Sixth and Eleventh Circuit precedent upholding Fla. Stat. § 784.041(1) as an ACCA/Guidelines predicate.
- **Dissents or Statements Respecting Denial (`dissent-from-denial = 0.04`)**: Conditional on denial, the most likely outcome (~96%) is an unsigned order denying certiorari with no separate writing. There is a modest (~4%) chance that a Justice—most plausibly Justice Sotomayor, Justice Jackson, Justice Gorsuch, or Justice Thomas—authors a short dissent from denial or statement respecting denial addressing either the interaction between *Borden* and strict-liability result elements under ACCA or the lower courts' recurring questions over applying *Marks* to *Borden*.
