## In brief

Mario Delaine asks the Court to decide whether his prior battery conviction counts as a "violent felony" under a federal sentencing law, after that finding triggered a 15-year minimum sentence.

## What happened

Delaine pleaded guilty to illegally possessing a firearm as a felon. Because he had three prior convictions the government said counted as "violent felonies," a federal law called the Armed Career Criminal Act required a minimum 15-year sentence instead of the roughly 4-year sentence he otherwise faced. Delaine argued his Florida felony-battery conviction should not count, because it only requires intentionally touching someone, not intending or knowing that the touch would cause injury. The district court and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals disagreed and applied the enhanced sentence; the Sixth Circuit denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a crime that requires only an intentional act, but does not require the person to have intended or known that the act would cause physical pain or injury, can still count as a "violent felony" for purposes of the mandatory minimum sentence. Some appeals courts say the intent requirement must cover the resulting injury, not just the act itself; others, including the court that decided Delaine's case, say intending the act is enough.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court then asked for a response from the government, which had initially waived its right to respond. The government's response deadline has since been extended to November 4, 2026; the docket shows no action yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Delaine, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case and overturn the Sixth Circuit's ruling. If the Court grants review, it will hear full briefing and arguments and decide the legal question itself, which would not by itself determine who wins. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, leaving Delaine's enhanced 15-year minimum sentence in place and leaving the current circuit split over this sentencing question unresolved.
