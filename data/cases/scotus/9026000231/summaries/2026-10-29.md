## In brief

Mario Delaine asks the Court to decide whether his prior battery conviction counts toward a mandatory 15-year prison term under a federal gun law.

## What happened

Mario Delaine pleaded guilty to illegally possessing a firearm as a felon. Because he had three prior convictions, including a Florida felony battery conviction, the district court applied the Armed Career Criminal Act's mandatory 15-year minimum sentence instead of a shorter guideline range. Delaine argued that his battery conviction should not count because the Florida law requires only an intentional touch, not an intent or awareness that the touch would cause injury. The Sixth Circuit disagreed and affirmed his 188-month sentence, holding that the law's requirement of an intentional act was enough. The court of appeals later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The law that triggers the mandatory minimum sentence covers past crimes that involved using force against another person with a guilty state of mind greater than recklessness. The Court is asked whether that state-of-mind requirement must cover the resulting injury, or whether it is enough that the underlying physical contact itself was intentional. Appeals courts disagree: some say a defendant must have intended or known about the harm the force would cause; others, including the court that decided Delaine's case, say it is enough that the defendant meant to make the physical contact, regardless of whether he intended or anticipated any resulting injury.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference, and the Court asked for a response from the government, which was initially waived. Briefing deadlines have since been extended, most recently to allow the government more time to respond; the case has not yet been scheduled for a conference decision on whether to grant review.

## What each outcome would mean

Delaine, who lost in the Sixth Circuit, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal question itself, which would not by itself determine who wins. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's ruling stands, leaving in place Delaine's 188-month sentence and the rule that an intentional touch causing injury can count as a violent felony in that circuit, without deciding the question for other circuits.
