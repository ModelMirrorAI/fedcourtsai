## In brief

Mario Delaine asks the Court to decide whether one of his past convictions counted as a "violent felony" that triggered a mandatory 15-year prison sentence.

## What happened

Delaine pleaded guilty to illegally possessing a firearm as a felon. Because prosecutors said he had three prior convictions for "violent felonies," the district court sentenced him under the Armed Career Criminal Act, which sets a 15-year mandatory minimum sentence instead of a much lower guideline range. Delaine argued that one of those convictions, a Florida felony battery conviction, should not count because the law only requires an intentional touch, not any intent or awareness that the touch would cause injury. The Sixth Circuit disagreed and upheld his 188-month sentence, siding with two other appeals courts; two different appeals courts have reached the opposite conclusion on similar state laws.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide what level of intent a law must require before a crime counts as a "violent felony" under this sentencing law. Specifically, is it enough that a person intentionally touched someone, even without meaning or knowing that injury would result, or must the law require that the person intended or was aware that the touch could cause injury?

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, after which the Court asked the government to respond. The government's response deadline has since been extended twice, most recently to December 3, 2026, so the Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Delaine, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide which interpretation of the sentencing law is correct, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, leaving Delaine's 188-month sentence and the lower court's interpretation in place. Either way, the decision so far affects only Delaine's case, though the legal question could matter in similar cases nationwide.
