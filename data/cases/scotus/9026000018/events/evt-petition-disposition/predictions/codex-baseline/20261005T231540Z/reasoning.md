Several features argue for review. Louisiana challenges a repeat problem under a fixed statutory expiration period rather than an isolated factual error. The petition describes three unsuccessful efforts to secure review and an 11–6 denial of rehearing en banc, with a substantive dissent (petition pp. 1–3, 16–17 and 25–29). The Court requested a response after an initial waiver, an affirmative sign of attention rather than merely routine briefing (snapshot, August 3 entry). The petition's conflict theory has force: Turner v. Rogers, 564 U.S. 431, 439–440 (2011), describes both an insufficient litigation window and a reasonable expectation of recurrence, and treats a 12-month period as too short to reach Supreme Court review. I checked that passage in CourtListener opinion 9441801; it supports the petition's duration concern without resolving this case's vehicle or recurrence questions.

The stronger downward adjustment is vehicle-specific. **Both sides acknowledge that Louisiana already won final judgment in the district court.** Louisiana argues that this does not answer the distinct PLRA preliminary-relief issues (petition pp. 28–29). Respondents argue that the final judgment independently superseded the preliminary injunctions and removed any live interest in reviewing them (opposition pp. 12–14). I find that a substantial obstacle to selecting this case even if the Fifth Circuit's duration analysis warrants correction. The existence of final judgment is common ground; its legal effect remains contested, and I do not treat advocacy as an adjudicated holding.

A second, partly independent obstacle is recurrence. Respondents describe materially different successive injunctions, changes to Louisiana's heat policies, the absence of a new preliminary-relief request, and the completed merits trial (opposition pp. 18–20). That makes the likelihood of the same challenged action recurring debatable even if 90 days cannot support full appellate review. A favorable answer to either presented question might therefore fail to change the result. The petition's repeated-injunction narrative keeps this risk from being conclusive, but cannot erase it.

The asserted split is also less clean than the questions alone suggest. Respondents identify published PLRA decisions taking the same mootness approach and distinguish most of the petition's shorter-duration cases as challenges to underlying conduct rather than review of interlocutory orders. They acknowledge closer non-PLRA injunction/stay analogues but characterize the conflict as shallow (opposition pp. 20–23). They additionally argue that the Fifth Circuit did not squarely decide the level-of-review issue (pp. 17–18). I treat these as contested vehicle and split weaknesses, not as independently verified proof that no conflict exists.

Those obstacles outweigh the upward pressure from the response request, state-petitioner status, and en banc disagreement. My **0.12 P(any grant)** is below the 0.2363 state-class anchor but materially above a near-zero dismissal of the petition's legal argument. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted estimate. The main uncertainty is whether the Court regards the mootness rule as sufficiently consequential and plainly erroneous to warrant corrective summary action despite the final judgment.
