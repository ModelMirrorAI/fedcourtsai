## Why 28%, rather than 5% or a likely grant

Three record-specific features support a substantial upward adjustment. First, the parties acknowledge an actual disagreement over the categorical permissibility of service awards. The petition, printed pages 7–18, describes the Eleventh Circuit's prohibition and contrary holdings in five circuits. Moses's opposition, pages 5–6, contests the disagreement's significance but acknowledges its existence. A numerical imbalance does not logically eliminate the conflicting treatment of the same practice. I treat the opposition's insistence that a split must be evenly balanced as advocacy, not a disqualifying rule.

Second, the Court requested a response after both respondents initially waived. This is a case-specific signal of attention beyond routine first distribution, even though it does not alter the harness's frozen band and does not establish four votes to grant. Third, the issue is a recurring national procedural question, not merely a dispute about the size of one settlement. The additional amicus filing modestly reinforces the presence of institutional interest, without establishing the brief's direction or persuasive quality.

The vehicle is mixed, rather than plainly defective. The petition, pages 14 and 18–19, explains that the first appeal vacated the earlier settlement, whereas the second appeal sustained the revised settlement and award with no further issues to litigate below. Moses's opposition, pages 6–9, emphasizes the unpublished summary order, the uncontested reasonableness of the $5,000 award, and finality. The Times' separate opposition, pages 2–3, takes no position on the question presented and principally requests finality after lengthy proceedings. I give limited weight to the claim that failure to contest excessiveness defeats a categorical legal challenge: the absence of that factual dispute can help isolate the legal question. Similarly, the opposition's description of the case as interlocutory-in-effect is not proof of an actual pending remand. Nevertheless, the summary-order vehicle, modest award, entrenched majority practice, and availability of other vehicles leave denial more likely than review. The 28% estimate is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted rate for response-requested class-action petitions.
