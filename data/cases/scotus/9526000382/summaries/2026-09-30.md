## In brief

Samuel Strulovitch asked the Court to pause a New York court order restricting his participation in a religious court proceeding; the Court denied the request.

## What happened

Strulovitch and Moshe Bain are embroiled in a commercial dispute over ownership of a nursing home. Strulovitch, an Orthodox Jew, brought the dispute to a beis din (a rabbinical court), which later issued a "seruv," a religious censure, against Bain for seeking a court order that blocked Strulovitch from pursuing the matter there. A New York trial court then preliminarily barred Strulovitch from pursuing any proceeding, religious or civil, outside that court, and ordered him to work to have the seruv withdrawn. Strulovitch appealed, but the state appellate court did not rule on his request to pause that order while the appeal proceeded, so he asked the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

Strulovitch asked a single justice to pause the state court's order while his state court appeal continues, arguing it violates his religious freedom by stopping him from pursuing his case before a religious tribunal and by forcing him to seek withdrawal of a religious ruling he agrees with. Bain opposed the request, and the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty filed a supporting brief.

## Where it stands

Justice Sotomayor asked Bain to respond to the application, received that response, and then denied the stay request, without prejudice to Strulovitch seeking relief again if necessary once he has exhausted his remedies in the state courts.

## What each outcome would mean

Strulovitch, as the applicant, sought a pause of the state court order while his appeal continues. Had the pause been granted, the restrictions on him would have been lifted temporarily during the appeal. Because it was denied, the state court order restricting his participation in the religious proceeding, and directing him to seek withdrawal of the seruv, remains in effect for now. The denial does not decide the underlying constitutional questions or the appeal itself, and the order notes Strulovitch may seek relief again later if state court remedies are exhausted.
