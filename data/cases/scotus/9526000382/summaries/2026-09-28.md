## In brief

Samuel Strulovitch asks the Court to pause a New York order barring him from pursuing a religious court case and requiring him to seek withdrawal of a religious ruling.

## What happened

Strulovitch and Moshe Bain dispute ownership of a nursing home. Strulovitch, an Orthodox Jew, brought the dispute to a rabbinical court, or beis din, consistent with his faith. That tribunal issued a "seruv," a religious censure, against Bain for having blocked Strulovitch from pursuing the religious proceeding. A New York trial court then ordered Strulovitch to stop pursuing any forum but the civil courts and to work to get the seruv withdrawn. Strulovitch says New York's appellate courts have not ruled on his request to pause that order despite months of asking.

## What the Court is being asked

Strulovitch is asking a single justice to temporarily block the state court's order while his appeal continues. He argues the order improperly stops him from taking a religious dispute to a faith tribunal and improperly forces him to ask that tribunal to reverse a religious ruling he agrees with, both of which he says violate his right to freely exercise his religion.

## Where it stands

The application was submitted to Justice Sotomayor, who asked Bain to respond by September 28, 2026. An outside group, the Becket Fund for Religious Liberty, has since filed a brief supporting Strulovitch. The docket shows no ruling yet on the stay request.

## What each outcome would mean

Strulovitch, the applicant, is asking the Court to pause the state court order while his appeal proceeds. If the stay is granted, Strulovitch would be free, for now, to continue pursuing the matter before the beis din and would not have to seek withdrawal of the seruv, while his state-court appeal continues. If the stay is denied, the trial court's order would remain in effect, keeping Strulovitch barred from the religious proceeding and requiring him to seek the seruv's withdrawal, while the underlying civil case and his appeal continue. Either way, the underlying ownership dispute would still need to be resolved in the lower courts.
