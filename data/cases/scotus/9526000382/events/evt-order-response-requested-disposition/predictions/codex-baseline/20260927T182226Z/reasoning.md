## Why 40%, rather than the pooled 10.47%

The response request is an affirmative attention signal, although not a commitment to grant relief. The application also presents more than an ordinary complaint about an adverse commercial ruling. Its opening and pages 7–11 describe an April order barring further proceedings before another tribunal and directing steps to withdraw a rabbinical censure. Pages 20–24 develop a potentially substantial distinction between declining civil enforcement of religious arbitration and prohibiting religious participation or compelling a request to reverse a religious determination. The latter command is the strongest basis for intervention. The applicants seek to suspend two provisions, not stop the ownership litigation or compel Bain to arbitrate (application pp. 6, 20–21, 26). If those descriptions hold up, the continuing-injury argument and the possibility of preserving secular litigation materially favor a stay.

The major counterweight is posture. On the applicants' own account, a single Appellate Division justice denied emergency relief in May, but the panel has not decided the fully briefed stay motion or the underlying appeal. Their jurisdictional discussion relies on potential appellate jurisdiction, practical finality, and prolonged inaction rather than an ordinary final state judgment (pp. 2–6, 9–13). Their effort to distinguish Yeshiva University on page 11 underscores that procedural obstacle; I use that distinction as their argument, not as an independently verified description of the comparator's entire history. Months of alleged ongoing harm strengthen their delay argument, but the application identifies no imminent execution, removal, or fixed contempt deadline that necessarily forecloses later relief. Further state appellate action remains a plausible alternative to Supreme Court intervention.

The equity case is also one-sided. The application acknowledges that Bain did not participate in the earlier arbitration and describes religious censure following his resort to civil court (pp. 6–9, 25). His interests in access to a civil forum and avoiding pressure cannot be assumed nonexistent merely because the censure lacks direct civil enforcement. I have no response with which to test the characterization of the injunction or the claimed exhaustion of state remedies. Nor do I infer that a party-specific judicial injunction necessarily fails neutrality analysis merely because the applicants call it a rule directed at one person.

For general stay principles, I checked Nken v. Holder, 556 U.S. 418, 434 (2009), through CourtListener's opinion text: likelihood of success, irreparable injury, harm to other parties, and public interest guide the inquiry. Nken involved a different procedural setting and does not by itself establish jurisdiction to intervene here. The application invokes it at pages 12–13; its jurisdictional theory must still do separate work.

Balancing these considerations yields **40% for an unqualified grant**, with denial the modal label. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted response-requested estimate. Concern about the religious-withdrawal directive could produce narrower relief rather than a full grant; an expressly mixed grant/denial stays in the complement of my number. I would move upward if the missing orders and response establish an unconditional compelled religious recantation with no viable prompt state remedy, and downward if they reveal a narrower, neutral litigation-control order or a readily available state avenue.
