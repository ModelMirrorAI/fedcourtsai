# Forecast: Cummins v. Illinois, No. 25-1030 (evt-petition-disposition)

**Bottom line.** The Court denies certiorari, most likely on the October 5, 2026 order list following the September 28 long conference, without a separate writing.

**Relists.** The docket shows two distribution entries: the March 4 distribution for the March 20 conference, which the March 16 call for a response pre-empted before the petition was ever considered, and the June 24 redistribution for the September 28 long conference. So the petition is at its first real conference, never yet relisted. I expect no further distribution: a denial straight off the long conference is the modal path. The minority path to one or two relists runs through a Justice drafting a statement or dissent respecting denial, or the Court weighing a GVR in light of Case v. Montana; I put roughly a one-in-four chance on at least one more distribution.

**CVSG.** None. This is a state criminal prosecution with no federal party and no federal statute, and the United States already stated its view of the emergency-aid standard as amicus in Case v. Montana last Term. A call for the Solicitor General's views is very unlikely.

**Which question, if taken.** If the Court took the case it would not adopt the petition's question as written, which is framed around the absence of a "fracas" and of any report of injury. It would reformulate to whether an occupant's failure to respond to prolonged knocking, with no report or observation of injury, can by itself furnish an objectively reasonable basis for a warrantless emergency-aid entry into a home, the welfare-check question Case v. Montana left open. A threshold jurisdiction question under 28 U.S.C. 1257 would have to be resolved first, because the state appellate court's reversal of a suppression order is interlocutory and no Cox Broadcasting exception plausibly applies.

**Summary disposition versus plenary review.** Conditional on any grant, a summary route is likelier than plenary review. The appellate court twice glossed the standard as "approximating probable cause," and Case v. Montana, decided January 14, 2026, after every state-court ruling here, rejected a probable-cause gloss. That is the shape of a GVR "in light of Case," and the State's brief in opposition spends three pages arguing against exactly that. A per curiam summary reversal on these facts, the police waiting 76 minutes with no injury report and no ambulance called, is the second summary path. Plenary review of a six-page error-correction petition with no alleged split, in an interlocutory posture, is the least likely grant path.

**Dissent or statement respecting denial.** Unlikely but live: the response was called for after the State waived, which means at least one chambers took an interest, and Justices Sotomayor and Gorsuch each wrote separately in Case v. Montana about the exception's limits. If a writing comes it would most plausibly be a short statement from Justice Sotomayor or Justice Gorsuch noting the finality bar while flagging the breadth of the emergency-aid rationale the Illinois court applied. I put the chance of any separate writing at about one in eight, conditional on denial.

**If granted (conditional only).** The likely ground would be that Brigham City's objective-reasonableness standard, applied "on its own terms" per Case, still demands some affirmative indication of serious injury or imminent threat, and that unanswered knocking plus a noise complaint does not supply one; the alternative outcome is a GVR directing the Illinois court to reapply the standard without the probable-cause gloss.
