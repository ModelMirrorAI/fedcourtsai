## Why 4%, rather than 17.2%

**Finality is the strongest negative.** The petition itself describes an interlocutory state appeal reversing suppression (petition pp. 1–2). Its jurisdiction discussion invokes section 1257(a) and timeliness but supplies no developed exception to finality. The opposition explains that Cummins has not been tried, convicted, or sentenced and argues that none of the four Cox finality exceptions applies (BIO pp. 7–10). It specifically preserves the possibilities of acquittal and later review following conviction. I regard this as a substantial threshold obstacle, not merely the State's preference that review wait. This conclusion is based on the supplied adversarial briefs; independent precedent verification was unavailable.

**The petition asks for application-level correction.** It expressly accepts the Brigham City/Case rule and the appellate court's factual recitation, challenging their application instead (petition pp. 3–5). It identifies no competing appellate rule. Its strongest point is the absence of a reported injury or visible struggle together with the 76-minute delay before entry. Those facts plausibly distinguish its situation from a readily apparent emergency. But the BIO answers with cumulative circumstantial evidence: an ajar door, lights, loud music, a barking dog, a parked vehicle, persistent nonresponse, and information about drug use (BIO pp. 2–6, 11–14). The appendix discusses the objective standard and the difference between inability and unwillingness to respond (App. 15a–20a). The dispute therefore looks substantially fact-bound even if the search's justification is debatable.

**Case offers a limited alternative route, not a strong plenary hook.** Both supplied briefs discuss Case v. Montana as already decided before the petition. I use their accounts of that precedent, not independent knowledge of its outcome or wording. The BIO acknowledges that the Illinois opinion predates Case and contains probable-cause language, but argues that the operative rule was repeatedly objective reasonable grounds, that one challenged prong was conceded, and that a more demanding probable-cause test would not prejudice petitioner (BIO pp. 14–16). That is a substantial answer to a GVR. Nonetheless, the intervening precedent relative to the lower decision keeps a small GVR possibility alive. The availability of ordinary postjudgment litigation weighs against immediate intervention on either route.

**The response request supplies the main positive signal.** The Court asked for a response after Illinois waived one. I retain nontrivial grant probability because that is affirmative attention and because the reply is unread. But the second distribution followed the response request and completed briefing: it does not establish that the Court actually considered the petition at the earlier scheduled conference and then relisted it on the merits. I retain the harness's count and band while discounting an interpretation of those entries as repeated substantive consideration. The summer interval is not itself evidence of a hold for a companion case.
