## What happened

Police in Kane County, Illinois, went to Christopher Cummins's house after midnight on a noise complaint. They found loud music, a barking dog, lights on, one door ajar, and his car in the driveway, but no one answered repeated knocking or phone calls. After learning Cummins had a prior drug conviction, officers entered without a warrant, more than an hour after arriving, to check on his safety. They found him asleep and later obtained a warrant to search the house, turning up firearms. A trial court suppressed that evidence, ruling the entry unjustified. The Illinois Appellate Court reversed, holding officers reasonably believed someone inside might need emergency help. The Illinois Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

Cummins argues the state appellate court stretched the rule allowing police to enter a home without a warrant when they reasonably believe someone inside needs urgent help, applying it even though officers saw no violence and had no specific report that anyone was hurt. He says this goes beyond what the Supreme Court's past decisions on emergency home entries allow. Illinois responds that the appellate court correctly applied that standard to the specific facts here, that the case is not properly before the Court yet because it is not a final judgment, and that any dispute is just about applying settled law to particular facts rather than a genuine legal disagreement needing correction.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for September 28, 2026, after full briefing including Illinois's brief opposing review and Cummins's reply. The Court has not yet acted on the petition.
