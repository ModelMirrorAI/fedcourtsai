## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a man's challenge to police entering his home without a warrant to check on his safety.

## What happened

Police responding to a noise complaint at Christopher Cummins's home in the middle of the night found a door ajar, loud music, a barking dog, and lights on, but got no response after repeated knocking and phone calls. Learning Cummins had a prior drug conviction, officers entered without a warrant to check on his safety. He was later charged with firearm offenses. A trial court suppressed the evidence from the search, but the Illinois Appellate Court reversed, finding the officers reasonably believed an emergency existed. The Illinois Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether the state appellate court correctly applied the Supreme Court's existing rule allowing police to enter a home without a warrant when they reasonably believe someone inside needs emergency help. Cummins argues the officers had no actual indication that anyone was hurt or that an emergency was underway, so the rule should not have excused the warrantless entry here.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court asked Illinois to respond after initially receiving a waiver of response. Illinois filed a brief opposing review, Cummins replied, and after a later conference the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Cummins, who lost in the Illinois Appellate Court, asked the Supreme Court to take the case. Because the petition was denied, the Illinois Appellate Court's ruling stands: the warrantless entry was lawful, the suppression order is reversed, and the firearms evidence may be used against Cummins as his case proceeds in Illinois courts. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with that ruling; it simply leaves the lower court's decision in place.
