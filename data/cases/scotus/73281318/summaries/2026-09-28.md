## In brief

Cummins asks the Court to review a state appellate ruling that police lawfully entered his home without a warrant to check on his safety.

## What happened

Police went to Christopher Cummins's home after a middle-of-the-night noise complaint, found a car in the driveway, a door ajar, loud music, a barking dog and lights on, and could not reach anyone inside despite repeated knocking and phone calls. They eventually entered without a warrant, found Cummins asleep, and later obtained a search warrant that turned up firearms evidence. Cummins was charged with firearm offenses. A trial court suppressed the evidence, ruling the entry unjustified. Illinois's intermediate appellate court reversed, holding officers reasonably believed someone inside might need emergency help. The Illinois Supreme Court declined further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the state appellate court correctly applied the rule allowing police to enter a home without a warrant when they reasonably believe someone inside needs emergency help, given that the officers here saw no violent confrontation and had no specific report that anyone was hurt or in danger.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for September 28, 2026, after briefing that included Illinois's brief in opposition and Cummins's reply; the docket shows no decision yet on whether to grant or deny review.

## What each outcome would mean

Cummins, who lost in the state appellate court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the legal question itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Illinois appellate court's ruling allowing the warrantless entry stands, and the suppression order stays reversed, leaving the firearms evidence available for use in Cummins's prosecution. Illinois's brief also argues the Court lacks jurisdiction because the ruling below is not a final judgment, which could provide another basis for denial.
