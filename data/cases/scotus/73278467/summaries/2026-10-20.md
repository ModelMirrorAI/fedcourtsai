## In brief

El Paso County officials ask the Court to review a ruling that let a transgender detainee's lawsuit over jail housing, commissary, and search policies proceed.

## What happened

Darlene Griffith, a transgender woman, was held as a pretrial detainee at the El Paso County Jail and placed in male housing under a policy that assigns detainees by genitalia. She was strip-searched partly by a male deputy, who she says behaved abusively, and was denied commissary items like underwear and lipstick given to cisgender women. She sued jail officials, claiming violations of equal protection and her Fourth and Fourteenth Amendment rights. A federal district court dismissed her claims. The Tenth Circuit reversed in part, letting some claims proceed, including an equal protection claim and a claim over the cross-gender search; a dissenting judge disagreed. The full appeals court declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions. First, whether courts must use a deferential standard that asks only if a prison policy is reasonably related to legitimate security needs when reviewing a transgender inmate's challenge to sex-based housing and related policies, rather than a more searching standard normally applied to sex discrimination claims. Second, whether jail officials violate the Constitution by having an officer of the detainee's birth sex, rather than their gender identity, conduct a strip search.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court then asked the Solicitor General for the federal government's views on whether to take the case. The government's brief recommends that the Court grant the petition, set aside the Tenth Circuit's decision, and send the case back for reconsideration in light of two of the Court's more recent rulings, rather than deciding the questions presented outright. The case has been distributed for a further conference since that brief was filed.

## What each outcome would mean

Roybal and the other jail officials are asking the Court to act. If the Court denies review, the Tenth Circuit's decision stands, and Griffith's claims proceed in the lower courts under the more searching standard that court applied. If the Court grants review and later rules for the officials, it would likely restore the more deferential standard and could end some or all of Griffith's claims. If the Court instead grants review, vacates the decision, and sends the case back—as the federal government recommends—the Tenth Circuit would reconsider the case in light of newer Supreme Court rulings without the Court itself deciding who is right.
