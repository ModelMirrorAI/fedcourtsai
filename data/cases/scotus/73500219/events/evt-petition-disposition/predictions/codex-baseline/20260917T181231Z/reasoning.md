## Why 6%, rather than the anchor alone

The petition presents a substantial, reasonably specific alleged conflict about what prejudice means when jurors receive extraneous information. Its central contention distinguishes damage to the deliberative process from an appellate conclusion that the verdict would have been the same. The case involves a published Sixth Circuit decision, an actual post-verdict investigation, and a new-trial order reversed on government appeal; those features make the legal question more concrete than an unsupported allegation of juror exposure. Sources: petition pp. 1–5, 14–17, 25–31; Appendix A pp. 21a–26a.

I independently checked the petition's strongest contrasting authority, **United States v. Dutkel, 192 F.3d 893, 899 (9th Cir. 1999)**, through CourtListener's citation search and an opinion-text search. Dutkel supports a deliberation-focused inquiry without requiring proof that the verdict would have changed. However, the retrieved opinion expressly distinguishes jury tampering from other misconduct; its facts concern an intentional scheme involving a juror. Applying that rule to inadvertently supplied exhibits is an additional step. This supports taking the split seriously without treating the petition's entire circuit grouping as independently established. I did not verify every cited authority.

Several features limit the upward adjustment. Most importantly, the appended Sixth Circuit opinion is more qualified than the petition's strongest characterization. It assumed the most demanding harmlessness burden, recited a reasonable-possibility-of-contribution test, and evaluated both the extraneous statements' limited relevance to Maund and the other evidence. The district court had relied on structural error, not made its own harmlessness determination. Thus the Court could see this as a dispute over applying harmless-error principles and distinguishing types of intrusion, rather than a clean conflict requiring resolution here. Sources: Appendix A pp. 21a–23a, 24a–26a; petition pp. 15–17, 35–38. These are vehicle assessments, not acceptance of either side's ultimate merits position.

The snapshot also shows a June 8 waiver by the United States and no later response request, opposition, amicus entry, or additional distribution. I treat that as modest negative attention evidence, not as a government concession or proof that no Justice is interested. The conference remains in the future: the long summer interval does not itself constitute a relist or repeated consideration. The docket's related-case numbers alone do not establish a lead-case hold. Together these considerations offset much of the split-based uplift, leaving **P(any grant) = 0.06**, slightly above the 5.12% matched anchor.

It is judgmental, not an estimated transition rate from the terminal statpack buckets. **0.001** for a new CVSG reflects the distinction between soliciting an existing federal respondent's brief and requesting outside views. **0.20** for a summary route is conditional on grant: the petition expressly seeks summary reversal in the alternative, but the competing prejudice formulations make plenary resolution more plausible. **0.035** for a separate denial writing is conditional on denial and describes only its existence. No cert-stage votes or merits judgment are forecast.

The **0.45** stakes score reflects the potential national reach of a rule about jury contamination and new trials, tempered by the bounded procedural issue. It is not a second estimate of grant likelihood or a claim about media attention.
