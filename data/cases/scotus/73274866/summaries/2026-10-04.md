## In brief

Two former university employees ask the Supreme Court to decide whether Title IX lets them sue their employers for sex discrimination in their jobs.

## What happened

Thomas Crowther, a former art professor at Augusta University, and MaChelle Joseph, the former head women's basketball coach at Georgia Tech, each sued Georgia's Board of Regents and related defendants, claiming sex discrimination and retaliation in their employment under Title IX, the federal law barring sex discrimination at schools that receive federal funds. Crowther was dismissed after a Title IX misconduct investigation; Joseph was fired following complaints about her treatment of players and staff. The Eleventh Circuit, reviewing the two cases together, ruled that Title IX does not let employees sue for employment discrimination at all, rejecting Joseph's claim and reversing a lower court ruling that had allowed Crowther's claim to proceed. Other appeals courts had allowed such suits.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal education law that bars sex discrimination by schools receiving federal money lets employees, not just students, bring their own lawsuits for sex discrimination in the terms of their jobs, or whether that remedy belongs only to Title VII, the separate federal law covering workplace discrimination.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition on May 18, 2026. The case was briefed on the merits, argument was set for November 30, 2026, and the docket shows the case was circulated to the justices on September 18, 2026, after the final reply brief was filed; no decision has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Crowther and Joseph, as the employees who petitioned the Court, are asking the justices to revive their Title IX claims. If the Court rules in their favor, employees at federally funded schools would be able to sue directly under Title IX for sex discrimination in their employment, and the case would return to the lower courts to proceed on that basis. If the Court rules for the Board of Regents and the other school defendants, Title IX would not provide that remedy, leaving employees to pursue only Title VII's separate discrimination process, and the Eleventh Circuit's rejection of Crowther's and Joseph's Title IX claims would stand.
