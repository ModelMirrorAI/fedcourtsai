## In brief

Two former university employees ask the Court to decide whether Title IX lets employees sue for sex discrimination in their jobs, rather than only under Title VII.

## What happened

MaChelle Joseph, a women's basketball coach, and Thomas Crowther, an art professor, both lost their jobs at Georgia public universities and sued under Title IX, the law barring sex discrimination at federally funded schools. Joseph alleged her program got fewer resources than the men's team and that she was fired for complaining; Crowther alleged bias in a sexual-harassment investigation that led to his non-renewal. The district courts split on whether Title IX allows such employment claims, given that Title VII already covers workplace discrimination. The Eleventh Circuit held that Title IX does not provide a private right to sue over employment discrimination, dismissing Joseph's claim and ordering dismissal of Crowther's.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law that bars schools receiving federal money from discriminating "on the basis of sex" lets employees, not just students, sue directly under that law for job-related discrimination, or whether only the separate federal employment-discrimination law provides that remedy for workers.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, received briefing including a Solicitor General brief supporting the universities, and heard argument on November 30, 2026; the case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Crowther and Joseph, as petitioners, are asking the Court to reverse the Eleventh Circuit's ruling against their Title IX employment claims. If the Court affirms, the Eleventh Circuit's decision stands: employees like Crowther and Joseph could not use Title IX to sue over job discrimination, leaving Title VII as their only avenue. If the Court reverses, employees of federally funded schools could sue directly under Title IX for workplace sex discrimination, and the case would return to the lower courts to address Crowther's and Joseph's claims on that basis. The Court could also vacate and send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
