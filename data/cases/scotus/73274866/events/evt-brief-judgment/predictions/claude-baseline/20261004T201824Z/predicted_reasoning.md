# Forecast: what the Court will do with the judgment below

*Crowther v. Board of Regents of the University System of Georgia*, No. 25-183,
on certiorari to the Eleventh Circuit. Question: whether Title IX provides
employees of federally funded educational institutions a private right of
action to sue for sex discrimination in employment. The Eleventh Circuit
(W. Pryor, C.J., joined by Luck and Ed Carnes, JJ.) held that it does not;
rehearing en banc was denied over a five-judge dissent.

**Judgment class: `affirmed`.** The Court will hold that Title IX's implied
private right of action does not extend to an employee's claim of sex
discrimination in their own employment, and will affirm the Eleventh Circuit.
The alternative the forecast gives real weight to is a straight `reversed`
(the Court holds the Cannon right of action, as ratified by Congress in 1986,
reaches any intentional sex discrimination the statute prohibits, employment
included, and remands for the Title IX claims to proceed). No other label is
live: there is no partial-affirmance seam in a single yes/no question
presented, no vehicle problem the parties have raised that would support a
DIG, and no recusal that would produce an equally divided Court.

**Vote lineup: 6–3 to affirm.** Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh and
Barrett in the majority; Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson in dissent. The two
Justices most likely to break from that lineup are Gorsuch (a Bostock-style
reading of "no person ... shall ... be subjected to discrimination" plus the
express text of 42 U.S.C. 2000d-7 referring to suits "for a violation" of
Title IX) and Barrett, and a 5–4 affirmance or a 5–4 reversal with one of them
crossing is the most plausible departure. Thomas and Alito are near-certain
affirmance votes; the three Democratic appointees are near-certain reversal
votes. Expect at least one separate writing on the majority side — most likely
a Thomas or Gorsuch concurrence saying Cannon itself should be reconsidered
or confined, and that Jackson v. Birmingham was wrongly decided — and a single
principal dissent, probably from Kagan or Jackson, built on North Haven, the
1986 ratification, and the eight-circuit consensus. (Authorship and writing
roles are unscored context; the lineup and the judgment class are the
checkable claims.)

**Ground.** The majority will frame the question as whether to *extend* a
judicially implied cause of action to a new class of plaintiffs and claims, not
whether the plaintiffs fall within the statute's substantive prohibition. It
will accept North Haven v. Bell as settling that section 901 covers
employment discrimination and that the government may terminate funding for
it, and then hold, following Sandoval, Gonzaga, Gebser and Virginia
Bankshares, that the scope of an implied private remedy is a distinct question
of congressional remedial intent that the breadth of the prohibition does not
answer. The decisive structural point will be Title VII: Congress extended
Title VII's "integrated, multistep" enforcement scheme to educational
employers in March 1972, three months before enacting Title IX, so inferring a
parallel Title IX damages remedy that bypasses the EEOC charge, the limitations
period and the damages caps would attribute to the same Congress an
inexplicable about-face. Great American v. Novotny and Brown v. GSA will supply
the analogy; Johnson v. Railway Express will be distinguished as resting on
Title VII's own legislative history. Jackson v. Birmingham will be read as a
retaliation holding tied to protecting Title IX's enforcement scheme, where the
plaintiff had no Title VII remedy, and left standing. The ratification argument
(2000d-7, the Civil Rights Restoration Act) will be rejected on the ground that
Congress can ratify only a judicial construction that existed, and no decision
of this Court before 1986 recognized an employee's Title IX
employment-discrimination suit. The Spending Clause clear-notice point will be
mentioned as reinforcing but not relied on as an independent ground, exactly
as the SG's brief suggests.

**Breadth.** Categorical. The holding will cover every employee of a funding
recipient suing over discrimination in their own employment, regardless of
whether Title VII actually reaches that employee — the student-employee,
small-institution and time-barred-Title-VII hypotheticals the petitioners and
their amici press will be answered with "that is for Congress." The Court will
not announce a rule for Title VI or the Rehabilitation Act (the respondents
expressly distinguish the Rehabilitation Act's 1992 incorporation of
employment standards), and it will say in terms that Cannon's student right of
action, Jackson's retaliation claim, and the funding-termination remedy for
employment discrimination are untouched.

**If the forecast is wrong and the judgment is disturbed**, the likely shape is
a `reversed` opinion by Gorsuch, Kagan or Barrett holding that Title IX's
private right of action, ratified by Congress against the backdrop of North
Haven, enforces the statute's prohibition on intentional sex discrimination
wherever that prohibition reaches (Jackson's framing), that overlapping
remedies are the norm rather than the exception (Johnson, Fitzgerald, CBOCS),
and that the Court will not "carve out" a class of victims the text includes.
That outcome would most plausibly be 5–4 or 6–3 with Gorsuch and/or Barrett
(and possibly Roberts) joining the three Democratic appointees.

**Timing.** Argument is set for November 30, 2026. Expect the decision between
late April and the end of June 2027; a 6–3 opinion with separate writings on a
contested implied-right question is a late-Term case, not a winter one.
