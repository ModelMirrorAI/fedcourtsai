# Rationale

I assign a 0.18 probability that the Supreme Court disturbs the Eleventh Circuit's judgment and predict an affirmance. The committed merits baseline points the other way: over the ten grant Terms before OT2025, the statpack has usable rows for OT2017-OT2024 and records 359 disturbed judgments among 515 parsed judgments, or 69.7%. Those rows show 515 parsed judgments among 557 grants, with 57 additional judgments excluded by the cert-order pool guard. The sample clears the 30-judgment floor. I adjust sharply downward from that baseline because the case-specific directional evidence is unusually strong.

The question presented asks whether employees of federally funded educational institutions may use Title IX's private right of action for sex discrimination in employment. Petitioners have a substantial textual and precedential argument: Title IX protects "no person"; *North Haven* treats employees as covered by the substantive prohibition; *Cannon* recognized the private action; and *Jackson* permitted an employee to sue for retaliation. They also argue that Congress ratified *Cannon* without excluding employees. Those points keep reversal materially possible.

The stronger forecast signal is the alignment among the judgment below, the Solicitor General, and the Court's recent doctrine. The pre-grant Solicitor General brief recommended granting certiorari but argued that the Eleventh Circuit was correct. It characterized the requested employee action as an extension of *Cannon*, relied on the modern clear-statement approach to implied rights, and treated Title VII's detailed express scheme as powerful evidence against a parallel Title IX remedy. The Court then granted review after receiving that recommendation. The respondents' brief likewise relies on *Medina*'s statement that the Court has retreated from *Cannon*'s expansive reasoning and on the Title VII/Title IX enactment sequence. Those premises fit the current six-Justice conservative majority particularly well.

The forecast is based on the provisioned petition, brief in opposition, questions presented, docket snapshot, and the three official pre-grant supplemental filings linked by the snapshot. The latest snapshot also shows that petitioners filed a merits brief on July 10, after the grant moment, but no merits-brief text was provisioned and I did not retrieve or rely on it. No respondent merits brief or oral-argument evidence informed this forecast.

My principal uncertainty is stare decisis characterization. Petitioners can plausibly persuade one or more members of the Court that applying *Cannon*, *North Haven*, and *Jackson* to an employee is not an extension at all, especially because later legislation ratified the private remedy. A petitioner victory might yield a vacatur or mixed judgment rather than a simple reversal because the consolidated Eleventh Circuit judgment contains other holdings. I nevertheless expect the Court to treat the private-remedy question as distinct from Title IX's substantive coverage and to affirm by approximately 6-3.
