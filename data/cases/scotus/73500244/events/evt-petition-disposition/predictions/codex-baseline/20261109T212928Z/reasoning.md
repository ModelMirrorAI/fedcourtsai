# What moves the forecast

The question presented is whether an automobile is per se an instrumentality of interstate commerce under 18 U.S.C. § 1201(a)(1). The petition's statement and reasons, especially printed pages 3–10, identify a potentially consequential disagreement between the Tenth Circuit's Chavarria decision and the Eleventh Circuit's Bryan decision. The issue can extend beyond kidnapping to other federal crimes with similar jurisdictional language. Two relists, two supportive amici, and a focused petition make this a materially stronger prospect than routine error correction.

The opposing briefs identify the central uncertainty rather than an undisputed square split. The BIO, printed pages 10–12, says Chavarria decided only whether an indictment's generic reference to a motor vehicle sufficed, leaving the narrower automobile category unresolved. Brown's reply, printed pages 1–3, answers that both cases actually involved ordinary SUVs and that Chavarria's reasoning rejected the other circuits' approach. A direct CourtListener MCP check of Chavarria, 140 F.4th 1257 (10th Cir. 2025), opinion 11071905, confirms its narrow concluding holding about generic motor vehicles and a deficient indictment. A second excerpt confirms its express distinction between cars and the broader vehicle category. Its reasoning creates substantial tension, but the government's distinction is rooted in the actual opinion, not merely an invented vehicle objection.

Brown's record is relatively clean on the principal question. The petition, BIO, and appendix agree that the case concerns a specifically identified Toyota Sequoia and that the appellate court rejected both the statutory and constitutional arguments. The provisioned `decision-below.txt` matches the December 31, 2025 judgment, docket 23-10870, party, and subject of appendix A. It is unpublished and applies Bryan as binding precedent, which reduces the need to take this particular vehicle. The pending McMichael petition is mentioned in the BIO and reply, but I did not retrieve its current posture or assume that it has been granted. It introduces some chance that another vehicle is preferred.

The government also objects that the petition's dominant-use rationale was not advanced below (BIO p. 10). The reply invokes the distinction between a preserved claim and a new supporting argument (pp. 7–8). I view that as a moderate complication, not a demonstrated waiver of the entire Commerce Clause claim. Neither side identifies an intervening decision requiring correction by GVR. These limitations keep denial more likely than review, despite the significant question.

My net upward adjustment from 34.55% to 41% reflects the focused federalism question and meaningful inter-circuit tension. It is deliberately modest: repeated distributions are already reflected in the anchor, the asserted split is qualified, and the government's opposition and the unpublished disposition favor restraint. A square, acknowledged split would warrant substantially more; treating this as a routine no-split petition would warrant less.
