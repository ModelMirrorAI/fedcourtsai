## Disposition and Summary-Disposition Route (`disposition`: 0.32; `summary-disposition-route`: 0.04)

The most likely ultimate disposition is **denial of certiorari** (`P(grant) = 0.32`, `P(deny) = 0.68`). Although the petition presents a square, recently deepened circuit split between the Tenth Circuit (*United States v. Chavarria*, 140 F.4th 1257 (10th Cir. 2025)) and five other circuits including the Eleventh Circuit (*United States v. Bryan*, 159 F.4th 1274 (11th Cir. 2025)) over whether an automobile is *per se* an "instrumentality of interstate commerce" under the federal Kidnapping Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1201(a)(1), and the Commerce Clause, the case suffers from a significant alternative-ground vehicle obstacle: the government also introduced trial evidence that the conspirators used cell phones during the kidnapping to coordinate the abduction and demand ransom, and the Eleventh Circuit noted in footnote 1 of its unpublished opinion that petitioner abandoned any challenge on appeal to the cell-phone instrumentality theory.

**Conditional on a grant**, plenary briefing and oral argument are overwhelmingly likelier than a summary disposition (`summary-disposition-route = 0.04`). There is no intervening Supreme Court decision warranting a grant, vacate, and remand (GVR), the government opposes relief, and the Court would not summarily reverse five courts of appeals on a substantial Commerce Clause and statutory-interpretation question without merits briefing and argument.

## Question Presented and Conditional Merits Path

If the Court does grant certiorari, it will take the single Question Presented as framed in the petition—whether an automobile is *per se* an instrumentality of interstate commerce under 18 U.S.C. § 1201(a)(1)—and leave any harmless-error or alternative-instrumentality questions regarding cell-phone evidence for the Eleventh Circuit to address on remand. If granted, the Court would likely apply the federalism and constitutional-avoidance principles of *United States v. Lopez*, 514 U.S. 549 (1995), *Jones v. United States*, 529 U.S. 848 (2000), and *Bond v. United States*, 572 U.S. 844 (2014), to hold that intrastate use of an automobile does not by itself satisfy § 1201(a)(1)'s "instrumentality of interstate or foreign commerce" element without a case-specific nexus to interstate commerce.
