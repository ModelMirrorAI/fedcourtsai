## In brief

A vaccine-injury claimant asks the Court to overturn lower-court rulings on her compensation claim, alleging fraud and collusion in the proceedings below.

## What happened

Jane Doe filed a claim under the National Vaccine Injury Compensation Program, arguing that vaccines she received caused, then aggravated, her multiple sclerosis. The United States Court of Federal Claims issued rulings on damages and other matters that Doe disputes; she says a special master and attorneys involved in her case colluded to suppress evidence, missed filing deadlines through no fault of her own, and improperly obtained and disclosed her protected military and veterans records. Doe, representing herself, appealed to the United States Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit, which ruled against her on December 9, 2025. She then asked the Supreme Court to take her case.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks the Court to decide whether a lower court's rulings against Doe can stand given her allegations of misconduct, evidence suppression, and missed deadlines that she says were caused by others, not her. It also asks whether those rulings can stand given her allegations that officials unlawfully obtained and used her protected personal records to decide her claim, even though she says that information was not needed under the program's rules.

## Where it stands

The government waived its right to respond to the petition. The case was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, and the docket shows a supplemental brief from Doe was filed and distributed around that time; no action on the petition has been recorded since.

## What each outcome would mean

Jane Doe is asking the Court to take her case. If the Court grants the petition, it would agree to hear the case in full and decide the questions raised, without yet deciding who is right. If the Court denies the petition, the Federal Circuit's December 2025 decision against Doe would remain in place, and her case would not proceed further at the Supreme Court. A denial would not mean the Court found the lower court's ruling correct, only that the ruling stands.
