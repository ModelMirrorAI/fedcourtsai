## In brief

Nexstar asks the Court to review whether a federal appeals court applied the wrong legal standard when upholding a labor board ruling against it.

## What happened

The National Labor Relations Board found that Nexstar's television station must recognize and bargain with a union after certifying producers as regular employees rather than supervisors, who are excluded from union protections. Nexstar argued the producers were supervisors and that the election process was flawed. The Second Circuit enforced the Board's order, applying what it called "considerable deference" to the Board's legal conclusions. Nexstar says this conflicts with an Eighth Circuit decision on similar producers and with a recent Supreme Court ruling ending judicial deference to agency legal interpretations, and notes the Second Circuit itself later described a different, non-deferential standard in another case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether courts reviewing labor board decisions must independently decide questions of law rather than deferring to the board's expertise, and whether the appeals court's deferential approach here conflicts with how other circuits, including one that addressed similar employees, have handled the same issue.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and the federal government, representing the labor board, waived its right to respond. The case was distributed for the Court's November 13, 2026 conference, where the justices will decide whether to take it.

## What each outcome would mean

Nexstar, which lost below, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the legal question itself, without deciding in advance who wins. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's decision enforcing the Board's order against Nexstar stands, and Nexstar must bargain with the union. The petition also raises the possibility that the Court could vacate the lower decision and send it back for reconsideration without a full hearing, which would set that ruling aside without deciding the case for either side.
