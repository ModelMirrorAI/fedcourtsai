## Why not near certainty

Washington's opposition, especially pp. 16–22 and 30–35, makes substantial certworthiness objections. Its argument is that CoreCivic and the other cited circuit cases prohibit control of federal operations, whereas this case imposes an ordinary wage obligation on a private employer. A ban on detention contracting is not the same law as a minimum wage. The provisioned detainee opposition likewise emphasizes that distinction, including CoreCivic's treatment of this very lower-court decision (pp. 12–17). I therefore treat the alleged split as contested doctrinal tension, not an established square conflict over an identical wage requirement.

There is also a serious limiting argument: the contract calls for compliance with applicable labor laws, federal reimbursement is capped rather than the contractor's payment itself, and ICE's standards require at least a dollar per day. The SG acknowledges that paying more is legally possible and advances a federal-decisionmaker/obstacle theory rather than literal impossibility (pp. 17–18). That makes preemption less automatic than the petition's broad rhetoric might suggest. The discrimination theory depends on the correct comparison between exempt public facilities and covered private facilities; the state supreme court's interpretation formally covers private state and federal contractors alike. These are reasons the Court could decline review despite the government's request.

For general legal context I checked *United States v. Washington*, 596 U.S. 832, 838–39 (2022), through CourtListener. It supports protection against discriminatory burdens on federal contractors, but also explains that neutral indirect cost increases do not alone violate immunity. That distinction supports both the petition's significance and respondents' nonfrivolous defense. I do not infer from the prior case that GEO necessarily wins, and a plausible merits reversal is not by itself a reason for certiorari.
