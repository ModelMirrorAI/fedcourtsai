## In brief

The federal government asks the Supreme Court to overturn a lower court injunction that blocked the ongoing East Wing renovation at the White House.

## What happened

After the National Park Service and related federal officials began demolishing and rebuilding the White House's East Wing, the National Trust for Historic Preservation sued, arguing the project lacked statutory authorization and would irreversibly harm historic and aesthetic interests. A district court preliminarily enjoined above-ground construction, while allowing security-related work to continue. The D.C. Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court then granted the government's request to stay that injunction while the government pursued this petition, finding the government likely to succeed on the merits. The government now asks the Court to take up the case itself.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the lower court was wrong to block construction of the new East Wing while the lawsuit continues. That involves whether the National Trust has the legal right to sue at all based on a member's objection to the building's appearance, whether federal law authorizes this kind of construction, and whether the balance of harms justified stopping the project.

## Where it stands

The petition for certiorari was filed November 5, 2026. The respondent's deadline to reply has been extended, at the respondent's request, from December 7, 2026 to January 6, 2027. The Court has not yet acted on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The National Park Service and other federal petitioners are asking the Court to reverse the lower court's injunction. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who prevails. If it denies the petition, the D.C. Circuit's decision upholding the injunction against the renovation stays in place, a result favoring the National Trust. The government has also asked the Court to summarily reverse or to grant, vacate, and remand the case without full briefing, which would set aside the injunction without a full merits decision for either side.
