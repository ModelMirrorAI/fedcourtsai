# Forecast: what the Court will do with No. 25-1333, Pitzka v. Wisconsin

**Disposition.** The petition will be denied, almost certainly on the order list
following the September 28, 2026 long conference (the October 5, 2026 list, or
the day after the conference if the Court releases a same-week list). A grant
of any kind is a roughly 1-in-100 event here.

**Relists.** The docket shows exactly one distribution (June 24, 2026, for the
Conference of September 28, 2026) and no relist. I expect no further
distribution: the modal path for a paid petition whose respondent has waived
is a single conference and a denial. The two ways a second distribution
happens are a call for a response (which would push the petition to a later
conference after the response arrives) and a relist off the long conference;
I put the combined chance at about 12%, most of it the long-conference
carryover rather than a genuine second look.

**Call for a response.** Wisconsin waived on June 2, 2026. The Court will not
call for a response. A call is the necessary precursor to any grant, and its
absence is why the grant number sits below the band floor.

**CVSG.** None. The case is a state arson prosecution turning on a state
other-acts evidence rule; no federal interest is implicated and the United
States has no stake. Under 1%.

**Which question, if taken.** If the Court were to take anything it would not
take the question as written. The QP bundles the presumption of innocence, the
privilege against self-incrimination, the right to testify, and the right to
counsel into one abstract due-process question about admitting a charged but
untried "other act" in a contemporaneous prosecution. A grant would require the
Court to reformulate it into something narrow, most plausibly whether the Due
Process Clause bars admission of uncharged-or-untried conduct under a
preponderance standard when the conduct is the subject of a pending charge in
the same jurisdiction. Even reformulated, the record does not present that
question cleanly, because trial counsel did not object to the admission and the
state courts reached it through an ineffective-assistance framing.

**Summary disposition versus plenary review.** Conditional on a grant, I put a
GVR at about 35% and plenary review at about 65%. There is no intervening
decision of this Court that squarely bears on admitting pending-charge conduct
as other-acts evidence; Villareal v. Texas (2026), which the petition leans on,
concerns consultation with counsel during a testimonial recess and is not a GVR
hook for an evidentiary ruling. So a grant, if it came, would more likely be a
plenary grant on a reformulated question than a remand in light of anything.

**Dissent from denial.** None expected. No Justice has shown a standing
interest in the constitutional limits of other-acts evidence in state trials,
the decision below is an unpublished per curiam, and there is no split to
flag. About 2%.

**If granted (conditional).** The Court would most likely hold that admission
of pending-charge conduct under the ordinary Huddleston/Dowling preponderance
gate does not violate due process, resolving the question against the
petitioner; a holding that such admission is structural error is very
unlikely.
