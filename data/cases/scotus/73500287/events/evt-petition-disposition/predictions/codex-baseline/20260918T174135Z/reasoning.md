## Case-specific adjustments

**Reasons to take the petition seriously.** The questions presented concern whether cross-shopping necessarily defeats an otherwise supported antitrust submarket and whether an intermediary-controlled, multi-supplier purchasing channel can constitute a market. The petition describes a published Fifth Circuit decision and a record developed through summary judgment. It says both courts disposed of the action on market definition, without resolving other antitrust grounds or the expert-exclusion motion (petition pp. 8–9). That gives the legal question a plausible connection to the judgment. The claimed implications for hospital purchasing and other channel-based markets, and the recorded amicus filing, make this more than an unsupported request to reconsider a private loss. These are upward considerations, but the hospital market-share and exclusion allegations remain the petitioner's account, not independent findings adopted here.

**The asserted conflict is less clean than the advocacy suggests.** The petition chiefly contrasts the Fifth Circuit with Whole Foods and Newcal (pp. 11–17). Its own quotation says the Fifth Circuit declined to rule on a core-customer theory it considered nonbinding even in the D.C. Circuit (p. 15). I checked the historical Whole Foods decision through CourtListener, not any subsequent history of this petition. The amended November 21, 2008 decision has separate Brown and Tatel opinions, the latter concurring in the judgment. Judge Ginsburg's concurrence in denial of rehearing en banc, joined by Chief Judge Sentelle, expressly characterized the judgment as having precedential reach confined to its particular facts because there was no controlling opinion for the court. See FTC v. Whole Foods Market, Inc., 548 F.3d 1028 (D.C. Cir. 2008), CourtListener opinion 9852752. That concurrence is evidence of a disputed precedential foundation, not itself a binding nationwide holding about submarkets. It nevertheless materially weakens a simple claim of opposing controlling circuit rules.

The petition describes Newcal as a pleading-stage derivative-aftermarket case, unlike this developed summary-judgment record (pp. 13, 16). I did not independently read Newcal and do not treat the petition's characterization as a verified square split. Several additional examples are district-court decisions. Moreover, the petition's account acknowledges substantial actual purchases outside the asserted channel and hospital exits from it (pp. 15–16). The question may therefore appear to concern application of agreed economic principles to evidence rather than a categorical prohibition on submarkets. The unresolved expert challenge and redacted evidence add uncertainty even though they do not establish an independent judgment ground.

**Procedural interest is limited so far.** There is one petition distribution, a response waiver, one recorded amicus, and no observed call for a response or CVSG. None establishes that the Justices have engaged the alleged split. A future response request is possible, and the amicus arrived after the initial distribution, so I do not treat the waiver as dispositive. Taken together, however, these considerations and the imperfect split outweigh the upward importance and vehicle considerations. I reduce the 5.12% anchor to **3.5%**, a judgmental adjustment rather than a fitted likelihood model.

## Other probabilities and significance

- **Further distribution: 15%.** The modal path is denial following the first scheduled conference. A response request, rescheduling, or closer consideration could generate additional distributions; the claim counts those entries, not only true conference relists. Conditional on further distribution, one or two additional entries are more plausible than a long sequence.
- **New CVSG: 1.5%.** Antitrust enforcement interests make consultation conceivable, but this private dispute has no existing invitation or demonstrated sovereign-party interest. This is an elicited subjective hazard, not the terminal CVSG bucket's frequency.
- **Summary disposition given a grant: 8%.** The supplied record identifies no intervening Supreme Court decision requiring a GVR. Resolving the asserted market-definition conflict would more naturally require plenary review. The conditional probability leaves a small allowance for an unforeseen intervening development or summary correction.
- **Separate writing given denial: 2.5%.** I expect an unexplained denial and do not forecast a particular Justice's vote or authorship. The existence of the historical Whole Foods dissent does not justify predicting a present separate writing here.
- **Significance: 0.55.** This is distinct from grant probability. A merits ruling could affect hospital procurement and the use of customer or channel submarkets beyond this industry; the predecision materials still describe a technical competition-law controversy rather than a general constitutional or institutional dispute.
