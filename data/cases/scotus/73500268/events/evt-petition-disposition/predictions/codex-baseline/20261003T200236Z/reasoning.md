## Why the probability is not near certainty

The September opposition, pages 7–13, makes a serious vehicle-specific argument: Roofing Designs was a commercial litigant pursuing quantified claims before bankruptcy, assigned a zero value to the Royal American claim, initially omitted Hartford, obtained confirmation, and amended only after the omission was challenged. Unlike an unexplained omission of an unknown claim, these circumstances could support judicial estoppel after an unrestricted inquiry. The opposition also correctly distinguishes Keathley's actual holding from petitioner's hoped-for categorical bad-faith requirement.

Those points preserve a meaningful denial possibility, but they do not convincingly establish that the lower courts already used the corrected legal standard. Several factual findings emphasized in the opposition concern inconsistency and judicial acceptance; their detail does not by itself cure the separately articulated restrictive inadvertence test. A GVR permits the Fifth Circuit to assess the significance of the complete record without this Court deciding whether petitioner ultimately wins. I therefore place substantially more weight on the explicit reasoning in the appended opinions than on the opposition's characterization that the analysis already satisfies Keathley. The 18% non-grant probability is principally denial; plenary review and summary reversal are very small alternatives within the grant probability.
