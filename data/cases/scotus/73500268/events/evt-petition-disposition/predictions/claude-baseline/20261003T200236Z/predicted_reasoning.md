# Predicted reasoning — Roofing Designs by JR, L.L.C. v. Royal American Construction, Inc. (No. 25-1367)

**Forecast: the Court grants the petition, vacates the Fifth Circuit's judgment, and remands for further consideration in light of *Keathley v. Buddy Ayers Construction, Inc.*, No. 25-6 (June 11, 2026).** The order comes out of the October 9, 2026 conference or the one after it, on an order list in mid-October 2026, with no opinion and most likely no separate writing.

## Relists

The docket shows **two distributions**: July 22, 2026 (for the September 28 conference) and September 23, 2026 (for the October 9 conference). The first was superseded when the Court called for a response on August 7, so the petition has not yet actually been considered at a conference; the October 9 conference is its first live consideration. I expect it to be disposed of at that conference or after at most one further distribution. A GVR order ordinarily issues the first time the Court considers a ripe petition whose lead case is already decided; the main path to a further distribution is a Justice preparing a short statement or the Court carrying the petition over one week. Roughly one chance in four of any further distribution.

## CVSG

None, and none will issue. The dispute is between private parties over a subcontract and a payment bond; the United States has no interest, and the Court has just resolved the governing legal question in *Keathley*. A CVSG would be pointless and is essentially excluded.

## Which question the Court takes

None, in the plenary sense. The petition's single question — whether judicial estoppel can rest on a "potential motive" for bankruptcy nondisclosure without evidence of bad faith — is the question *Keathley* answered on June 11, 2026, when a unanimous Court held that the Fifth Circuit's two-factor inadvertence rule (knowledge plus hypothetical motive) was too rigid and too broad and that courts must look to the totality of the circumstances. The petition itself asked only for a hold and a GVR. The Court will not set this case for argument; if it acts favorably at all it will be in the cert order. If any question were taken, it would be the QP as written, but that is not the live scenario.

## Summary disposition versus plenary review

Conditional on any grant, a **GVR is the near-certain route** (about 0.96). The Fifth Circuit's unpublished per curiam resolved the inadvertence prong by quoting *In re Coastal Plains* and *Love v. Tyson Foods* for the proposition that a debtor's omission is inadvertent "only when" the debtor lacks knowledge or has no motive, and that "a lack of inadvertence may therefore be found when there is any potential motivation for concealing a claim" — the formulation *Keathley* rejected by name. That is the paradigm GVR posture: a judgment resting on a legal premise the Court has since repudiated, from the same circuit, decided weeks before the lead case. The respondents' answer — that the record would satisfy a totality standard anyway — is an argument for the Fifth Circuit on remand, not a reason for the Court to do the totality analysis itself in the first instance. A summary reversal is not in play: *Keathley* did not adopt petitioner's bad-faith rule, and the Court has nothing to reverse outright.

## If the Court denies

Denial would mean the Court accepted the respondents' position that the district court's record-specific findings — a corporate representative who had sworn to a six-figure damages figure in the district court and then signed schedules valuing the same claims at $0 and omitting the Hartford claim entirely — make the outcome the same under any standard. If that happens I expect **no separate writing** in most cases; the residual chance of a short statement or dissent respecting denial is modest (about 0.12), most plausibly from Justice Sotomayor, who concurred separately in *Keathley*, or Justice Jackson as its author. A dissent from a GVR, should the Court grant, is likewise unlikely; Justices Thomas and Gorsuch concurred in *Keathley* and have no reason to object to a remand.

## If granted (conditional merits-shaped content)

On remand the Fifth Circuit would reconsider the inadvertence prong under a totality-of-the-circumstances inquiry. The record the respondents describe gives the panel a plausible path to the same result, so a GVR here is a remand for a second look rather than a prediction of ultimate victory for the petitioner.
