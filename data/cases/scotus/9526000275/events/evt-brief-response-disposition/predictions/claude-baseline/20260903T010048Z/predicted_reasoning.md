# Forecast: disposition of application 26A275 (injunction pending appeal)

**Bottom line: the application will be denied, most likely by September 4, 2026, in a
brief unexplained order.** P(unqualified grant) = 0.05.

## Disposition and timing

The applicants asked for an injunction by September 3, 2026, ahead of the Michigan
Board of State Canvassers' September 4 ballot-finalization meeting, and Justice
Kavanaugh set a response deadline of noon September 2 — the Court is plainly moving
on the applicants' clock. Expect a disposition within one to two days of the
response (i.e., by September 3–4). The self-imposed urgency evaporates once the
Michigan ballot is finalized, so an order after September 4 would most likely be a
denial framed as moot or near-moot.

The modal order is a flat denial. Three features of the application make an
unqualified grant very unlikely despite the sympathetic equities:

1. **The relief sought is the Court's most disfavored form.** This is not a stay of
   a lower-court order; it is a mandatory injunction ordering state election
   officials to certify an initiative for the ballot — affirmative relief the Court
   reserves for cases where the applicant's right is indisputably clear. The Court
   grants such injunctions against state election administration on the eve of
   ballot printing essentially never.
2. **Jurisdiction is contestable.** The application rests 28 U.S.C. § 1257
   jurisdiction on a "constructive denial" theory: the Michigan Supreme Court has
   not ruled on the mandamus petition filed August 27, so its inaction should be
   treated as a final judgment. A.A.R.P. v. Trump involved a federal district
   court's inaction under the Court's appellate jurisdiction over federal courts;
   extending it to an undecided state-court original action would be a significant
   jurisdictional move the Court can avoid simply by waiting for Michigan.
3. **The Michigan Supreme Court can still act.** State law gives the mandamus
   petition "highest priority," and the state court frequently rules peremptorily
   in ballot cases in the final days. If it grants relief, this application is
   withdrawn or denied as moot; if it denies relief, the Court can deny here
   without prejudice to a renewed application — but by then the ballot is final.

A grant, if it comes, would look like a short administrative injunction preserving
the possibility of ballot placement while the Michigan Supreme Court rules — that
scenario, plus a small chance the full Court is persuaded by the due-process claim
(the Board's own on-record concession that the nine affidavits would have cured the
three-signature deficiency), is what the 5% covers. A dismissal or withdrawal
(roughly 10–13% combined) follows if the Michigan Supreme Court acts first.

## The escalation ladder from here

- **Further response request: none.** The requested response was filed September 2;
  that rung is spent (the claim is vacuous on this record).
- **Referral to the full Court: more likely than not (0.55).** A politically
  salient election application on which a response was called for is the kind a
  Circuit Justice typically refers rather than resolves in chambers, and any
  separate writing requires the full Court. The main paths to no referral: the
  Michigan Supreme Court moots the application before the Justice acts, or
  Kavanaugh denies alone on the jurisdictional defect to beat the September 4
  deadline — in-chambers denials of clearly flawed emergency applications still
  happen, and the compressed timeline favors one.
- **Amicus filings: probably none (0.12).** The docket shows zero amici and the
  window before disposition is roughly one day. Election-integrity groups on both
  sides move fast and the citizen-only-voting subject attracts organized interest,
  but a same-day amicus on an application this compressed is the exception.

## Separate writings

If the application is denied, a short statement or dissent from denial is plausible
but not likely — the jurisdictional posture gives Justices who might sympathize with
the merits (the Board conceded the affidavits were substantively valid) an easy
reason to stay silent. If any Justice writes, expect a statement noting that denial
is without prejudice pending the Michigan Supreme Court's disposition, rather than a
dissent urging the injunction.
