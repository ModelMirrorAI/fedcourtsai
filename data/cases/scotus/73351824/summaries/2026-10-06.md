## In brief

Former foster parents asked the Court to revive their challenge to Indian child-welfare laws after being denied a role in the children's case; the Court declined.

## What happened

Nathan and Kellie Reyelts served as foster parents to twins who qualified as Indian children under federal and state law. After more than a year, county officials moved the children to a relative identified by the Red Lake Nation. The Reyeltses sought to intervene in the children's case, seeking custody and arguing that the federal Indian Child Welfare Act and Minnesota's parallel law unconstitutionally treat Indian children differently from other children. The trial court denied intervention, in part because they raised that constitutional argument, and the Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed, declining to reach the constitutional question.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asked the Court to decide two things: whether laws giving different child-welfare protections to children eligible for tribal membership deny equal protection to those children and to non-Indian adults seeking to care for them, and whether it violates free-speech and petition rights for a court to deny someone a role in a child's case specifically because that person argued those laws are unconstitutional.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after briefing that included a request for a response and an extension of time for the respondents to reply. The Supreme Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Reyeltses, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up their case and reverse the Minnesota Supreme Court's decision. By denying the petition, the Court leaves that decision in place: the Reyeltses' exclusion from the children's case stands, and the constitutional questions about ICWA and MIFPA go unaddressed by the Court. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Minnesota courts' reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling as the final word in this case.
