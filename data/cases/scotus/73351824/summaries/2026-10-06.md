## In brief

Former foster parents asked the Court to review whether Minnesota's handling of an Indian child welfare law case violated their constitutional rights; the Court denied the petition.

## What happened

Nathan and Kellie Reyelts cared for twin foster children for over a year before Minnesota officials moved the children to a relative under state and federal laws governing the placement of Native American children in foster care. The Reyeltses tried to intervene in the children's case, partly to argue those laws were unconstitutional. Minnesota courts denied them intervention, with lower courts stating that their challenge to the laws showed a poor attitude toward the children's tribal heritage. The Minnesota Supreme Court affirmed, declined to rule on the constitutional question, and set aside the part of the appeals court's opinion that had addressed it. The Reyeltses then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The Reyeltses asked the Court to decide two things: first, whether the federal and state laws governing placement of Native American children in foster care treat those children and prospective caregivers unequally based on race in a way the Constitution forbids; and second, whether it violated their free speech and petition rights for state courts to deny them a role in the case partly because they argued those laws were unconstitutional.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the respondents filed a brief in opposition after an extension of time. The Supreme Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Reyeltses asked the Court to take up their case. With the petition denied, the Minnesota Supreme Court's decision stands: the Reyeltses remain excluded from the children's case, and the constitutional questions they raised about the child welfare laws go unaddressed by this Court. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Minnesota courts' reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling in place without deciding the underlying issues.
