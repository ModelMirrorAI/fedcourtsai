## Why 16%, rather than the 5.12% anchor

The principal upward adjustment is a substantial federal question that Brackeen expressly left unresolved. In Haaland v. Brackeen, 599 U.S. 255, 292-294 & n.10 (2023), the Court rejected the federal plaintiffs' route to redress and identified state proceedings as an available place to challenge ICWA. Kavanaugh separately emphasized the equal-protection issue at 333-334. I verified those passages in CourtListener opinion 10516275. This supports a real prospect of interest in a state-court vehicle, not a conclusion that any such vehicle is automatically reviewable or meritorious.

These petitioners allege a concrete lost opportunity for permanent custody and identify a trial-court statement tying their constitutional challenge to the denial of intervention. Their second question presents a potentially serious obstacle to litigating the first. The response request and three amicus entries reinforce the upward adjustment. Sources: snapshot docket entries; questions-presented.txt; petition pp. 24-36. I treat the petition's characterization of the statutory classifications and its accounts of harm as advocacy, not established constitutional conclusions.

The largest downward adjustment is the procedural vehicle. The Minnesota Supreme Court expressly declined to decide equal protection because the applicants were not proper parties, and vacated the intermediate court's constitutional discussion. It affirmed a discretionary best-interests ruling and rejected the particular custody filing route. Sources: petition Appendix A, pp. 1a-3a, 20a, 31a-36a. That leaves party status, state procedure, causation, and reviewability between the broad constitutional question and a merits decision; the lower judgment is not a clean final adjudication sustaining ICWA's constitutionality.

The First Amendment claim is more substantial than ordinary error correction, but its factual premise is contested by the judgment itself. Appendix A, pp. 32a-35a, assumes the asserted protection for purposes of analysis, then finds neither demonstrated retaliatory animus nor decisive causation. It describes other grounds for denying intervention, including concern that the prospective intervenors would obstruct alternative placements. The petition argues that these state-law grounds are inadequate, interwoven with the federal violation, and inconsistent with state practice (pp. 28-32). That is a meaningful answer, but not an established fact. A Supreme Court intervention would require looking through the alternative-ground analysis rather than simply applying Brackeen's footnote.

I found no clearly established, square conflict of appellate holdings in the material I read. The petition's asserted urgency and other troubling examples are not substitutes for one. Taken together, the live constitutional opening, response request, and procedural-rights theory justify roughly tripling the private baseline, while the alternative state-law grounds and missing adversarial briefing prevent an aggressive grant forecast. The final 16% is a judgmental calibration, not a fitted model or an empirically estimated likelihood ratio. Denial remains the clear modal result.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The 38% further-distribution probability reflects the nonroutine question and response request, tempered by the absence of a genuine completed-conference relist so far. The 12% CVSG probability recognizes federal and tribal interests without conflating a respondent response request with a request for the Solicitor General's views. Neither incremental claim has a published transition baseline in the pack.

Conditional on any grant, I put 30% on disposition in the cert order because a narrow procedural correction is conceivable; plenary review remains more likely given contested causation and the scope of the equal-protection theory. Conditional on denial, 20% for an aggregate dissent or statement reflects the previously expressed concern in Brackeen, not a prediction of any Justice's private vote. These conditionals are not multiplied by the top-level grant or denial probability in the JSON.

The 0.80 big-case score measures potential stakes, not the chance of review. Reaching the first question could affect placement rules beyond Minnesota and the relationship between tribal classifications and equal protection. The second question could instead yield a narrower procedural ruling. The personal stakes for the children are substantial, but I do not use the petition's descriptions of their medical circumstances to infer which placement is factually best.
