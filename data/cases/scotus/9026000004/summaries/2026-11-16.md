## In brief

Samsung asked the Court to decide whether Georgia could treat its business registration as consent to be sued there; the Court denied review.

## What happened

Jordan Brewer was badly burned in Georgia when a battery in his vape pen exploded. He sued Samsung Electronics America in Georgia state court. Samsung did not respond, and the trial court entered a default judgment against it for about $10.9 million. Samsung spent years trying to undo that judgment, arguing among other things that Georgia courts lacked personal jurisdiction over it. Georgia's courts repeatedly ruled against Samsung, largely on procedural and state-law grounds, and the Georgia Court of Appeals most recently reversed a trial court order that had set aside the judgment. The Georgia Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

Samsung asks whether a state can treat a company's registration to do business there as consent to be sued in that state's courts on any claim, even though the state's registration law does not say registering has that effect, and even though the courts that read the law that way did so only after Samsung had already registered. Brewer counters that Samsung never properly raised this federal question in the Georgia courts, waived the jurisdictional defense through its own litigation conduct, and that the Court's 2023 ruling in a similar case already settled the underlying issue.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conferences in September and November 2026, with full briefing including a response and a reply completed by late October. The Court denied the petition on November 16, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Samsung, which lost in the Georgia Court of Appeals, asked the Court to take up and reverse that result. The denial leaves the Georgia Court of Appeals' decision in place, meaning the default judgment against Samsung stands and Samsung remains liable for the damages awarded to Brewer. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Georgia courts' reasoning on jurisdiction; it simply leaves that outcome undisturbed, and the question Samsung raised about registration-based jurisdiction remains unresolved by the Court.
