## In brief

Samsung asks the Court to review whether Georgia courts can exercise jurisdiction over it based on business registration alone, after lower courts ruled against it.

## What happened

Jordan Brewer, badly burned when a battery in his vape pen exploded, won a $10.8 million default judgment against Samsung in a Georgia trial court after Samsung failed to respond to the lawsuit. Samsung spent years trying to undo that judgment, arguing among other things that Georgia courts lacked personal jurisdiction over it. The Georgia Court of Appeals twice ruled against Samsung, and the Georgia Supreme Court twice declined to review the case. Samsung says it only raised its federal due-process jurisdiction argument properly later; Brewer's brief in opposition says Samsung never properly raised that argument in the Georgia courts and separately waived the jurisdictional defense by its own conduct and admissions.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state can treat a company's registration to do business there as consent to be sued on any matter in that state's courts, even when the state's registration law does not explicitly say that registering has this effect, and even when courts only started reading the law that way after the company first registered.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for November 13, 2026, after full briefing including Brewer's brief in opposition and Samsung's reply. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Samsung, which lost in the Georgia courts, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the jurisdictional question itself, without that decision being preordained. If the Court denies review, the Georgia Court of Appeals ruling against Samsung stays in place, leaving Brewer's judgment intact, and the jurisdictional question would not be decided by the Court in this case.
