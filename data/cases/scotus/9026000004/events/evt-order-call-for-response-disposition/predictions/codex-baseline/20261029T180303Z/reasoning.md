## Why 14%, rather than the anchor or a likely grant

The claim that Samsung registered in 1985, before Georgia's 1992 consent precedent, adds a potentially narrower notice question. Those facts justify moving materially above the 5.11% private-petitioner anchor, but a response request is a low threshold, not four votes to grant.

The legal theory is not as straightforward as the petition suggests. The official **Mallory v. Norfolk Southern Railway Co., 600 U.S. 122, 134–35 & n.5 (2023)** opinion reserved other schemes but rejected a magic-words requirement; it discussed Pennsylvania Fire's state-court construction and Georgia's approach. Thus statutory silence alone does not establish a direct conflict. My inference is that the split over different registration regimes may be less clean than a disagreement on one federal rule. The cited out-of-state decisions are the petition's account, not independently checked holdings.

The strongest downward adjustment is vehicle quality, visible in the provisioned appellate opinion (Appendix B, pp. 2a–13a). This is a collateral default-judgment dispute involving two appeals, venue, the failure to answer, and limits on reopening a final judgment. The appellate court did not expressly decide the consent-notice question. The petition says it preserved that argument, but the underlying briefs are not provisioned. I cannot establish forfeiture or an adequate independent state ground from this record; their potential to obstruct review nevertheless matters.
