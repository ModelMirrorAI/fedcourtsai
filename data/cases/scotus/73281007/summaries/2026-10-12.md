## In brief

The Department of Labor asked the Supreme Court to decide whether its in-house judges may order farms to pay penalties and back wages for violating H-2A guest worker program rules.

## What happened

Sun Valley Orchards, a New Jersey farm, hired foreign workers through the H-2A visa program in 2015. The Department of Labor found the farm had violated program rules covering kitchen access, housing, transportation, and workers' contract terms, and its in-house judges ordered the farm to pay over $500,000 in penalties and back wages. Sun Valley challenged the process in federal court, arguing that only a real court, not an agency judge, could decide such claims. The district court sided with the Department. The Third Circuit reversed, ruling that the claims resembled a breach-of-contract suit seeking money damages, which the Constitution reserves for courts rather than agency adjudicators.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's separation of powers allows the Department of Labor to decide, through its own administrative process rather than a court, that a farm owes money penalties and back pay for violating the terms of employing H-2A workers. A second question asks whether a federal immigration statute even authorizes the Department to use that in-house process in the first place, as opposed to suing in court.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to both questions, and the case was argued on November 10, 2026, after full briefing by both sides and numerous outside groups. The case now awaits a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

The Department of Labor, representing the federal government, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court and uphold its authority to decide these cases itself. If the Court affirms the Third Circuit's decision, agencies like the Department would be barred from deciding such penalty and back-wage claims in-house and would instead have to sue employers in federal court, as Sun Valley argued. If the Court reverses, the Department's in-house process would be upheld, and Sun Valley's liability for the penalties and back wages could stand. The Court could also send the case back without resolving the dispute for either side.
