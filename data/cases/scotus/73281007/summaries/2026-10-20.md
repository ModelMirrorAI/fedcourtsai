## In brief

The Department of Labor asks the Supreme Court whether it may use in-house judges, rather than federal courts, to fine farms over H-2A worker contract violations.

## What happened

Sun Valley Orchards, a New Jersey farm, hired foreign workers under the H-2A visa program in 2015 alongside domestic workers in similar jobs. After an investigation, the Department of Labor found the farm had violated program requirements covering kitchen access, housing, transportation, and guaranteed work hours, and it imposed over half a million dollars in penalties and back wages through its own administrative proceedings. The farm challenged that process in federal court, arguing the agency's in-house adjudication violated the Constitution's separation of powers and lacked clear statutory authorization. The district court sided with the Department, but the Third Circuit reversed, ruling that the farm was entitled to have the case decided by a federal judge rather than an agency court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution requires claims like these — enforcement actions seeking money penalties and back wages for violations of farmworker contract terms — to be decided by federal courts rather than by the agency's own administrative judges. A second question asks whether the federal immigration statute even gives the Department authority to resolve such claims administratively in the first place, as opposed to only in court.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to these two questions and the case was argued on November 10, 2026, with briefing now complete; a decision has not yet been recorded on the docket.

## What each outcome would mean

The Department of Labor is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling in favor of Sun Valley Orchards. If the Court sides with the Department, agencies could continue resolving these enforcement actions in-house, and the farm's win below would be undone, likely restoring the original penalties and back wages. If the Court instead affirms, Sun Valley's win stands, and the Department would need to pursue such penalty and back-wage claims in federal court rather than through its own administrative judges. The Court could also vacate and send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
