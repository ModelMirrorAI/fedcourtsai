## In brief

The government asks the Supreme Court whether Article III lets the Labor Department fine H-2A farm employers through its own judges rather than federal court.

## What happened

Sun Valley Orchards, a New Jersey farm, hired foreign and domestic workers under the H-2A visa program in 2015. The Labor Department investigated and found violations involving kitchen access, beverage charges, transportation, housing conditions, and early termination of workers, ultimately imposing over half a million dollars in civil penalties and back wages through its own administrative law judges. Sun Valley challenged the process, arguing that only a federal court could decide such claims. A district court upheld the agency's authority, but the Third Circuit reversed, holding that the claims resembled a breach-of-contract action seeking traditional legal remedies, so the Constitution required a federal judge rather than an in-house tribunal.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's assignment of judicial power to independent courts bars the Labor Department from using its own officials to decide and impose fines and back wages for alleged violations of H-2A program rules, or whether this kind of case fits within a recognized exception allowing executive agencies to decide disputes connected to immigration and government-granted benefits. A second question asks whether the underlying immigration statute even authorizes the agency to run such in-house proceedings in the first place, apart from the constitutional issue.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari limited to these two questions, briefing and amicus filings are complete, and the case was set for oral argument on November 10, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Department of Labor, representing the federal government, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling. If the Court affirms the Third Circuit's decision, Sun Valley's win stands, and the Department would be unable to use its in-house judges to impose penalties and back wages in this type of case, instead needing to sue in federal court. If the Court reverses, the Department's authority to adjudicate these claims administratively would be upheld, and the case would return to the lower courts for further proceedings consistent with that ruling. The Court could also vacate the decision and send the case back without resolving which side is right.
