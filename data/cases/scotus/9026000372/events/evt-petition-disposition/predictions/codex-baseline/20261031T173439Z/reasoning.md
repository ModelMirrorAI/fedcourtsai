## Why adjust slightly below the anchor?

**The question has substance.** The petition's single question asks whether appellate mandate recall can revive a judgment already vacated for lack of personal jurisdiction after later legislation and later conduct create jurisdiction. It challenges reinstatement of a $655.5 million judgment, not merely an interlocutory step. Petition pages 6–17 develop a real distinction between curing jurisdiction during an ongoing appeal and resurrecting a case after completion of appellate review. Its reliance on *Pennoyer*, *Calderon*, and *Plaut* supplies a recognizable conflict-with-Supreme-Court-precedent theory. That makes a near-zero grant estimate inappropriate even without a circuit split.

**But this is not a demonstrated split requiring national resolution.** The petition points to state cases and general jurisdiction/finality principles, not opposing federal appellate holdings on materially equivalent PSJVTA mandate recall. Its claim that the decision is unprecedented is not itself an established conflict. The lower court applies an abuse-of-discretion framework to an exceptionally unusual statutory and procedural history. Neither the panel disposition nor the rehearing order in the appendix identifies a dissent. Lack of a recorded dissent does not establish how every judge voted.

**The appendix materially qualifies the petition's framing.** The panel acknowledged that the district court originally lacked jurisdiction. It nevertheless distinguished Congress compelling reopening from a court exercising its own recall power, relied on jurisdiction-curing and waiver authorities, and emphasized the original dismissal was without prejudice. It also explained that plaintiffs could pursue the same claims in an already-filed second action, so petitioners sought a new trial rather than freedom from the underlying claims. See appendix printed pages 16a–27a, particularly the recall analysis and voidness discussion. Those considerations do not conclusively answer whether a vacated judgment can be revived, but they give the Court a narrower way to view the result than a general license to reopen all final judgments.

**Recent prior review reduces the force of a simple error-correction narrative.** The provisioned petition and appendix describe the Court's 2020 remand and its June 20, 2025 decision in *Fuld v. Palestine Liberation Organization*, 606 U.S. 1. I separately consulted that earlier opinion through CourtListener, not the disposition of this petition. Its syllabus confirms that the PSJVTA's targeted personal-jurisdiction provision survived Fifth Amendment review because of its connection to United States conduct and the political branches' foreign-policy judgment. The new mandate-recall question remains distinct; the prior decision did not itself decide it. My inference is nevertheless that the Court may regard this as a narrow implementation dispute following its earlier intervention rather than another plenary vehicle.

**The observed procedural interest is weak.** Both respondent groups waived, including the Solicitor General as counsel for the federal respondents. Waivers are the respondents' decisions, not a Court determination of merit; they do not foreclose a later response request. Still, the first distribution contains no affirmative attention signal beyond ordinary processing. Petition page 6 also reports a denied stay application in August 2026. That is an earlier, separate request, not the outcome being forecast. I give it little weight because interim relief involves additional requirements.

Together, the substantial legal question and unusually high stakes keep the probability near the band anchor, while lack of a square split, the defensible narrow grounds below, and the waivers move it modestly down to **4%**. The key uncertainty is whether four Justices see the completed-appeal distinction as a clean constitutional boundary worth enforcing immediately.
