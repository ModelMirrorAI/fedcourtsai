# Forecast: PLO v. Sokolow, No. 26-372

**Disposition: denied** (P(grant) ≈ 4%).

**Relists.** The docket shows one distribution, for the November 13, 2026 conference. Both the Sokolow plaintiffs (Sept. 22) and the federal respondents (Oct. 16) waived a response. The likeliest path is a plain denial on the November 16 order list with no further distribution. The main way the docket moves again is a **call for a response**. This is a paid petition from experienced counsel in a high-profile ATA case that has already been to the Court three times, and some Justice may want to see the government's and plaintiffs' answer before voting. I put that at about one in three. If a response is called for, the petition gets distributed again once briefing finishes (probably January–February 2027). After that it is most likely denied at its first post-response conference. A relist without a response call is unlikely.

**CVSG.** None. The United States is already a respondent (an intervenor below, defending the PSJVTA), so the Court has no occasion to invite the SG's views.

**Question, if granted.** The Court would take the QP as written: whether a court of appeals' equitable power to recall its mandate allows it to reinstate a judgment vacated for lack of personal jurisdiction, where jurisdiction rests on a later-enacted statute. The Plaut separation-of-powers point would come along as a subsidiary argument. If granted, the main difficulty for petitioners is that the 2019 order refusing to recall the mandate was itself vacated by this Court's 2020 GVR (No. 19-764) in light of the PSJVTA. The recall motion was therefore still live when the statute took effect, which blunts the "final judgment reopened" framing.

**Summary disposition.** Unlikely. No intervening decision calls for a GVR, and a summary reversal of a discretionary mandate recall that the Court's own 2020 GVR effectively invited is implausible.

**Dissent from denial.** Unlikely. If one comes, the most plausible writers are Justice Gorsuch (on finality and void-judgment history) or Justice Thomas (who wrote separately on the due-process framework in Fuld). I expect no separate writing.
