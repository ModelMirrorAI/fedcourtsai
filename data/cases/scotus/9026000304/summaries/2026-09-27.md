## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review whether officials it designated to lead U.S. Attorney's offices after vacancies arose were validly serving, after appeals courts ruled they were not.

## What happened

The U.S. Attorney for the District of Nevada resigned, and after a series of temporary appointments, the Attorney General designated Sigal Chattah as First Assistant U.S. Attorney and had her act as, or exercise delegated powers of, the U.S. Attorney. Several criminal defendants challenged her authority to supervise their prosecutions. The district court and the Ninth Circuit agreed with the defendants: a person can become the automatic "acting officer" under a federal vacancies law only if she was already the first assistant when the vacancy began, and the Attorney General cannot get around that by delegating her all the office's other powers instead. The government appealed, while the defendants' separate cross-appeal was dismissed for lack of jurisdiction.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two related questions about a law governing temporary leadership of vacant federal offices. First, whether someone who is named "first assistant" only after a vacancy already exists can still automatically take over as the acting head of that office, or whether only the person who held that post when the vacancy began qualifies. Second, whether, separately from that acting-officer rule, an agency head can hand a non-acting official essentially all of the office's ordinary powers through a delegation, without that being treated as an unauthorized workaround of the acting-officer rules.

## Where it stands

The government filed its petition on September 8, 2026. The respondents' deadline to respond was extended, on their motion, from October 8 to November 5, 2026. The petition has not yet been distributed for a conference.

## What each outcome would mean

The United States, as petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit and uphold Chattah's authority. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide the questions itself, resolving nothing yet about who is right. If it denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, meaning Chattah could not supervise these prosecutions either as acting officer or through delegation, and the defendants' challenge succeeds on that point.
