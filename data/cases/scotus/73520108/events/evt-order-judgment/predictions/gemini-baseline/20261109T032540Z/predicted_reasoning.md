# Predicted Reasoning: *Goldman Sachs Bank USA v. Brown* (No. 25-1408)

## Judgment and Vote Lineup

The Court will **reverse** the judgment of the United States Court of Appeals for the Fourth Circuit and remand for further proceedings (`probability` of disturbing the judgment below = **0.79**).

The expected vote split is **7–2**:
- **Majority (7):** Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett.
- **Dissent (2):** Justices Sotomayor and Jackson.

A 6–3 split (if Justice Kagan joins the dissenters on the ground that automatic-stay remedies are inseparable from ongoing bankruptcy administration) or an 8–1 / 9–0 decision (particularly if the opinion remands for individualized application to respondent Brown's open Chapter 13 proceeding while reversing outright as to respondent Maze's closed Chapter 7 proceeding and the Fourth Circuit's categorical reasoning) are the primary alternative lineups.

## Question Reached and Procedural Posture

The Court will reach the single question presented in the petition: whether, and under what circumstances, a bankruptcy court may override the Federal Arbitration Act (FAA), 9 U.S.C. § 2, and refuse to honor a valid arbitration agreement with respect to private damages claims under 11 U.S.C. § 362(k) for alleged willful violations of the automatic stay.

A dismissal as improvidently granted (DIG) is unlikely despite two procedural wrinkles raised by respondents at the certiorari stage—the bankruptcy court's September 10, 2026 dismissal of the original complaint without prejudice for failure to plead actual damages (with 21 days' leave to amend), and respondents' invocation of 11 U.S.C. § 105(a) alongside § 362(k). Both points were squarely aired in respondents' September 14, 2026 supplemental brief and petitioner's September 15, 2026 supplemental response before the Court relisted the case through four conferences and granted certiorari on November 6, 2026. Having granted review with full awareness of the amended-complaint posture and the § 105(a) argument, the Court will decide the merits of the § 362(k) arbitrability question rather than DIG the case. Nor is there any apparent basis for a recusal that could produce an equally divided Court.

## Doctrinal Ground (`majority-ground`)

Applying the statutory-conflict framework of *Shearson/American Express, Inc. v. McMahon*, 482 U.S. 220 (1987), *CompuCredit Corp. v. Greenwood*, 565 U.S. 95 (2012), and *Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis*, 584 U.S. 497 (2018), the majority will hold that a party seeking to displace the FAA bears the heavy burden of demonstrating a "clear and manifest" congressional command in the text or structure of the Bankruptcy Code, and that neither 11 U.S.C. § 362(k) nor generalized bankruptcy policies satisfy that standard. Specifically:

1. **No textual override in § 362(k) or 28 U.S.C. § 157(b):** Section 362(k)(1) creates a private statutory cause of action enabling an individual injured by a willful stay violation to recover actual damages, costs, attorneys' fees, and, in appropriate circumstances, punitive damages. Like the statutory causes of action in *McMahon* (RICO and the Exchange Act), *CompuCredit* (CROA), and *Epic Systems* (the NLRA), § 362(k) makes no mention of arbitration and does not vest exclusive, non-waivable adjudicatory authority in the bankruptcy courts. Furthermore, classifying a claim as "core" under 28 U.S.C. § 157(b)(2) addresses the allocation of authority between Article III district courts and Article I bankruptcy courts, not whether Congress displaced bilateral arbitration under the FAA.
2. **Rejection of the Fourth Circuit's five purposive factors:** The majority will reject the Fourth Circuit's cumulative reliance on centralization, the debtor's "breathing spell," uniformity of appellate review, specialized judicial expertise, and the public deterrent value of punitive damages. Under *McMahon* and *Epic Systems*, general judicial-administration preferences, the availability of punitive damages, and doubts about arbitral expertise do not establish an irreconcilable statutory conflict with the FAA.
3. **Distinction between § 362(k) private damages claims and § 105(a) contempt:** Addressing respondents' central counterargument, the majority will distinguish a private statutory action for damages under § 362(k) from a bankruptcy court's civil contempt authority under § 105(a) (and *Taggart v. Lorenzen*, 587 U.S. 554 (2019)). While a private contract cannot strip a court of its own power to police and punish contempt of its statutory orders, a debtor's affirmative damages claim under § 362(k) is a private claim for monetary recovery subject to the FAA, and under *Dean Witter Reynolds Inc. v. Byrd*, 470 U.S. 213 (1985), and *KPMG LLP v. Cocchi*, 565 U.S. 18 (2011), the possibility of parallel or piecemeal proceedings does not defeat enforcement of an arbitration clause as to the arbitrable statutory claim.

## Breadth of the Holding (`ground-breadth`)

The majority will adopt a rule reaching beyond closed Chapter 7 proceedings to hold that private § 362(k) damages claims are generally subject to arbitration under the FAA unless enforcing the arbitration agreement would directly interfere with the ongoing administration or distribution of the bankruptcy estate, while leaving intact the bankruptcy court's non-arbitrable authority over § 105(a) contempt proceedings and equitable administration of an active estate.

In applying this rule to the two respondents:
- As to **respondent Maze** (whose Chapter 7 estate was fully administered, whose debts were discharged, and whose case was closed before the adversary proceeding was filed), the Court will hold that compelling arbitration of his § 362(k) claim cannot conflict with the Bankruptcy Code because any monetary recovery belongs solely to Maze and has no impact on estate administration or creditor distribution (aligning with *MBNA America Bank, N.A. v. Hill*, 436 F.3d 104 (2d Cir. 2006)).
- As to **respondent Brown** (whose Chapter 13 plan was confirmed in November 2023 and remains open), the Court will hold that the Fourth Circuit erred in treating the mere pendency of a Chapter 13 case or the "core" nature of § 362(k) as sufficient to refuse arbitration of a post-confirmation damages claim, and will direct that her § 362(k) damages claim likewise proceed to arbitration (or be evaluated on remand under that strict estate-interference standard), while noting that any genuinely non-arbitrable request for prospective injunctive or contempt relief in the open Chapter 13 case cannot be used to bootstrap a putative nationwide class action for § 362(k) damages out of arbitration.

## Expected Authorship and Separate Writings

- **Majority Opinion (`majority-author`):** Justices **Gorsuch** (`0.130`), **Barrett** (`0.130`), **Kavanaugh** (`0.125`), and **Thomas** (`0.120`), along with Chief Justice **Roberts** (`0.115`) and Justice **Kagan** (`0.110`), are the primary candidates to author the opinion of the Court. Justice Gorsuch authored *Epic Systems Corp. v. Lewis* and several recent bankruptcy decisions (*Harrington v. Purdue Pharma L.P.*, *Badgerow v. Walters*); Justice Barrett has frequently written in bankruptcy and civil-procedure cases (*Bartenwerfer v. Buckley*); and Justice Kavanaugh authored *Coinbase, Inc. v. Bielski*.
- **Separate Writings (`separate-writers`):**
  - **Dissent:** Justices **Sotomayor** (`0.30`) and **Jackson** (`0.28`) are the likeliest authors of a dissenting opinion (or an opinion concurring in part and dissenting in part as to respondent Brown's open Chapter 13 case), emphasizing that the automatic stay under § 362(a) is a foundational in rem protection and that bifurcating § 362(k) damages from § 105(a) stay enforcement burdens impecunious debtors and fractures bankruptcy administration.
  - **Concurrence:** Justice **Thomas** (`0.24`) or Justice **Gorsuch** (`0.20`) may write separately to question whether *McMahon*'s non-textual "inherent conflict" inquiry has any remaining vitality at all where a federal statute contains no express textual exception to the FAA.
