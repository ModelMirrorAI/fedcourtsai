The Supreme Court is very likely (about a four in five chance, or 79%) to disturb the Fourth Circuit's ruling, most likely by reversing it in a 7–2 vote. In this case, two bankrupt consumers sued Goldman Sachs for continuing to send automated Apple Card collection notices after they filed for bankruptcy, violating the "automatic stay" (a federal law that immediately halts debt-collection efforts during bankruptcy). Although the consumers' credit-card contracts required private arbitration instead of court lawsuits, the lower courts refused to enforce those arbitration clauses. Because the Supreme Court strictly enforces the Federal Arbitration Act unless Congress clearly says otherwise in the statute's text—and the bankruptcy provision allowing consumers to sue for money damages mentions no exception for arbitration—the Court is expected to rule that private damages claims for stay violations must be sent to arbitration when they do not interfere with administering the bankruptcy estate.
