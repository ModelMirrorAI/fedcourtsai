## In brief

A bank asks the Court to decide whether bankruptcy courts can refuse to send a debtor's stay-violation damages claim to arbitration.

## What happened

Goldman Sachs Bank continued trying to collect credit card debt from Rhea Ann Brown and Gregory Maze after they filed for bankruptcy, despite being notified of the automatic stay that halts such collection efforts. Brown and Maze sued Goldman in bankruptcy court, seeking damages under a Bankruptcy Code provision allowing recovery for willful stay violations. Goldman asked the court to send the dispute to arbitration under the parties' credit card agreement. The bankruptcy court refused, the district court affirmed, and a divided Fourth Circuit panel affirmed again, reasoning that arbitration would conflict with bankruptcy law's purposes. One judge dissented, saying the ruling conflicts with a Second Circuit decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a bankruptcy court may decline to enforce an otherwise valid arbitration agreement when a debtor brings a claim for damages over a stay violation, or whether federal arbitration law requires such claims to go to arbitration instead of bankruptcy court.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for the October 16, 2026 conference, and the docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Goldman Sachs, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether such claims can be forced into arbitration, resolving nothing until it rules. If the Court denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Brown and Maze's claims would continue in bankruptcy court rather than arbitration, and that result would hold at least within the Fourth Circuit. A denial would not mean the Supreme Court agrees the lower court was right—only that the decision remains in place.
