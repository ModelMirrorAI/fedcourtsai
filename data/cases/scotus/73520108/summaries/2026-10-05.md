## In brief

Goldman Sachs asks the Court to decide whether bankruptcy courts can refuse to send debtors' money-damage claims over automatic-stay violations to arbitration.

## What happened

Two bankruptcy debtors, Rhea Ann Brown and Gregory Kevin Maze, accused Goldman Sachs of continuing to demand credit card payments after they filed for bankruptcy, violating the automatic hold on debt collection that bankruptcy filing triggers. They sued in bankruptcy court for damages under the Bankruptcy Code. Goldman Sachs sought to force the dispute into arbitration under the parties' credit card agreement. The bankruptcy court, a district court, and the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals all declined to compel arbitration, reasoning that arbitrating these claims would conflict with bankruptcy law's purposes. One Fourth Circuit judge dissented, saying the ruling conflicts with a Second Circuit decision reaching the opposite result.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, and under what circumstances, a bankruptcy court can decline to enforce an otherwise valid arbitration agreement when a debtor seeks money damages for a creditor's violation of the automatic hold on collection efforts that bankruptcy filing imposes, or whether federal arbitration law requires sending such claims to arbitration instead.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference twice, most recently October 9, 2026, after the parties filed opposition, reply, and supplemental briefs. The docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will take the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Goldman Sachs asked the Court to take the case after losing in the Fourth Circuit, which ruled for Brown and Maze. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the arbitration question itself, deciding nothing about the outcome at this stage. If it denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Brown and Maze's claims against Goldman Sachs continue in bankruptcy court rather than arbitration, and the underlying bankruptcy litigation—already proceeding there—continues.
