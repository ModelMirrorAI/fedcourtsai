## In brief

A bank asks the Court to decide whether debtors suing it for collecting debts after they filed bankruptcy must take their damages claims to arbitration.

## What happened

Goldman Sachs Bank continued trying to collect credit card debt from Rhea Ann Brown and Gregory Maze after each filed for bankruptcy, despite being notified of the automatic halt on collection efforts that a bankruptcy filing triggers. Brown and Maze sued Goldman in bankruptcy court, seeking damages under a Bankruptcy Code provision that lets debtors recover for willful violations of that halt. Goldman asked the bankruptcy court to send the dispute to arbitration under the parties' credit card agreement. The bankruptcy court refused, the district court agreed, and the Fourth Circuit affirmed, finding that arbitrating such claims would conflict with bankruptcy law's purposes. Goldman then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a bankruptcy court can refuse to send a debtor's damages claim for a collection-halt violation to arbitration, even though the parties signed a valid arbitration agreement, because arbitrating that kind of claim would conflict with bankruptcy law's goals.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference multiple times between October and November 2026. On November 6, 2026, the Court granted the petition, meaning it will hear the case in full; no date for argument or decision is shown in this record.

## What each outcome would mean

Goldman Sachs, which lost in the lower courts and asked the Court to take the case, is now asking the Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit's decision. If the Court reverses, Brown's and Maze's damages claims would have to go to arbitration rather than being decided in bankruptcy court. If the Court affirms, the Fourth Circuit's ruling keeping those claims in bankruptcy court would stand. If the Court vacates, it would send the case back without deciding the arbitration question for either side.
