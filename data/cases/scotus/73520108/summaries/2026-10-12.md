## In brief

Goldman Sachs asks the Court to decide whether bankrupt debtors' damages claims over debt-collection violations must go to private arbitration rather than bankruptcy court.

## What happened

Two bankruptcy debtors, Rhea Ann Brown and Gregory Maze, accused Goldman Sachs of continuing to demand credit card payments after they filed for bankruptcy, violating the automatic hold on debt collection that bankruptcy filing triggers. They sued in bankruptcy court for damages under a Bankruptcy Code provision allowing such suits. Goldman Sachs asked the court to send the dispute to arbitration under the parties' card agreement. The bankruptcy court refused, and the district court and the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, reasoning that arbitration would conflict with bankruptcy law's purposes. One Fourth Circuit judge dissented.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a bankruptcy court can refuse to send a debtor's damages claim over a collection-hold violation to arbitration, even though the parties had signed a valid agreement to arbitrate disputes between them, and if so, under what circumstances that refusal is allowed.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply and supplemental briefs from both sides, and was distributed for the Court's conference on October 9, 2026. The docket does not yet show any action taken at that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Goldman Sachs, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the arbitration question itself, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision requiring the dispute to stay in bankruptcy court stands, leaving Brown and Maze's case there rather than in arbitration. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right, only that its ruling remains in place.
