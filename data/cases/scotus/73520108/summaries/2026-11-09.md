## In brief
The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether banks can be forced into arbitration over claims they illegally kept demanding payment from bankruptcy filers.

## What happened
Rhea Ann Brown and Gregory Kevin Maze each filed for bankruptcy, which under federal law automatically stops creditors from trying to collect old debts. They allege Goldman Sachs Bank kept demanding credit card payments from them anyway, even after being warned. They sued Goldman in bankruptcy court seeking damages under the law protecting debtors from such collection efforts. Goldman asked the court to send the dispute to arbitration instead, citing an arbitration clause in its card agreement. The bankruptcy court, a district court, and the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals all refused to compel arbitration, finding it would conflict with bankruptcy law's goals.

## What the Court is being asked
The Court is asked whether, and under what circumstances, a bankruptcy court can refuse to enforce an arbitration agreement and instead keep a debtor's damages claim for illegal debt-collection efforts in bankruptcy court rather than sending it to arbitration.

## Where it stands
The Supreme Court granted Goldman's petition for certiorari on November 6, 2026, after the case had been distributed for several conferences. The case will now proceed to full briefing and argument on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean
Goldman Sachs, which lost in the lower courts, asked the Supreme Court to take the case and is seeking to overturn the Fourth Circuit's ruling. If the Court ultimately reverses, Goldman's arbitration agreement would be enforced, sending Brown and Maze's claims to arbitration rather than bankruptcy court. If the Court affirms, the Fourth Circuit's decision keeping the dispute in bankruptcy court would stand, and Goldman would have to defend against the claims there. If the Court instead vacates the decision, the case would return to the lower courts without a final ruling on the arbitration question for either side.
