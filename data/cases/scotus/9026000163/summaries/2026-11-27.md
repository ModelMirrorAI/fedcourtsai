## In brief

A man convicted of money-laundering conspiracy asks the Court to throw out his conviction, arguing he was tried in the wrong location.

## What happened

Pierre Yvelt Almonor was convicted by a jury in North Carolina of conspiring to commit money laundering, tied to a scheme where stolen funds were wired through accounts he controlled. He argued the case against him involved two separate money-laundering schemes, one in Illinois tied to him and one in North Carolina tied to a different person, and that he had no connection to North Carolina and did not know of the other participant. The trial court and the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals rejected his argument that the trial was held in the wrong location, and the appeals court also upheld his conviction and sentence on several other grounds. His request for rehearing was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two things: first, whether someone can be tried in a location connected only to a different participant's actions, when that participant's conduct there was not something this person could have anticipated; and second, whether the evidence showed one unified conspiracy linking Almonor to the North Carolina transactions, or instead showed separate, unconnected schemes that merely shared a common organizer.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, with the government's opposition and the petitioner's reply both filed. The case was distributed for the Court's conference of December 11, 2026, and the docket does not yet show any action taken at that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Almonor is asking the Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit's decision upholding his conviction. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions about trial location and conspiracy proof. If it denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision affirming his conviction and sentence stands, and the case ends. The petition also raises the possibility of a GVR, sending the case back for reconsideration in light of a recent related decision, without deciding the questions itself.
