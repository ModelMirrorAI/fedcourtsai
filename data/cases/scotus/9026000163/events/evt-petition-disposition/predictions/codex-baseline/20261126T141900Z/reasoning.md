The first question raises the estimate above the band average. Section 1956(i)(2) permits trial where an act furthered the conspiracy. The Fourth Circuit expressly declined to require that Almonor could reasonably foresee an act in the district, even as it acknowledged that the Second Circuit requires foreseeability. The Third, Sixth and Ninth Circuits likewise reject the additional requirement. Almonor challenged venue before and during trial and specifically urged the Second Circuit rule on appeal. His own conduct was in Florida. Whether another participant’s conduct could expose him to trial in North Carolina gives the constitutional question practical force. See `questions-presented.txt`, petition pp. 13–19 and appendix pp. 4a–7a, 17a–23a.

Two considerations limit that increase. First, the government identifies no Second Circuit decision setting aside venue for lack of foreseeability, and the Court has passed on earlier petitions raising the issue. The corpus confirms that *Renteria*, No. 18-1052, was denied after one distribution. The reply fairly points out that a binding rule can change where prosecutors bring cases before a court ever has to reverse a conviction. Second, the government says the loss finding at sentencing would sustain venue even under Almonor’s test. The matched Fourth Circuit opinion in No. 24-4022 attributes the Charlotte loss to Almonor because he recruited others and could foresee that others were laundering money. It leaves open whether he could foresee anyone acting **in that district**. That distinction is Almonor’s best answer, though the parties would still dispute whether a different venue instruction could have changed the result. The opinion was unpublished, and the appendix records that no judge requested a rehearing poll. See opposition pp. 10–13, reply pp. 2–3, 7, and appendix pp. 10a–11a, 24a.

The second question asks what ties participants working with a common organizer into one conspiracy. The panel cited *Kotteakos* and the trial court gave Almonor’s requested multiple-conspiracies instruction. The government treats his recruitment of other participants as evidence of one enterprise. Almonor answers that he could recruit people into his own operation without sharing a purpose with the participant linked to Charlotte. Deciding what the evidence proved would make summary reversal unusually fact-dependent. The alternative request for reconsideration under *Abouammo* has a concrete intervening decision behind it, but that decision addressed document falsification and expressly recognized the overt-act rule for conspiracy. I assign 18% to either summary route combined, conditional on a grant. See petition pp. 27–32, opposition pp. 12–15, reply pp. 8–12, and *Abouammo*, 608 U.S. 597, 606–07.
