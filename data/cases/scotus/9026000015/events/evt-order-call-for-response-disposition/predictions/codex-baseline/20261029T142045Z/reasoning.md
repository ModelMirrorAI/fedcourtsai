## Why move substantially above the anchor?

The petition's strongest feature is the apparent conflict between accepting the sincerity and site specificity of petitioners' religious exercise and minimizing the burden because other locations remain available. QP 1 and the petition at printed pages 18–26 identify a constitutional constraint on courts' theological judgments, rather than merely requesting correction of a park-management decision. The actual panel discussion at App.17a–19a expressly acknowledges the claimant-perspective standard but then relies on park access, seasonal absence of cormorants, and nesting elsewhere. That juxtaposition makes the alleged error concrete and potentially attractive for review.

The petition reports a panel dissent and six votes against denial of rehearing, including judges with differing jurisprudential outlooks (printed pages 15–17). That supports taking the alleged burden seriously without turning circuit judges' votes into Supreme Court votes. The risk of irreversible alteration of a sacred site increases the practical importance of reviewing the preliminary-injunction decision rather than requiring final judgment first. The response request strengthens the inference that at least one Justice wants the City's explanation.

## Why denial remains more likely

First, the petition overstates its cleanliness. The operative analysis is significantly about **Texas RFRA**, whose burden test the panel distinguishes from federal RFRA and the constitutional coercion standard (App.15a–18a). The petition's proposed splits collect decisions under several different statutes and constitutional settings. Those cases may illustrate an important common principle without presenting squarely inconsistent holdings on the same federal legal question. Federal constitutional restrictions on judicial reasoning remain a possible review route, but this is not simply a federal-RFRA split case.

Second, **the alternative ground matters**. The panel says that even a substantial burden would not change its result because the City's plan satisfies strict scrutiny (App.19a). Correcting QP 1 alone therefore would not necessarily change the judgment. QP 2 must overcome a record-specific determination about feasible construction and bird-management alternatives. The appended majority rejects the petitioners' characterization of the City's admission and recounts modifications, meetings, engineering options, and testimony (App.26a–38a). The City's failure to commission a specially framed study is not self-evidently the same as having considered no alternatives whatsoever. I do not accept the petition's account as an uncontested admission.

Third, this remains an interlocutory, fact-heavy dispute about government-owned property, with limited briefing/preservation concerns expressly identified by the panel at App.16a n.7 and App.18a n.8. The panel's citations to Lyng and Navajo Nation identify an additional doctrinal complication; I rely on the staged opinion for that observation and do not claim those authorities resolve this case. These considerations leave ample room to deny review without endorsing judicial substitution of theology. The missing BIO prevents checking how sharply the City can articulate those objections, and the appendix cap limits my independent examination of the later opinion sections.
