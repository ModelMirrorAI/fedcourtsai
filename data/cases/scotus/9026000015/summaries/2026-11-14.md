## In brief

Two religious practitioners ask the Court to revive their challenge to a San Antonio park renovation project they say will destroy a sacred worship site.

## What happened

Gary Perez and Matilde Torres, leaders in the Lipan Native American Church, sued the City of San Antonio over a park renovation project affecting a riverbend in Brackenridge Park that they consider sacred. They argued the City's plans to remove trees and deter nesting cormorants would substantially burden their religious exercise, violating the Texas Religious Freedom Restoration Act and the First Amendment. A federal district court denied most of their requested injunction, and the Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding they had not shown a substantial burden and that the City's plan satisfied strict scrutiny. The Fifth Circuit denied rehearing en banc over several judges' dissents. Perez and Torres then petitioned the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The petitioners ask the Court to decide two questions: first, whether courts may reject a religious believer's own sincere understanding of what their faith requires when deciding if government action burdens that practice, rather than accepting the believer's account; and second, whether the government can show it used the least restrictive means available when it did not consider alternatives that would reduce the impact on religious exercise until after the lawsuit began.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026. The docket shows no action recorded since that conference; briefing continued afterward, with the City's brief in opposition filed October 30, 2026, and the petitioners' reply filed November 12, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Perez and Torres, who lost in the lower courts, are asking the Court to take up their case and reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling. If the Court grants cert, it would hear full arguments on both questions and decide them; granting review does not by itself mean either side wins. If the Court denies cert, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the ruling that the City's project did not substantially burden the petitioners' religious exercise and that the City's plan satisfied strict scrutiny. A denial would not mean the Supreme Court agrees with that outcome, only that the lower court's ruling remains final.
