## In brief

Two religious practitioners ask the Court to revive their claim that San Antonio's park construction plans illegally burden their worship at a sacred riverbend.

## What happened

Gary Perez and Matilde Torres are ceremonial leaders of the Lipan Native American Church who perform religious ceremonies at a bend of the San Antonio River inside a city park, involving specific trees and nesting cormorants they say are essential to the rituals. The City of San Antonio plans construction that would remove most of the surrounding trees and use deterrents to keep cormorants from nesting there. Perez and Torres sued, claiming this violates their religious freedom rights under the First Amendment and Texas law. A federal district court and, after proceedings that included a question sent to the Texas Supreme Court, the Fifth Circuit Court of Appeals both ruled against them, finding no substantial burden on their religious exercise because they could still access other parts of the park.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions. First, whether courts may second-guess a person's sincere religious understanding of what their faith requires when deciding if government action burdens that religious exercise, rather than accepting the person's own account. Second, whether the government can meet its burden of showing it used the least restrictive means available when it admits it never looked into less burdensome alternatives before the lawsuit began, only afterward.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference. The Court then asked the City of San Antonio to respond, and the city's deadline to do so has been extended twice, most recently to October 30, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Perez and Torres, who lost in the lower courts, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling against Perez and Torres stands, leaving the city free to proceed with its construction plans, though a denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was correct.
