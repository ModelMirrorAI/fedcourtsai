## In brief

Two religious practitioners ask the Court to revive their claim that a city park renovation project unlawfully burdens their ability to worship at a sacred riverbend.

## What happened

Gary Perez and Matilde Torres, members of the Lipan-Apache Native American Church, hold ceremonies at a specific bend of the San Antonio River in Brackenridge Park, a city-owned park in San Antonio. They sued the City, saying its renovation plans—repairing retaining walls, removing or relocating trees, and deterring nesting birds—would destroy features their religion requires. A federal district court partly granted and partly denied preliminary injunctive relief, and the Fifth Circuit, after several rounds of opinions and a certified question to the Texas Supreme Court, affirmed that the City's plans did not substantially burden the petitioners' religious exercise and that the City's approach survived strict scrutiny.

## What the Court is being asked

The petitioners ask the Court to decide two questions. First, whether courts may reject a religious claimant's own sincere understanding of what their religious practice requires when deciding if government action burdens that practice, rather than accepting the claimant's account and assessing the burden from there. Second, whether a government can show it used the least restrictive means available when it did not look into less burdensome alternatives until after the lawsuit was filed, rather than before.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after the City waived and then was asked to respond, filed its opposition, and the petitioners replied; the docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Perez and Torres, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case and reverse the Fifth Circuit's rulings against them. If the Court grants review, it would hear full briefing and arguments and decide the legal questions itself, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the rulings that the City's plans do not substantially burden the petitioners' religious exercise and that the City's approach satisfies strict scrutiny, and the City's renovation project could proceed under that judgment.
