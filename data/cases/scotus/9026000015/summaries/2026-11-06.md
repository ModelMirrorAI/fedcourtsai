## In brief

Two religious practitioners ask the Court to revive their claim that a city park renovation project unlawfully burdens their religious exercise at a sacred riverbend.

## What happened

Gary Perez and Matilde Torres, members of the Lipan-Apache Native American Church, have long worshipped at a riverbend in San Antonio's Brackenridge Park that they consider sacred, believing ceremonies there require specific trees and nesting cormorants. The City of San Antonio planned park renovations, funded by a 2017 bond, involving repairing retaining walls, removing or relocating trees, and deterring birds from nesting. Perez and Torres sued, claiming this violated their religious rights under the First Amendment and Texas law. A federal district court partly granted and partly denied preliminary injunctive relief; the Fifth Circuit, after several revised opinions and a certified question to the Texas Supreme Court, ultimately affirmed the denial of the broader relief, finding no substantial burden and that the City's plan survived strict scrutiny.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two things: first, whether courts may reject a religious claimant's own sincere understanding of what their religious practice requires when deciding if government action burdens that practice, or must instead accept the claimant's account; and second, whether the government can show it used the least restrictive means available when it did not consider workable alternatives until after being sued.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026. The City initially waived its right to respond but was later asked to do so, and after two extensions filed its brief in opposition on October 30, 2026. The docket shows no action since the petition was distributed.

## What each outcome would mean

Perez and Torres, who lost below, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide the legal questions, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the ruling that the City's park plan did not substantially burden Perez and Torres's religious exercise and that it satisfied strict scrutiny, so the City could proceed with the renovation project as planned.
