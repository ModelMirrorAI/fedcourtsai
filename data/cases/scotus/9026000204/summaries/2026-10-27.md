## In brief

The government asked the Supreme Court to decide whether a federal ban on home distilling of spirits is a lawful use of Congress's taxing power.

## What happened

Several hobbyists and an advocacy group sued, arguing that the federal prohibition on producing distilled spirits in homes or connected sheds and yards exceeds Congress's constitutional powers. A federal district court agreed and blocked enforcement. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding the ban is not a valid exercise of the taxing power or the Necessary and Proper Clause, which lets Congress pass laws needed to carry out its other powers. Days later, the Sixth Circuit reached the opposite conclusion in a separate case called Ream, upholding the same ban. The Department of Justice then asked the Supreme Court to review the Fifth Circuit's ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the law barring distilled-spirits production in homes and nearby structures is a necessary and proper way for Congress to carry out its power to collect taxes on spirits, or whether it goes beyond that power.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, on October 9 and October 16, 2026. The Court granted the petition on October 19, 2026; the case has not yet been briefed or argued on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, as petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's decision, which had ruled for McNutt and the other challengers. If the Court affirms, the home-distilling ban stays struck down and unenforceable against McNutt and the Hobby Distillers Association's members. If it reverses, the ban is upheld and may again be enforced against them.
