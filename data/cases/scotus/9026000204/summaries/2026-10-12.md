## In brief

The federal government asks the Supreme Court to reinstate the longstanding ban on distilling spirits at home, which a lower court struck down.

## What happened

Hobby Distillers Association and several individuals who want to distill spirits at home sued the Department of Justice and the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau, arguing that the federal ban on home distilling exceeds Congress's constitutional powers. A district court agreed and permanently blocked enforcement against them. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, holding the ban is not a valid exercise of Congress's taxing power. Eleven days later, the Sixth Circuit upheld the same ban in a different case, creating a conflict between circuits. The government then asked the Supreme Court to review the Fifth Circuit's decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law barring people from producing distilled spirits in homes or nearby structures is a valid way for Congress to carry out its power to collect taxes, even though the law does not itself impose or collect any tax.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a response from the distillers and a reply from the government, and was distributed for the Court's conference on October 9, 2026. The docket shows no action recorded since that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the home-distilling ban is constitutional, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling striking down the ban stands, leaving the distillers' win in place, at least in that circuit, while a conflicting ruling upholding the ban stands in the Sixth Circuit. A denial would not mean the Supreme Court agrees the ban is unconstitutional.
