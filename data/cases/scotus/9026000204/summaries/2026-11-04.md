## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to uphold its longstanding ban on home distilling after a lower court struck it down.

## What happened

Federal law has long barred producing distilled spirits in homes or nearby structures, backed by criminal penalties. Scott McNutt and other hobbyists, along with the Hobby Distillers Association, sued, arguing the ban exceeds Congress's power. A district court agreed and blocked enforcement against them. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, ruling the prohibition does not properly carry out Congress's taxing power. Shortly afterward, the Sixth Circuit, in a separate case called Ream, upheld the same law. The Department of Justice then asked the Supreme Court to review the Fifth Circuit's decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether Congress's power to tax supports a ban on producing distilled spirits at certain locations, including homes, as a legitimate way of helping collect the alcohol tax, or whether that ban goes beyond what the taxing power allows.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court granted it on October 19, 2026. The case has not yet been briefed on the merits, argued, or decided; a recent filing asks to skip preparing a joint appendix of record materials.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government is asking the Court to reverse the lower court and uphold the ban; McNutt and the other respondents, who won below, are defending the Fifth Circuit's ruling. If the Court affirms, the ban would remain unenforceable against McNutt and the Association's members, as the lower courts ordered. If the Court reverses, the ban would be upheld as constitutional, allowing its enforcement against them. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Fifth Circuit's decision and send the case back without resolving the constitutional question for either side.
