## In brief

The federal government asked the Supreme Court to uphold its longstanding ban on distilling spirits at home, after a lower court struck the ban down.

## What happened

Hobby distillers, including Scott McNutt and the Hobby Distillers Association, sued the Department of Justice and the Alcohol and Tobacco Tax and Trade Bureau, arguing that the federal ban on producing distilled spirits in homes and connected areas goes beyond Congress's constitutional powers. A district court agreed and blocked enforcement against them. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, ruling the prohibition does not properly carry out Congress's power to tax, since it reduces rather than raises revenue and intrudes on authority reserved to the states. A different appeals court, the Sixth Circuit, had reached the opposite conclusion in a separate case called Ream. The government then asked the Supreme Court to review the Fifth Circuit's decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law banning home distilling, which Congress passed alongside its tax on distilled spirits, is a valid way of carrying out Congress's power to collect that tax, or whether it goes beyond what that taxing power allows.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted the government's petition on October 19, 2026. The case has not yet been briefed on the merits or argued; the Court recently granted a motion to skip printing a joint appendix of record materials.

## What each outcome would mean

The government is asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling, which favored McNutt and the other distillers. If the Court reverses, the home-distilling ban would be upheld and enforceable against them. If it affirms, the lower court's decision striking down the ban as unconstitutional would stand, and McNutt and the association would remain free from its enforcement. If the Court vacates the decision, it would send the case back without resolving the ban's constitutionality for either side.
