# Forecast of the Court's judgment

The Court will **reverse** the Fifth Circuit's judgment and remand for proceedings consistent with upholding the challenged home-distilling restriction. I predict a **7–2** decision: Roberts, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh, Barrett, and Jackson in the majority; Thomas and Gorsuch dissenting. The probability that the judgment below is disturbed, including vacatur or a mixed judgment as alternatives to reversal, is **0.82**.

The majority will hold that Congress may use the Necessary and Proper Clause to protect collection of the distilled-spirits excise tax by channeling production into locations amenable to inspection. It will reject the Fifth Circuit's reasoning that prohibiting some otherwise taxable production necessarily defeats a tax-collection justification. A restriction can reduce the nominal tax base while improving effective collection. The forecast follows the rival positions in the provisioned petition, pp. 14–20, and opposition, pp. 10–12.

The majority will apply the latitude for revenue-protective regulation illustrated by *Felsenheld v. United States*, 186 U.S. 126, 132–33 (1902). It will distinguish *United States v. Dewitt*, 76 U.S. 41, 44, on the ground that suppressing an untaxed competing product to increase sales of taxed products is different from regulating production of the taxed article itself. Those are forecast applications, not holdings already issued in this case; the historical opinions were checked through CourtListener.

The holding will be limited to production-site controls within the established spirits-tax scheme. It will not recognize a general federal power to prohibit activities in the home merely because they could conceal taxable conduct. The historical anti-evasion justification and direct connection to the taxed commodity will supply the limiting principle. The Court will resolve the taxing-power question without overruling *Gonzales v. Raich* or deciding an independent Commerce Clause justification, which the government's provisioned reply, pp. 2–4, expressly declines to advance.

The dissent will argue that a categorical residential prohibition goes beyond facilitating collection and intrudes on state police powers, particularly where a willing hobby distiller could register and submit to inspection. It will emphasize the availability of less sweeping collection measures and the danger of transforming a tax into general regulatory authority. I do not forecast an author or separate concurrence.

I expect a plenary decision during the remainder of October Term 2026, most likely by June 2027. A DIG is a small residual possibility, not the central forecast: the provisioned petition's standing account identifies a concrete enforcement warning and planned conduct, and the grant presents an agreed circuit conflict. No recusal appears in the supplied record, so an equally divided affirmance is especially unlikely. Timing and writing predictions are contextual, not additional scored claims.
