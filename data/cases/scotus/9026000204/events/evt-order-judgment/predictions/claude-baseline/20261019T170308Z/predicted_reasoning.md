# Forecast: DOJ v. McNutt (No. 26-204), judgment after the grant

**Judgment: reversed.** The Court reverses the Fifth Circuit and holds that the
federal ban on distilling spirits in or next to a dwelling house (26 U.S.C.
5178(a)(1)(B), enforced through 5601(a)(6)) is a valid exercise of Congress's
power under the Necessary and Proper Clause, as a way of carrying the taxing
power into execution. A plain reversal is likelier than a vacatur and remand.
If the government wins, nothing is left for remand: the government has
abandoned its commerce-power defense, and the respondents' standing (McNutt's
at least) was never seriously disputed. So the Court reverses outright.

**Vote: 7–2.** The majority is Roberts, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Kavanaugh,
Barrett and Jackson. Thomas and Gorsuch dissent.
- Thomas has dissented before from broad readings of the Necessary and Proper
  Clause (Comstock, Raich), so he is the most likely dissenter.
- Gorsuch's skepticism of federal criminal reach into the home makes him the
  second.
- Barrett or Kavanaugh is the most likely third vote for affirmance.
- A 6–3 split is the main alternative. An affirmance would most likely be 5–4
  or 6–3 and rest on an NFIB-style limit: a ban on a taxable activity is not
  "proper" to raising revenue from it.

**Ground.** The majority applies the McCulloch/Comstock means-end test. It
relies on:
- the Felsenheld line, which lets Congress impose any reasonable rule on the
  manufacture of goods subject to an internal-revenue tax;
- the documented, rampant evasion of the spirits tax when the ban was enacted
  in 1868;
- the ban's unbroken 160-year history as evidence that it is constitutional.

It rejects the Fifth Circuit's reasoning that an outright ban on a taxed
activity cannot be "plainly adapted" to collecting the tax. Location rules
make stills inspectable, and the inspection system is what makes the tax
collectable.

**Breadth.** The ground is narrow and tied to excise-taxed goods. The Court
says expressly that it does not reach the commerce-power question, which the
government waived, and leaves Gonzales v. Raich alone. A separate writing
(likely Kavanaugh or Barrett) may stress the historical pedigree as limiting
the holding. The Ream petition (No. 26-93) is held and then GVR'd or denied
after this decision.

**Procedural exits.** A DIG is very unlikely: both sides asked the Court to
decide the question and the vehicle is clean. An equally divided Court is
unlikely, since no recusal is apparent.
