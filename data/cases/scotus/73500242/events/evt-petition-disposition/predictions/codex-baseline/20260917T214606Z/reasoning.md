## Legal and vehicle assessment

The strongest affirmative argument is substantive rather than merely sympathetic. The petition, pages 9–11, argues that Section 1981(b)'s protection of contractual benefits and conditions is not exhausted by allowing a customer to finish eating. A discriminatory dress-code condition could plausibly present a recurring commercial civil-rights question. The Fifth Circuit appendix, pages 5a–7a, confines its analysis to whether the alleged discrimination concerned a protected contractual activity; it does not decide the remaining burden-shifting steps. I therefore do not treat the petition's account as an adjudicated finding of racial intent or assume that a favorable statutory ruling would establish ultimate liability.

The petition's characterization of a categorical requirement to leave is also broader than the panel's wording. The panel recognizes benefits beyond food, but finds neither loss of a cognizable benefit nor sufficiently severe harassment on this record. Resolving the case could therefore require untangling the dress-code terms, the manner of placing the shawl, and competing descriptions of the encounter, rather than answering a clean departure-required rule in isolation. Sources: petition pages 3–11 and appendix 2a–7a.

The historical comparison supplies genuine doctrinal tension but not a verified square split on these facts. Christian, 252 F.3d at 872–73, uses a commercial-establishment framework allowing objectively discriminatory, markedly hostile service to support the claim. However, its application at 878–79 involved a shopper forced to leave and abandon her purchase. The retrieved passages therefore do not establish that the Sixth Circuit has sustained this petitioner's particular completed-meal claim. The petition's additional circuit citations have not been independently checked. This limits the upward adjustment for conflict.

The alternative summary-judgment argument is not frivolous: petition pages 7–9 invokes Tolan and identifies disputed treatment of the dress code, physical contact, and time spent at the restaurant. But I do not equate an asserted record error with a likely summary reversal. My 30% cert-order-route probability is conditional on the small 2.5% grant probability; it is not a 30% unconditional chance of summary relief. The increments and denial-writing probability are judgmental forecasts, not estimates from unavailable conditioned tables.
