## In brief

Shipbuilders ask the Court to review a ruling that let former employees' decades-old wage-suppression lawsuit proceed despite the usual time limit on antitrust claims.

## What happened

Susan Scharpf and another former naval engineer sued General Dynamics, Huntington Ingalls, and other shipbuilders and consultancies, claiming they secretly agreed for decades not to recruit each other's engineers, suppressing wages. Because the named plaintiffs stopped working in the industry years earlier, the lawsuit was filed well after the four-year deadline for antitrust claims unless that deadline was paused. A federal district court dismissed the case as too late, finding the companies' unwritten, undocumented agreement did not count as the kind of active cover-up needed to pause the clock. A divided federal appeals panel reversed, holding that an agreement deliberately kept off paper can count as concealment; one judge dissented. The appeals court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether people suing over a secret business agreement adequately show that the other side hid it - which would pause the deadline for suing - merely by alleging the agreement was never written down, or whether more active steps to mislead people are required.

## Where it stands

The shipbuilders' petition asking the Court to hear the case has been fully briefed and was distributed for the Court's January 9, 2026 conference. The Court then invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views, and the docket shows no action since. A later filing asked that the petition be held while that invitation is pending.

## What each outcome would mean

General Dynamics and the other shipbuilders, who lost before the appeals court, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear the case fully and decide the legal question; granting does not decide who is right. If it denies the petition, the appeals court's ruling stands, letting Scharpf's lawsuit go forward in the lower courts. Because the Court first asked for the government's views, it will likely wait for that input before deciding whether to grant or deny.
