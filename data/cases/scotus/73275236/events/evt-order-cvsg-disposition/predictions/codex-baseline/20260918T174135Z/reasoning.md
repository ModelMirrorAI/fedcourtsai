## Why 30%, rather than the approximately 35% anchor

The strongest grant-side evidence is the Court's actual invitation, reinforced by a divided appellate ruling and two docketed industry amicus filings. The petition identifies a recurring federal limitations issue with potentially substantial consequences for old antitrust claims and treble damages. The alleged no-poach arrangement involves naval-engineering employers, making both competition policy and federal procurement plausible subjects for the United States' assessment. That last point is an inference about why views could help, not a claim about the unprovided government's position. I have not read the amici's text and use their filings only as an attention signal.

The grant-side substantive account comes from the petition, especially printed pages 1-3, 8-14 and 33-34: the majority allegedly allows an ordinary characteristic of conspiracy, its unwritten nature, to defeat a congressionally specified limitations period; Chief Judge Diaz dissented; and Fifth, Sixth and Ninth Circuit decisions allegedly demand something more affirmative. Petitioners argue that interlocutory review is justified by discovery burdens and settlement pressure, invoking pleading-stage precedents rather than claiming final judgment below.

The opposition materially weakens the clean-conflict story. Its printed pages 13-16 and 18-24 argue that the Fourth Circuit required intentional concealment, not just an unwritten agreement, and relied on coded language and other conduct. Its vehicle discussion at pages 25-26 identifies additional alleged representations and limited written teaming agreements that could support concealment independently. Those allegations are advocacy, not established facts, but they undermine the prospect of a categorical answer resolving the case cleanly. The appeal reversed a dismissal and remanded, leaving factual development ahead.

I independently checked one central comparator rather than treating the briefs' descriptions as verified holdings. CourtListener's March 2, 2012 opinion in Carrier Corporation v. Outokumpu Oyj, 673 F.3d 430, 446-47, distinguishes inadequately particularized allegations of secret meetings from detailed allegations of rules preventing a paper trail and coded identities. The latter were sufficient affirmative concealment. This supports the opposition's position that the difference may concern factual allegations and pleading particularity rather than incompatible legal tests. It does not establish that the Fourth Circuit applied the test correctly or disprove every asserted conflict. I did not independently verify the Fifth and Ninth Circuit authorities.

Balancing those points, I make a modest net downward adjustment to 30%. The CVSG prevents treating this as an ordinary factbound petition with negligible odds, but it is not a vote to grant. A later government recommendation favoring review could substantially change the assessment; it is unobserved here. I do not turn an anticipated pro- or anti-enforcement policy preference into an assumed recommendation.

- **Writing given denial, 7%:** a reasoned public disagreement is possible after a divided lower-court ruling, but no particular Justice's position is observable here. The probability concerns aggregated existence only and is conditional on denial.
- **Significance, 0.58:** meaningful nationwide implications for antitrust tolling, employees and major contractors, moderated by a technical question and interlocutory posture. This score measures stakes if decided, not grant likelihood.

The largest limitations are the unknown Solicitor General recommendation, the disputed fit between the question and the complaint, and the absence of independently retrieved lower-court opinion text. General web retrieval attempts returned no usable content; they supplied no evidence. The forecast remains grounded in the provided pre-decision materials, the committed aggregates, and the single historical comparator.
