## In brief

A doctor convicted of illegally prescribing opioids asks the Court to decide how appeals courts should assess whether a flawed jury instruction on his intent was harmless.

## What happened

Frank Parasmo, a New York physician, was convicted at trial of unlawfully distributing opioids to patients. After his conviction, the Supreme Court ruled in another case that such prosecutions require proof the doctor subjectively knew he was prescribing improperly, not just that his conduct was objectively unreasonable. Parasmo argued the jury in his case had been wrongly told to apply the objective standard. The Second Circuit agreed the instruction was flawed but, over a dissent, ruled the error was harmless because it found the evidence of guilt overwhelming. The dissenting judge argued the court had improperly weighed contested evidence itself rather than leaving that to a jury.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide how appeals courts should handle a situation where a jury was told to use the wrong legal standard on a factual question the defendant disputed at trial. Specifically, may an appeals court excuse that mistake by concluding on its own that the evidence against the defendant was strong, or must it instead ask only whether the defendant presented enough evidence that a jury could have ruled the other way, without the appeals court weighing whose evidence was stronger.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference, and the Court later asked for a response from the government despite an earlier waiver; the government's response deadline was extended to December 4, 2026. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Parasmo, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on the question and decide it, without yet determining who wins. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling upholding Parasmo's conviction stands, and the case does not return to the Supreme Court.
