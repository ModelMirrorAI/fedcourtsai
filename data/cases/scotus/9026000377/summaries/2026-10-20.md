## In brief

A doctor convicted of illegally prescribing opioids asks the Court to decide how appeals courts should judge whether a flawed jury instruction on intent was harmless.

## What happened

Frank Parasmo, a New York physician, was convicted at trial on 32 counts of unlawfully distributing opioids. After his conviction, the Supreme Court ruled in another case that juries must find a doctor subjectively knew he was prescribing improperly, not merely that his conduct was objectively unreasonable. Parasmo argued his jury had been wrongly instructed under the old, objective standard. The Second Circuit agreed the instruction was flawed but, in a divided decision, ruled the error harmless because it found the evidence of guilt overwhelming. A dissenting judge argued the majority had improperly weighed the evidence itself rather than asking whether Parasmo had plausibly disputed his intent, a role reserved for the jury.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide which test appellate courts should use when a jury was wrongly instructed on a contested element of a crime: whether judges may themselves weigh how strong the government's evidence is and excuse the error on that basis, or whether they instead may only ask if the defendant presented evidence that could rationally have led a jury to a different result.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 16, 2026 conference. After that conference, the Court asked the government to respond by November 4, 2026; the docket shows no further action since.

## What each outcome would mean

Parasmo, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide which legal standard applies, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling affirming Parasmo's conviction stands, and the case does not return to the Supreme Court. A denial would not mean the Court agrees that the lower court's approach was correct, only that the conviction remains in place.
