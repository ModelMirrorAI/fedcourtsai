## In brief

A doctor convicted of illegally prescribing opioids asks the Court to decide how appeals courts should review jury-instruction errors that go uncorrected.

## What happened

Frank Parasmo, a New York physician, was convicted at trial on 32 counts of unlawfully distributing opioids. After his conviction, the Supreme Court ruled in another case that prosecutors must prove a doctor knew his prescriptions were unauthorized, not merely that they were objectively unreasonable. Parasmo argued his jury had been wrongly instructed under the old, objective standard. The Second Circuit agreed the instruction was flawed but, in a divided decision, ruled the error harmless because it found the evidence of guilt overwhelming. A dissenting judge argued the error was not harmless because Parasmo had contested his intent and presented evidence a jury could have credited.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether an appeals court, reviewing a conviction after a flawed jury instruction on a disputed element of the crime, may excuse the error by weighing the trial evidence itself and concluding the government's case was strong—or whether it must instead ask only whether the defendant presented enough evidence that a jury could rationally have ruled the other way, leaving that evidentiary judgment to a jury rather than judges.

## Where it stands

The United States, as respondent, waived its right to respond to the petition, but the Court later asked for a response, due November 4, 2026. The petition was distributed for the Court's October 16, 2026 conference, and the docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Parasmo, who lost in the Second Circuit, is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on how courts must evaluate these instructional errors, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Parasmo's conviction and the harmless-error finding in place. Either way, no decision has yet been made on the underlying legal question.
