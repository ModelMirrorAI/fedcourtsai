## Anchor and adjustments

The frozen sal-v4 **baseline** cert anchor is **0.050890782339271884**, about 5.09%. These are private petitioners; the state court named as respondent does not turn them into a state petitioner. For an arrival forecast the appropriate population is the private caption class's baseline **reached** risk set, not petitions that ultimately remained baseline or ended without relisting. The committed `metrics/statpack.md` table agrees on the population: prior-Term baseline reached rates span roughly 4.0% to 5.9%, with annual weighted denominators between 1,196 and 1,740 for Terms 2017–2025. I use the exact frozen pooled rate, not an average of rounded table entries, and exclude the current Term as an anchor.

I adjust substantially downward for this record's lack of a developed decisional conflict and its unusually difficult procedural vehicle. The question has genuine general importance: whether attorney-only electronic access, combined with degraded paper intake, denies self-represented parties constitutionally adequate treatment. The filing rule and paired exhibits make that concern concrete. But the petition's reasons-for-granting section principally argues an important unresolved question, not conflicting appellate holdings. Different administrative practices across states are not by themselves a conflict about constitutional law. The petition's reliance on access-to-courts authorities supports an argument; it does not establish that those decisions already dictate the requested electronic-access remedy. See petition pp. 14–24 and appendix pp. 42, 49–60.

The principal obstacle is the **judgment actually under review**. The May 21, 2026 state supreme court order declines original mandamus jurisdiction because a May 28 hearing was scheduled, citing Rule 245 and *Key v. Currie*, 305 S.C. 115, 406 S.E.2d 356 (1991). It discusses docket management and possible filing restrictions, not a constitutional holding approving attorney-only electronic filing. I verified *Key* through CourtListener: it restricts original jurisdiction where ordinary trial-court proceedings are available, absent an extraordinary reason. That reinforces a potential independent state procedural ground. It is not a definitive determination that Supreme Court jurisdiction is absent here. See appendix pp. 1–3 and the retrieved *Key* opinion.

The petition asserts that its federal question was raised and passed on, but the cited state order does not expressly decide the evidentiary-fidelity question. General references to unequal paper-filing burdens do not eliminate uncertainty about preservation of the specific constitutional remedy now requested. A completed original proceeding also does not, by itself, establish that this is a suitable vehicle to decide a question tied to the separate civil action. The claimed injury, relief sought in mandamus, subsequent hearing, and availability of ordinary review create substantial redressability, continuing-injury, and finality questions. I treat these as risks, not proven jurisdictional defects.

The August 3 trial-court order further weakens the asserted clean causal chain. It postdates the May judgment being reviewed, and petitioners describe its separate review track. Its appendix text also supplies grounds besides the photographic rendering: dismissal as a sanction and denial of amendment as an additional sanction, with alternative futility reasoning. The order evaluates the pleaded privacy claim; it does not expressly find that a scanner caused the adverse result. Petitioners' claim that the intact photograph would change the outcome is therefore advocacy, not an established finding. See petition pp. 12–14, 22–23; appendix pp. 29–31. This distinction is central to the downward adjustment, not an endorsement of the sanctions or filing policy.

These compounded obstacles justify reducing the 5.09% population anchor to 0.3%. That remains nonzero because meaningful access and evidence integrity could attract attention and because this record includes a documented administrative rule rather than only a generalized grievance. The calibration is judgmental; the retrieved corpus sample does not estimate the grant rate for this precise procedural class.

## Other claims and uncertainty

The mechanical `relist-increment` claim is **0.99**: from the observed count of zero, even the first ordinary distribution satisfies it. It is emphatically not a 99% chance of a second conference. I assign only about **2%** to a repeat distribution after the first, allowing for rescheduling as well as genuine relisting. The mode is one total distribution, followed by denial.

For shape, the statpack's paid-segment relist cut reports 10,204 resolved petitions in bucket 0, 2,532 in bucket 1, 495 in bucket 2, and 482 in bucket 3+. Grant-family shares are roughly 1.8%, 13.5%, 41.2%, and 37.1%, respectively. These are terminal buckets, not forward hazards or substitutes for the arrival anchor. The CVSG cut reports 164 resolved CVSG petitions with about 34.8% grant-family outcomes, versus 13,549 without a CVSG and about 6.4%. I do not multiply these overlapping signals. The absence of a federal party or a developed federal-program issue, together with the vehicle concerns, supports **0.001** for a future CVSG.

There are no amicus briefs in the single-entry snapshot; this is not a forecast of ultimate support. The statpack's zero-amicus bucket has n=11,109 resolved and about 3.8% grant-family outcomes, but counts support at disposition, so I use it only as context, not as an additional independent penalty.

Conditional on a grant, **0.15** goes to disposition in the grant order, leaving **0.85** for ordinary merits proceedings. No provisioned companion or intervening authority supplies a concrete GVR trigger, and the requested constitutional extension is not an obvious summary reversal. Conditional on denial, **0.006** goes to a noted dissent or statement. The per-Justice writing probabilities are small, unconditional marginal forecasts, not votes or a distribution summing to one. A noted dissent need not include a written opinion, and I expect no separate writing.

The **0.35 stakes score** reflects potentially broad consequences for self-represented litigants if the constitutional question were decided, independently of the very low chance that this petition supplies the vehicle.
