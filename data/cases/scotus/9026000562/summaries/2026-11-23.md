## In brief

Two self-represented former tenants ask the Court to review South Carolina court rules that route non-lawyers' filings through paper scanning, arguing it degraded evidence in their dismissed lawsuit.

## What happened

James McNeil and Meaghan Poyer, representing themselves, sued their former landlord and property managers in South Carolina state court over a security deposit, alleged safety problems, and other claims. After extensive motions practice, the trial court dismissed the entire case with prejudice, partly as a sanction for what it called abusive filing conduct. Before that, McNeil and Poyer had asked the South Carolina Supreme Court to order the trial court to rule on pending motions; that court declined, saying a hearing was already scheduled and cautioning the pair about excessive filings. McNeil and Poyer then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review the South Carolina Supreme Court's order.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state violates self-represented litigants' constitutional rights to fair treatment and a meaningful hearing by letting only lawyers file documents electronically, while requiring non-lawyers to submit paper that court scanners convert into lower-quality, hard-to-search images.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and the respondent's response was due November 27, 2026. The petitioners filed a supplemental brief on November 13, 2026, asking the Court to seek a response if none is filed and arguing for various outcomes; the docket does not yet show the Court's action on the petition itself.

## What each outcome would mean

McNeil and Poyer, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case and overturn the result below. If the Court denies review, the South Carolina Supreme Court's order stands, leaving in place the lower court's dismissal of their lawsuit and its handling of their filings. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the electronic-filing question itself, which could result in affirming the state court's order, reversing it in the petitioners' favor, or vacating it and sending the case back for another look without deciding who is right.
