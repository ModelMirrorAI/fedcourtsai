## In brief

Two women ask the Court to revive their civil rights lawsuit against prosecutors and officials over a criminal case that Tennessee courts later said was not actually a crime.

## What happened

Connie Reguli and Wendy Hancock were prosecuted in Tennessee after Hancock kept her child from state custody during a dependency dispute; Reguli was Hancock's attorney. Tennessee appellate courts later vacated both convictions, finding the charged conduct did not meet the statute's terms. Reguli and Hancock then sued the prosecutors, police officials, a state agency lawyer, and the city, alleging the officials knowingly brought charges that omitted an essential element of the crime. The district court dismissed all claims, citing prosecutorial and qualified immunity. The Sixth Circuit affirmed, reasoning in part that the officials may have made a reasonable mistake about what the law required.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether officials who bring charges by leaving out a required part of a criminal law can still be shielded from liability by being treated as having made a reasonable legal mistake. It is also asked whether prosecutors who draft an indictment this way can claim the broad immunity usually given to advocacy work, and whether an appeals court may decide a case using a legal theory neither side argued or briefed, based on facts it altered from what the plaintiffs had alleged.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed on October 9, 2026, and the response was due November 16, 2026. All named respondents have since filed waivers of their right to respond, and the docket shows no further action since.

## What each outcome would mean

Reguli and Hancock, as petitioners, are asking the Court to overturn the Sixth Circuit's ruling, which affirmed dismissal of their suit and left the lower court's judgment for the officials and the city in place. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on these immunity and procedure questions and decide the legal issues itself, without presuming either side correct. If it denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision affirming dismissal of the lawsuit stands, and the case ends in the officials' favor.
