## In brief

Two women convicted of a crime later held not to exist ask the Court to revive their civil rights lawsuit against the prosecutors and officials involved.

## What happened

Connie Reguli, a lawyer, and her client Wendy Hancock were prosecuted and convicted under a Tennessee custodial-interference law after Hancock kept her daughter from state custody during a dependency dispute. Tennessee appellate courts later vacated both convictions, holding the charged conduct did not fit the statute as written. Reguli and Hancock then sued the prosecutors, police officials, a state child-welfare lawyer, and the city, claiming malicious prosecution and conspiracy. The district court dismissed all claims on immunity and pleading grounds, and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, reasoning in part that officials had made a reasonable mistake about what the law required.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks the Court to decide whether officials who leave an essential requirement out of a criminal charge, allegedly knowing the conduct wasn't a crime, can still be shielded by immunity because a court later calls it a reasonable misunderstanding of the law. It also asks whether prosecutors who draft such a charge can claim the strongest form of immunity reserved for courtroom advocacy, and whether an appeals court may adopt a legal theory neither side argued and recast the facts in a way that favors the defendants at an early stage of the case, before any evidence is taken.

## Where it stands

The case is a petition asking the Supreme Court to take it up. It was filed and docketed on October 15, 2026, with the respondents' response due November 16, 2026; the docket shows no further action yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Reguli and Hancock, as petitioners, are asking the Court to hear their case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the immunity and procedural questions itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies the petition, the Sixth Circuit's ruling stands, leaving the dismissal of Reguli and Hancock's claims against the prosecutors, officials, and city in place.
