## In brief

Connie Reguli and Wendy Hancock ask the Court to revive civil rights claims against Tennessee officials and prosecutors after their criminal convictions were overturned.

## What happened

Reguli, a lawyer, and her client Hancock were prosecuted in Tennessee for custodial interference after Hancock kept her daughter from child-welfare officials during a custody dispute. Both were convicted, but Tennessee's appeals court later threw out the convictions, ruling the indictment had left out a required element of the crime. Reguli and Hancock then sued the prosecutors, police officials, a state attorney, and the City of Brentwood, claiming the prosecution violated their constitutional rights. A federal district court dismissed all claims, finding the officials were protected by immunity and that there was still probable cause to prosecute. The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed that dismissal.

## What the Court is being asked

The petitioners ask the Court to decide whether officials who leave out a required part of a criminal law when charging someone can still be shielded from liability by treating their actions as a reasonable mistake about what the law covers, rather than as creating a prosecution without adequate legal basis. They also ask whether prosecutors who help write such a charge can claim the strongest form of immunity, and whether the appeals court could adopt this reasoning on its own, without either side having argued it, when reviewing the case at the early dismissal stage.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed: the city and two officials filed a brief opposing review, and the other respondents waived their right to respond. The docket shows no further action since that opposition brief was filed.

## What each outcome would mean

Reguli and Hancock, who lost in the lower courts, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, leaving the dismissal of all claims against the officials and the city in place. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether the lower court's immunity reasoning was proper, deciding the legal question itself rather than resolving who ultimately wins the underlying dispute.
