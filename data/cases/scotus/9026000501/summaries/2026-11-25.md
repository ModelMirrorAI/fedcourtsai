## In brief

Two women whose criminal convictions were later overturned ask the Court to revive their lawsuit against police, prosecutors, and a state official over their prosecution.

## What happened

Connie Reguli, an attorney, and her client Wendy Hancock were indicted and convicted under a Tennessee custodial-interference law after Hancock kept her daughter from state custody. Tennessee appellate courts later threw out both convictions, ruling the indictment had omitted a required element of the crime. Reguli and Hancock then sued the investigating officers, two prosecutors, a state attorney, and the city, claiming malicious prosecution and conspiracy. The district court dismissed all claims, citing qualified and absolute immunity and finding no lack of probable cause. The Sixth Circuit affirmed, reasoning that the officers may have made a reasonable mistake about what the law required.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether officials can be shielded from liability for pursuing charges by treating their alleged removal of a required element of a crime from a charging document as a reasonable misunderstanding of the law, rather than as fabricating a crime. It is also asked whether prosecutors can claim full protection from suit for preparing an indictment under these circumstances, and whether the appeals court could rely on a legal theory about a mistaken reading of the law that neither side had argued.

## Where it stands

The respondents other than the City of Brentwood, Lori Russ, and David O'Neil have waived their right to respond. Those three respondents filed a brief opposing review. The petition has not yet been distributed for a conference, and the docket shows no further action since that filing.

## What each outcome would mean

Reguli and Hancock, who lost in the lower courts, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the dismissal of the lawsuit and the finding that the officials are protected from these claims.
