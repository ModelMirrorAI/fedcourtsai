## In brief

The government asked the Court to decide when prolonged immigration detention without a bond hearing violates due process, but the case was later dismissed.

## What happened

Carol Williams Black and Keisy G.M. are lawful permanent residents who were detained by immigration authorities under a law requiring detention of certain noncitizens with criminal convictions, without any hearing on whether they posed a flight risk or danger. Black was detained seven months before a court ordered a bond hearing; G.M. was detained twenty-one months. Lower courts split: one judge granted Black relief, another denied G.M.'s petition. The Second Circuit ruled that both men's detentions had become "unreasonably prolonged," entitling them to bond hearings, and that the government had to justify Black's continued detention by clear and convincing evidence. The government asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The question was whether, and at what point, detention under this mandatory-detention law becomes long enough that the Constitution requires giving the detained person a hearing on flight risk and danger, and if so, whether the government must prove the need for continued detention by a heightened standard of evidence. The Court also added a question of its own: whether G.M.'s case had become moot.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to G.M.'s case, and the case was briefed and scheduled for argument. Shortly before argument, G.M. received a pardon for the conviction underlying his case, and the parties jointly stipulated to dismiss the case; the docket shows it was dismissed under the Court's rule governing stipulated dismissals.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, had asked the Court to reverse the Second Circuit's ruling in favor of G.M. and Black. With the case dismissed by joint stipulation, no decision was issued on whether prolonged detention without a bond hearing violates due process or on what standard would apply at such a hearing. The Second Circuit's decision in G.M.'s case is not disturbed by any Supreme Court ruling, but because the case became moot after G.M.'s pardon, the underlying legal questions remain unresolved for other cases and detainees going forward.
