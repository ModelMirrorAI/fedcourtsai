## In brief

The government asked the Supreme Court to decide whether immigrants held in prolonged mandatory immigration detention are entitled to a bond hearing, but dismissed the case before argument.

## What happened

Carol Black and Keisy G.M., both lawful permanent residents, were held under a law requiring detention of certain immigrants with criminal convictions while their removal cases proceeded, without any hearing on whether they posed a flight risk or danger. Black was detained seven months, G.M. twenty-one months. Both sued for release. Lower courts split: one ordered Black released after a hearing where the government had to prove by clear and convincing evidence that detention was still justified; the other denied G.M. relief. The appeals court ruled for both men, holding that prolonged detention without a bond hearing violates due process, and the government asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether there is a point at which this kind of mandatory immigration detention becomes so long that the Constitution requires giving the detained person a hearing on flight risk and danger, and if so, whether the government must prove by a demanding evidentiary standard that continued detention is justified. The Court also added a question of its own: whether G.M.'s case was still alive given subsequent developments, including a pardon of the conviction underlying his case.

## Where it stands

Shortly before argument, the government notified the Court it intended to dismiss the case, the parties filed a joint stipulation to dismiss, and the case was dismissed under the Court's rule allowing parties to end a case by agreement.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, sought to overturn the appeals court ruling favoring Black and G.M. Because the parties jointly dismissed the case before decision, the Supreme Court did not rule on whether prolonged detention requires a bond hearing or who bears the burden of proof. The lower court's decision favoring Black and G.M. remains in place, but only as to their own cases; it sets no nationwide Supreme Court precedent, and the legal question remains open for future cases in other courts.
