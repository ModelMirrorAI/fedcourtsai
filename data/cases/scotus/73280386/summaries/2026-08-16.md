## In brief

The government asked the Court to decide whether immigrants held under a mandatory-detention law are entitled to bond hearings after prolonged confinement, and the Court granted review.

## What happened

Carol Williams Black and Keisy G.M., both lawful permanent residents, were detained by immigration authorities under a law requiring detention of certain noncitizens facing removal for past crimes, without any hearing on whether they posed a flight risk or danger. Black was held seven months, G.M. twenty-one months. Both won habeas corpus rulings ordering bond hearings; the Second Circuit affirmed for Black and, in G.M.'s case, sent the matter back for further proceedings, finding their prolonged detention without a hearing raised due process concerns. The government appealed to the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions: first, whether there comes a point when holding someone under this mandatory-detention law without a hearing becomes long enough that the Constitution requires giving them a bond hearing; second, if so, whether the government must prove by clear and convincing evidence that continued detention is justified, rather than placing that burden on the detained person.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari as to G.M.'s case, adding a question about whether his case is now moot, and the case has been briefed and set for argument on October 13, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, represented by immigration officials, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court. If the Court affirms, the requirement of bond hearings, and in Black's case the higher evidence burden on the government, would stand. If it reverses, that requirement would be eliminated, letting mandatory detention continue without such hearings. If it vacates, the case would return to the lower court without a final decision on these questions, including on whether G.M.'s case remains live.
