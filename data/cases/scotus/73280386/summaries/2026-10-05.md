## In brief

The government and a detained lawful permanent resident disputed whether prolonged immigration detention requires a bond hearing; the case was later dismissed as moot.

## What happened

Keisy G.M., a lawful permanent resident, was detained by immigration authorities for twenty-one months without a hearing on whether he posed a flight risk or danger, under a law requiring detention of certain noncitizens with qualifying criminal convictions pending removal proceedings. He sued, arguing the Constitution entitled him to a bond hearing. A companion case involved another detained resident, Carol Black. The appeals court ruled that prolonged detention without such a hearing violated due process guarantees, and ordered hearings; in Black's case it also required the government to prove by a heightened standard that detention was justified. The government asked the Supreme Court to review both rulings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether there is a point at which this kind of detention, while removal proceedings are ongoing, becomes long enough that the Constitution requires a hearing on flight risk and danger, and if so, whether the government must prove at that hearing, by a demanding standard, that continued detention is justified. The Court also asked the parties to address whether G.M.'s case had become moot.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review as to G.M.'s case in June 2026 and the case was briefed, argued, and scheduled for oral argument on October 13, 2026. Before argument occurred, G.M. received a pardon of his underlying conviction, the government notified the Court of a forthcoming dismissal, both sides filed a joint stipulation to dismiss, and the case was dismissed in September 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, as petitioner, sought to overturn the lower court's rulings favoring G.M. and Black. Had the Court reached a decision, affirming would have upheld the requirement of bond hearings, and in Black's case the heightened evidentiary burden on the government; reversing would have eliminated that requirement, letting mandatory detention continue without such hearings; vacating would have erased the lower court's decision without resolving the question for either side. Instead, the case was dismissed by agreement of the parties, leaving no ruling from the Supreme Court on the questions presented, while the lower court's decision in G.M.'s favor remains undisturbed by this Court.
