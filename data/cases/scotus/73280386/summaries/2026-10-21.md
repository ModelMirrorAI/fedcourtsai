## In brief

The government and an immigrant detainee disputed whether prolonged immigration detention without a bond hearing violates due process; the Court dismissed the case after a pardon mooted it.

## What happened

Keisy G.M., a lawful permanent resident, was detained by immigration authorities for 21 months under a law requiring detention of certain noncitizens with criminal convictions, without ever receiving a hearing on whether he posed a flight risk or danger. He sued for release, arguing his prolonged detention without a hearing violated due process. The Second Circuit agreed, ruling that due process entitled him to a bond hearing once detention became unreasonably prolonged, and ordered further proceedings. A companion case involving another detainee, Carol Black, was resolved the same way but later became moot when he left the country. The government asked the Supreme Court to review the Second Circuit's ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asked the Court to decide whether there is a point at which immigration detention without a hearing becomes long enough that the Constitution requires giving the detainee a hearing on flight risk and danger, and if so, whether the government must prove the need for continued detention by especially strong evidence. The Court also asked the parties to address whether G.M.'s case had become moot.

## Where it stands

After the Court granted review and the case was briefed and argued, New York's governor granted G.M. a full pardon of the conviction underlying his case, eliminating the basis for his detention. The petitioners then filed a joint stipulation to dismiss, and the Court dismissed the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, as petitioner, asked the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling that detainees like G.M. are entitled to bond hearings after prolonged detention. Because the case was dismissed by the parties' joint agreement rather than decided, the Second Circuit's ruling in G.M.'s favor is not disturbed by this Court, but the dismissal also means the Supreme Court issued no decision on the legal questions presented, leaving the circuit disagreement over prolonged immigration detention unresolved by this case.
