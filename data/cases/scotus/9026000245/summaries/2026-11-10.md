## In brief

AstraZeneca and other drug and medical-device makers ask the Court to shut down terrorism-financing lawsuits brought by American victims of attacks in Iraq.

## What happened

Joshua Atchley and hundreds of other Americans, or their families, were killed or injured by the Iraqi militia Jaysh al-Mahdi, which had taken over Iraq's Ministry of Health. They sued AstraZeneca and other pharmaceutical and medical-device companies under the federal Anti-Terrorism Act, claiming the companies paid bribes and supplied free goods to the Ministry knowing the militia was diverting them to fund attacks on Americans. A district court dismissed the case, but the D.C. Circuit twice revived it, most recently after this Court sent the case back for reconsideration in light of its ruling in a different case, Twitter v. Taamneh. The D.C. Circuit held the companies could be sued both for directly causing the harm and for aiding and abetting the militia's attacks.

## What the Court is being asked

The companies ask the Court to decide two questions: first, whether a company can be sued for aiding and abetting terrorism when its assistance is not tied to any specific attack and when it did not act with any desire to help an attack succeed; and second, whether a company can be held directly responsible for a terrorist attack merely because it did business with a government agency that mostly performed legitimate functions but had allegedly been infiltrated by terrorists.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief opposing review, a reply, and amicus briefs. After being distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, the Court did not grant or deny the petition; instead it invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views on whether the case should be heard.

## What each outcome would mean

AstraZeneca and the other drug and device makers, as petitioners, are asking the Court to overturn the D.C. Circuit's decision, which ruled in favor of Atchley and the other victims. If the Court eventually grants review and later reverses, the victims' lawsuit would likely be narrowed or dismissed. If the Court denies review, or grants review and affirms, the D.C. Circuit's decision allowing the lawsuit to proceed would stand, and the case would continue in the lower courts. A decision to vacate would set aside the D.C. Circuit's ruling and send the case back without resolving the issues for either side. The Solicitor General's views, once filed, may influence whether the Court takes the case at all.
