## Why above the anchor

The most important upward adjustment is the combination of a renewed invitation after an earlier Supreme Court remand and a potentially recurring dispute over Taamneh's nexus and culpability requirements. The petition's statement and reasons for review, especially pp. 3–6, 10–15 and 27–30, argue that the D.C. Circuit allowed liability without a sufficiently specific connection between the assistance and the attacks. Its principal secondary-liability comparator is Ashley v. Deutsche Bank, 144 F.4th 420 (2d Cir. 2025); its direct-liability comparators include Rothstein and Kemper. The reply, pp. 3–10, insists these are differences in legal standards rather than merely different allegations. If that characterization persuades the SG, the case has a credible plenary-review path.

The petition also describes the government's earlier recommendation to vacate and remand in light of Taamneh, including concern about the alleged assistance's connection to the attacks (petition pp. 10–11). That is legitimate, preexisting procedural history, not the outcome now being predicted. It makes a second invitation more consequential than generic federal interest alone. Liability for companies supplying an American-supported foreign ministry also gives the executive branch a concrete foreign-policy interest. Those considerations support moving roughly eleven percentage points above the frozen anchor.

## Why still below one half

The opposition supplies a substantial vehicle objection, not just a denial that a split exists. Its introduction and pp. 11–29 characterize the pleaded conduct as repeated, deliberately structured cash and in-kind bribes to a militia controlling the Ministry, not ordinary sales of medicines with incidental diversion. On a motion to dismiss, those allegations must be taken as allegations rather than decided against plaintiffs. The actual decision below, pp. 2–5 and its nexus/culpability discussion, expressly adopts Taamneh's framework and treats the pleaded financing as affirmative and culpable. It does not announce liability for all routine commerce with a government agency.

Independent primary-source checking of Taamneh confirms a flexible relation between culpability and nexus, rather than the strict tracing rule suggested by the broadest version of petitioners' argument: 598 U.S. 471, 493–503, 506. The CourtListener search of opinion 9395548 for `nexus` returned both the rejection of either side's categorical position and the requirement for stronger culpability where the connection is attenuated. That supports taking the opposition's fact-sensitive distinction seriously; it does not establish that the D.C. Circuit applied the standard correctly.

The opposition, pp. 23–29, argues that Ashley distinguished ordinary or insufficiently connected banking activity and preserved claims for tailored assistance. I could not independently retrieve Ashley through the two MCP searches, so I treat the asserted split as contested on the supplied filings, not independently established. For direct liability, the earlier SG recommendation expressly resisted plenary review at that time, and the opposition says that remains a factbound sovereign-intermediary distinction (opposition pp. 29–33; petition pp. 28–29). An earlier recommendation for a Taamneh remand is therefore not an earlier recommendation to grant the present questions for argument.

Finally, this remains an interlocutory appeal at the pleading stage, with further proceedings available below (opposition pp. 35–36). The April 23, 2026 en banc order in appendix pp. 137a–138a records denial without any member requesting a vote; the provisioned lower-court docket also notes no dissent. That weakens the picture of a manifest, internally contested refusal to follow the remand. On balance, substantive concerns and executive interest nearly offset, but do not eliminate, the ordinary reluctance to review fact-intensive interlocutory applications of recent precedent.
