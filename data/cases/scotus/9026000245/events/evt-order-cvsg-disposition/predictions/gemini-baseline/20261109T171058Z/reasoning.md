## 3. Case-Specific Analysis

### A. Factors Favoring Certiorari (Upward Adjustments)

1. **Second CVSG at the First Conference Following a *Taamneh* GVR:**
   The single strongest upward signal beyond the baseline CVSG rate is that the Court already issued a CVSG and GVR in this exact case in OT2023 (No. 23-9), and yet—when the D.C. Circuit reinstated its reversal on remand—the Court did not simply deny certiorari at the November 6, 2026 conference (`distribution_count: 1`). Calling for the Solicitor General's views a second time indicates that at least four Justices (or a critical bloc of the Court) have serious reservations about whether the D.C. Circuit's remand opinion comports with *Taamneh* and *Smith & Wesson Brands, Inc. v. Estados Unidos Mexicanos*, 605 U.S. 280 (2025), and want the current administration's views on whether plenary review is now warranted.
2. **Substantial Tension with *Taamneh* and *Smith & Wesson*, Plus Friction with the Second Circuit's Decision in *Ashley v. Deutsche Bank*:**
   - **Question 1 (JASTA Aiding-and-Abetting Liability):** Petitioners highlight two legal flashpoints in the D.C. Circuit's remand opinion:
     - *Nexus and Fungibility:* Under *Taamneh*, 598 U.S. at 497, 501–03, and *Smith & Wesson*, 605 U.S. at 292–94, a plaintiff must show a concrete nexus between the defendant's assistance and the specific terrorist attacks that injured the plaintiff, or else clear the "pervasive, systemic, and culpable assistance" (near-common-enterprise) bar. The D.C. Circuit acknowledged that the complaint does not trace petitioners' cash or medical goods to any specific attack injuring any plaintiff, yet held that a "near-common enterprise" showing was unnecessary because the attacks were geographically and temporally bounded (Iraq from 2005 to 2011) and "[m]oney is fungible" (Pet.App. 22a–25a). By contrast, in *Ashley v. Deutsche Bank Aktiengesellschaft*, 144 F.4th 420, 444–45, 448 (2d Cir. 2025), the Second Circuit rejected a "fungibility" theory of nexus for attacks spanning Afghanistan from 2011 to 2016 and required "pervasive, systemic, and culpable" aid where transactions were not tied to specific attacks.
     - *Culpability and Scienter:* In *Taamneh*, the Court drew on common-law and criminal aiding-and-abetting principles (`United States v. Peoni`, 100 F.2d 401, 402 (2d Cir. 1938)) to ask whether defendants participated in the wrong "as something that they wished to bring about" and warned that "the more attenuated the nexus, the more courts should demand that plaintiffs show culpable participation through intentional aid that substantially furthered the tort." 598 U.S. at 498, 506. In *Atchley II*, the D.C. Circuit stated that borrowing criminal intent definitions is "entirely inappropriate" in a civil remedial statute (quoting *Passaic Daily News v. Blair*, 308 A.2d 649, 656 (N.J. 1973)) and held that "unusual" and illegal commercial practices (kickbacks and off-the-books "free goods" to win Ministry contracts) sufficed alongside knowledge that diverted resources supported Jaysh al-Mahdi's insurgency (Pet.App. 26a–33a). In *Ashley*, by contrast, the Second Circuit held that illegal money laundering by banks did not support an inference of "intent to further [a terrorist organization's] terrorist activities." 144 F.4th at 444–45.
   - Because the ATA contains broad venue provisions (18 U.S.C. § 2334(a)) and over 75% of ATA suits are brought in the D.C. or Second Circuits, any gap in pleading standards between those two courts carries outsized practical weight.
3. **High Stakes, Repeat-Player Advocates, and Foreign-Policy Concerns:**
   Petitioners are five of the world's largest pharmaceutical and medical-device manufacturers, represented by Lisa Blatt (Williams & Connolly) and a coalition of top Supreme Court practitioners, supported by the U.S. Chamber of Commerce, PhRMA, NAM, AdvaMed, WLF, and former national security officials. Moreover, even in No. 23-9, the Solicitor General's brief criticized the D.C. Circuit's foreseeability reasoning as misunderstanding *Taamneh* (U.S. Br. 15 n.1) and noted that the United States had encouraged private medical-supply contracts with the Iraqi Ministry of Health while declining to designate Jaysh al-Mahdi as an FTO. With a new administration in office in 2026 and the *Taamneh* GVR already exhausted, there is a meaningful probability (~35–40%) that the Solicitor General recommends plenary review on at least Question 1.

### B. Factors Counseling Against Certiorari (Moderating / Downward Adjustments)

1. **Interlocutory Posture (Rule 12(b)(6)) and Unresolved Factual/Threshold Issues:**
   The case arrives in an interlocutory posture from the reversal of a motion to dismiss. As respondents emphasize, the D.C. Circuit remanded substantial issues to the district court—including whether petitioners' alleged conduct satisfies the remaining statutory elements of direct liability under 18 U.S.C. § 2333(a) ("act of international terrorism") and the factual development of petitioners' defense regarding U.S. government encouragement and the operation of the Iraqi Ministry of Health. The Court and the Solicitor General frequently view interlocutory pleading disputes where key facts are sharply contested as premature for plenary review.
2. **Prior Solicitor General Opposition to Plenary Review and Fact-Specific Pleading Allegations:**
   In No. 23-9, the United States explicitly opposed plenary review on both questions, concluding on Question 2 (proximate cause for direct liability as against *Rothstein v. UBS AG*, 708 F.3d 82 (2d Cir. 2013), and *Kemper v. Deutsche Bank AG*, 911 F.3d 383 (7th Cir. 2018)) that the D.C. Circuit applied settled causation principles to the "particular and unusual allegations in this case—namely, that the Ministry was controlled by Jaysh al-Mahdi" (U.S. Br. 21–22). On Question 1, the complaint here alleges not routine arm's-length services (as in *Taamneh* or the commercial banking in *Ashley*), but that petitioners negotiated and delivered 20% cash bribes and off-the-books batches of "free goods" packaged for street resale (such as $3.7 million of hemophilia drugs in a single payoff) at a Baghdad Ministry headquarters openly occupied by Jaysh al-Mahdi fighters.
3. **Reconcilability of *Atchley II* and *Ashley* in Lower-Court Practice:**
   As respondents document, *Ashley* itself reaffirmed *Kaplan v. Lebanese Canadian Bank, SAL*, 999 F.3d 842, 860 (2d Cir. 2021), including *Kaplan*'s statement that "absence of proof of intent is not fatal" under *Halberstam* and *Taamneh*'s sliding scale (144 F.4th at 439 n.13). Furthermore, multiple district courts within the Second Circuit applying *Ashley* in 2026 (*Zobay v. MTN Group Ltd.*, 2026 WL 2892155, at *6 (E.D.N.Y. Sept. 25, 2026); *Moses v. BNP Paribas, S.A.*, 2026 WL 2925874, at *3, *6 (S.D.N.Y. Sept. 29, 2026); *Freeman v. HSBC Holdings PLC*, 2026 WL 880107, at *14 (E.D.N.Y. Mar. 31, 2026)) have expressly concluded that *Ashley* and *Atchley II* apply the same *Taamneh* framework to materially different factual allegations without conflict. If the Solicitor General takes that same view and recommends denial in light of the interlocutory posture, historical CVSG patterns indicate an ~80–85% probability of denial.
4. **Near-Closure of the GVR Pathway:**
   In the general `high`-band and `cvsg` base rates, GVRs account for 12.4% and 5.5% of all dispositions, respectively. Here, because the Court already GVR'd this exact case for *Taamneh* in 2024 and the D.C. Circuit already addressed both *Taamneh* and *Smith & Wesson* on remand, a second GVR is very unlikely (~2%). Thus, almost the entire grant-family probability must come from a full plenary grant (`granted`).
