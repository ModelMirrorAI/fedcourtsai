## II. Procedural History and Posture

Petitioners (five major pharmaceutical and medical-device manufacturer groups: AstraZeneca, GE Healthcare, Johnson & Johnson, Pfizer/Pharmacia & Upjohn, and Roche/Genentech) seek review of a unanimous D.C. Circuit panel decision (*Atchley v. AstraZeneca UK Ltd.*, 166 F.4th 490 (D.C. Cir. 2026) (*Atchley II*), Pet. App. 1a–32a) reversing the Rule 12(b)(6) dismissal of Antiterrorism Act (ATA), 18 U.S.C. § 2333(a) & (d)(2), claims brought by American service members, civilians, and family members killed or injured in Iraq between 2005 and 2011 by Jaysh al-Mahdi (JAM), a Hezbollah-trained terrorist militia.

The litigation has already been before the Supreme Court once:
1. **District Court (2020):** Judge Leon dismissed the complaint under Rule 12(b)(6) (474 F. Supp. 3d 194, Pet. App. 99a–150a), concluding that plaintiffs failed to plausibly plead either direct ATA causation or Justice Against Sponsors of Terrorism Act (JASTA) aiding-and-abetting liability based on defendants' medical-supply transactions with the Iraqi Ministry of Health (which was controlled by Moqtada al-Sadr's movement and JAM).
2. ***Atchley I* (D.C. Cir. 2022):** A unanimous D.C. Circuit panel (Pillard, Wilkins, and Edwards, JJ.) reversed as to both aiding-and-abetting and direct liability (22 F.4th 204, Pet. App. 33a–98a), and the D.C. Circuit denied rehearing en banc without recorded dissent.
3. **First Certiorari Petition and GVR (No. 23-9, `scotus/72483367`):** While rehearing en banc was pending in the D.C. Circuit, this Court decided *Twitter, Inc. v. Taamneh*, 598 U.S. 471 (2023). Petitioners sought certiorari. The Court called for the views of the Solicitor General (CVSG). The Solicitor General filed an amicus brief recommending that the Court GVR in light of *Twitter* (arguing that the D.C. Circuit had misapplied the *Halberstam* framework by focusing on assistance to JAM generally rather than a nexus to the specific terrorist acts, and expressing skepticism of direct liability while recommending that the D.C. Circuit reconsider both holdings on remand). On June 24, 2024, the Court GVR'd *Atchley I* for further consideration in light of *Twitter* (144 S. Ct. 2675).
4. ***Atchley II* on Remand (D.C. Cir. 2026):** After supplemental briefing (including following this Court's intervening decision in *Smith & Wesson Brands, Inc. v. Estados Unidos Mexicanos*, 605 U.S. 280 (2025)), the same D.C. Circuit panel unanimously reinstated its judgment. Writing for the panel, Judge Pillard dedicated 30 pages to analyzing the complaint's allegations under *Twitter* and *Smith & Wesson*, concluding that plaintiffs plausibly pleaded that defendants' corrupt kickbacks, cash bribes, and unstructured deliveries of millions of dollars in fungible, price-elastic medicines directly to a JAM-run Ministry of Health—coupled with direct in-person dealings at JAM headquarters and the lack of legitimate financial firewalls—constituted "pervasive, systemic, and culpable" participation forming a "near-common enterprise" under *Twitter* (598 U.S. at 502) and *Smith & Wesson* (605 U.S. at 291). The panel also reinstated its direct-liability holding because *Twitter* did not address primary liability under § 2333(a). The D.C. Circuit again denied rehearing en banc without a noted dissent on April 24, 2026 (Pet. App. 152a).

## III. Analysis of Certiorari Factors

### A. Factors Weighing Toward Certiorari (Upward Adjustments from the 5.19% Anchor)

1. **Return After Prior GVR & SG Participation (`scotus/72483367`):**
   This case returns to the Court after a GVR in No. 23-9 where the Solicitor General previously filed an amicus brief criticizing the D.C. Circuit's *Atchley I* analysis. When a circuit court reinstates its prior judgment on remand following a GVR—and when the panel leaves intact a portion of its prior opinion (here, Part III on direct liability) verbatim—petitions challenging the remand decision receive heightened scrutiny at the Court.
2. **Elite Repeat-Player Counsel, D.C. Circuit Origin, and High Financial Stakes:**
   Petitioners are represented by Lisa S. Blatt (Williams & Connolly) alongside Kannon Shanmugam (Paul, Weiss), Neal Katyal (Milbank), and Jeffrey Wall (Sullivan & Cromwell), representing major multinational healthcare corporations facing potential treble-damages liability across hundreds of plaintiffs. Petitions from the D.C. Circuit (`cadc`) also grant at a higher rate (8.3% grant+GVR in the statpack) than the overall circuit average.
3. **Supreme Court Attention to Secondary Liability Standards:**
   The Court has repeatedly intervened in recent terms to cabin expansive secondary-liability theories in statutory and tort suits—most notably in *Twitter, Inc. v. Taamneh*, 598 U.S. 471 (2023) (JASTA), *Smith & Wesson Brands, Inc. v. Estados Unidos Mexicanos*, 605 U.S. 280 (2025) (PLCAA/aiding-and-abetting), and *Cisco Systems, Inc. v. Doe*, 609 U.S. ___ (June 23, 2026) (No. 24-856, `scotus/72487129`, rejecting ATS and TVPA aiding-and-abetting liability). Petitioners forcefully argue that the D.C. Circuit defied *Twitter* and *Smith & Wesson* by treating commercial corruption (kickbacks and free-goods "bonuses" paid to secure Ministry contracts) as establishing culpable participation in terrorist attacks against Americans.

### B. Factors Weighing Against Certiorari (Downward/Moderating Signals)

Despite the strength of petitioners' advocacy, several weighty institutional and procedural considerations make a grant unlikely:

1. **Absence of Cert-Stage Amicus Briefs or New Executive Branch Involvement:**
   In *Atchley I* (No. 23-9), the Chamber of Commerce and PhRMA filed a cert-stage amicus brief, and the Court issued a CVSG before ultimately choosing a GVR rather than plenary review. In the present docket (No. 26-245), **zero amicus briefs** have been filed at the cert stage (`amicus_briefs: null` in `context.json`, and no third-party entries appear on the docket snapshot), placing the petition in the `0` cert-amicus bucket (3.8% grant-family rate vs. 14.8%–22.7% when amici file). Respondents filed their BIO on time without a waiver or call for response.
2. **Lack of a Genuine Legal Circuit Split on the Governing Rule:**
   Petitioners claim a circuit split with the Second Circuit's post-*Twitter* decision in *Ashley v. Deutsche Bank Aktiengesellschaft* (*Wildman v. Deutsche Bank*), 144 F.4th 420 (2d Cir. 2025), as well as earlier Second, Fifth, and Ninth Circuit decisions. However, close inspection of *Ashley/Wildman* (144 F.4th at 442–46) confirms that the Second Circuit and D.C. Circuit recited and applied the exact same legal standard from *Twitter* and *Smith & Wesson*: namely, that where a plaintiff does not plead a strict transaction-to-attack link, the plaintiff must plausibly allege "pervasive, systemic, and culpable" assistance amounting to a "near-common enterprise" (144 F.4th at 445; Pet. App. 20a).
   - In *Ashley/Wildman*, the Second Circuit held that routine commercial banking services provided to Pakistani fertilizer manufacturers or international money-laundering networks—at least one or more steps removed from the Taliban/Haqqani Syndicate—reflected at most passive indifference or attenuated fungibility, whilst explicitly distinguishing cases like *Kaplan v. Lebanese Canadian Bank*, 999 F.3d 842 (2d Cir. 2021), where a defendant gave special treatment and sanctions-evading assistance directly to known front entities of a terrorist organization.
   - In *Atchley II*, the D.C. Circuit emphasized that plaintiffs alleged direct dealings with a Ministry captured and controlled by JAM, carried out inside JAM offices gated by JAM checkpoints and weapons, involving illicit cash bribes and unmonitored batches of high-value, portable, price-elastic medicines ("virtually as good as cash") outside the official KIMADIA tracking system.
   Thus, the disagreement between *Atchley II* and *Ashley* turns on the fact-bound application of *Twitter* and *Iqbal* to a specific 200-page complaint rather than a square conflict over the governing legal test.
3. **Exhaustive Remand Opinion Foreclosing Another GVR:**
   Whereas in 2024 the Court GVR'd *Atchley I* because it predated *Twitter*, the D.C. Circuit in *Atchley II* thoroughly addressed both *Twitter* (2023) and *Smith & Wesson* (2025). Petitioners fleetingly suggest a GVR in light of *Cisco Systems, Inc. v. Doe* (decided June 23, 2026), which cited *Twitter* in holding that the Alien Tort Statute and Torture Victim Protection Act do not authorize aiding-and-abetting causes of action at all. Because JASTA (18 U.S.C. § 2333(d)(2)) *expressly* creates a statutory aiding-and-abetting cause of action under the ATA, *Cisco Systems* has no bearing on the statutory question here, making a second GVR virtually out of the question.
4. **Interlocutory Posture (Rule 12(b)(6) Reversal) and Unanimity Below:**
   The case remains at the motion-to-dismiss stage. The D.C. Circuit panel explicitly noted that discovery and summary judgment will test whether plaintiffs can actually prove that defendants acted with culpable awareness rather than duress or ordinary commercial motives, and whether the Iraqi Ministry of Health had sufficient operational separation from JAM to defeat causation. Moreover, not a single judge on the D.C. Circuit voted to rehear the case en banc in either 2022 or 2026. The Supreme Court already declined to grant plenary review in No. 23-9 even when the Solicitor General criticized the D.C. Circuit's reasoning, opting instead for a GVR. Having already given the D.C. Circuit the opportunity to re-examine the pleadings under *Twitter*, the Court frequently declines to spend a full merits slot conducting a fact-intensive Rule 12(b)(6) review of a single complaint's allegations before summary judgment.
