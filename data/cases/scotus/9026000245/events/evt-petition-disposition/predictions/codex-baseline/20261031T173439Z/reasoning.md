## Anchor and calibration

The authoritative context places this private corporate petition in `baseline` under `sal-v6` and freezes `cert_anchor.rate` at 0.0518752467429925, or approximately 5.19%, with a ten-Term lookback and digest `sha256:a059cb872f890b63515d8e2ac6ef798347ae7231a62d08543a5b7431bd941faa`. That is my starting point: the reached-band prior, not the much smaller terminal-baseline grant rate. The committed statpack's sal-v6 table is consistent with that prior across the displayed Terms before 2026. I do not use the 2026 row to set the anchor or reclassify this petition into a stronger band because of its facts.

I move materially above 5.19% for three related, case-specific reasons. First, this is a return after the Court previously granted, vacated, and remanded the earlier petition, No. 23-9, for reconsideration under Twitter, Inc. v. Taamneh, 598 U.S. 471 (2023). The prior Solicitor General's concerns about the nexus and culpability analysis, described in petition pages 10-11, make the renewed decision more consequential than an ordinary first-time application of settled law. That earlier disposition is pre-prediction procedural history, not the outcome of this petition. Second, petition pages 14-20 and reply pages 2-5 identify a concrete claimed disagreement with Ashley v. Deutsche Bank, 144 F.4th 420 (2d Cir. 2025), over attack-specific nexus, fungibility, and the required state of mind. Third, two supporting amicus entries and the combination of overseas commerce, humanitarian operations, and attacks on Americans give the question practical importance beyond these parties.

The strongest downward adjustments are vehicle quality and uncertainty about whether a real rule conflict exists. The D.C. Circuit did not simply authorize liability for ordinary medicine sales: its renewed opinion describes alleged cash bribes, off-the-books goods, repeated dealings, and knowledge of terrorist use. Appendix pages 22a-28a expressly reason through Taamneh's nexus and culpability framework. The opposition, especially pages 24-29, argues that Ashley involved materially different and more peripheral assistance and retained earlier precedent allowing claims on stronger facts. It also identifies district courts applying both appellate decisions without perceiving a conflict. These are advocacy claims, not independently verified findings that no split exists.

Taamneh itself preserves the possibility of broader liability where ordinary services are supplied in an unusual manner to known terrorists. A CourtListener excerpt from that opinion, near slip-opinion page 32, confirmed that qualification. It makes respondents' factual distinction substantial rather than merely rhetorical. The independent Ashley citation lookup returned no result, so I could not verify the parties' competing characterizations against its full opinion. This uncertainty keeps the asserted split from warranting a grant-majority forecast.

The case remains interlocutory after reversal of a dismissal, with further issues and discovery below. That posture is a reason for denial, though not a categorical obstacle: Taamneh also addressed pleading sufficiency. The second question, about proximate causation and a sovereign intermediary, is a weaker cert vehicle than the aiding-and-abetting question. Both sides acknowledge that the earlier SG brief opposed plenary review of the causation issue at that time; I do not transform a recommendation for the earlier GVR into a present recommendation for plenary review. The current record contains no such recommendation. Balancing genuine follow-on precedent concerns against these obstacles produces 24%, not either the unadjusted floor or a probability above one-half.

I assign 44% to at least one additional distribution. The modal path is denial without another distribution, but a relist for closer examination or a new SG invitation is a material alternative. The 22% new-CVSG probability reflects the foreign-policy issues and the government's earlier involvement, discounted because the Court already has its prior explanation and now faces a relatively focused pleading dispute. Those two increments overlap; they are not independent events to add together.

Conditional on a grant, I assign only 12% to disposition in the cert order. Taamneh has already been applied on remand, and the record identifies no new controlling decision requiring a routine second GVR. A grant would more likely address the disputed nexus and culpability standards through plenary review. Conditional on denial, I assign 6% to a noted dissent or statement. The individual order-writing probabilities are low and independent, not a categorical distribution; Thomas and Alito receive the largest, still small, probabilities. There is no basis for forecasting ordinary unpublished cert votes.

The 0.64 significance score concerns stakes if decided, not the 0.24 chance of review: the petition can affect substantial victim litigation and commercial or aid activity in unstable regions without presenting a constitutional question.
