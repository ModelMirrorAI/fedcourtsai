## In brief

Two brothers ask the Court to revive their claim that a Michigan school district violated their free-speech rights by making them remove "Let's Go Brandon" sweatshirts.

## What happened

D.A. and X.A., then middle schoolers at Tri County Area Schools in Michigan, wore "Let's Go Brandon" sweatshirts to school. School officials told them to remove the sweatshirts, citing a dress-code ban on profane or vulgar clothing, because the phrase originated as a stand-in for a vulgar phrase about President Biden. The students, through their mother, sued, claiming the school violated their First Amendment rights. The district court sided with the school, and a divided Sixth Circuit panel affirmed, holding that school officials could reasonably treat the phrase as vulgar even though it is a political slogan. The full Sixth Circuit declined to rehear the case, over one judge's dissent.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a school may treat a political phrase as banned "vulgar" speech, under a rule allowing schools to restrict profane or lewd student speech, when the phrase itself is not plainly profane or lewd but originated from a vulgar expression. The students argue the school's rule should reach only speech that is plainly vulgar on its face; the school argues officials may reasonably restrict speech whose known meaning is vulgar, however it is phrased.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for the September 28, 2026 conference, after the school filed its opposition and the students filed a reply. The docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The students, D.A. and X.A., are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the underlying free-speech question, resolving nothing about who wins until it rules. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the ruling that the school could require the students to remove their sweatshirts and the judgment against their claims.
