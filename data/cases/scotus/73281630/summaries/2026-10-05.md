## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear two Michigan students' bid to revive their free-speech lawsuit over being told to remove "Let's Go Brandon" sweatshirts at school.

## What happened

Brothers D.A. and X.A. wore sweatshirts bearing the phrase "Let's Go Brandon" to their Michigan middle school. School officials told them to remove the sweatshirts, saying the phrase is widely understood as a stand-in for a vulgar phrase about President Biden and violated the school's dress code banning profanity. The students sued, arguing their speech was political and nondisruptive. The district court ruled for the school, and a divided Sixth Circuit panel affirmed, holding school officials could reasonably treat the phrase as vulgar under a Supreme Court precedent allowing schools to restrict lewd or profane speech. One judge dissented. The full Sixth Circuit declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The students asked the Court to decide how far schools can go in treating a sanitized or coded phrase as equivalent to profanity it is said to stand for, when deciding whether they may restrict student speech that is otherwise political and causes no disruption. They argued lower courts disagree about whether this rule applies only to speech that is clearly and plainly profane or lewd, or more broadly to speech an administrator reasonably believes carries a vulgar meaning.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, the Court asked the school district to respond, and after further briefing the Court denied the petition, ending the case at the Supreme Court.

## What each outcome would mean

The students, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up their case and overturn the Sixth Circuit's ruling for the school district. Because the Court denied review, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands: the school district's treatment of the sweatshirts as restrictable did not violate the students' First Amendment rights, and the students' lawsuit remains dismissed. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning, only that it left that ruling in place.
