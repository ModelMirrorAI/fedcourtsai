## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear two Michigan middle schoolers' challenge to their school's ban on "Let's Go Brandon" sweatshirts.

## What happened

D.A. and X.A., sixth and eighth graders at Tri County Middle School, wore "Let's Go Brandon" sweatshirts to school, a phrase widely understood as a stand-in for a vulgar insult directed at President Biden. School staff told them to remove the shirts, citing a dress-code ban on profane or vulgar clothing. Their mother sued on their behalf, arguing the school violated their free-speech rights. A federal district court ruled for the school, and a divided Sixth Circuit panel affirmed, holding school officials could reasonably treat the phrase as vulgar even though it is political speech. One judge dissented, saying the ruling gave schools unchecked power to suppress speech.

## What the Court is being asked

The students asked the Court to decide how far schools may go in restricting student speech that carries a hidden or implied vulgar meaning but is not vulgar on its face, when that speech is also political. They argued a long-standing rule letting schools restrict lewd or vulgar speech should not extend to a phrase that only indirectly evokes a profanity, especially when it expresses a political viewpoint and causes no disruption at school.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, then the Court asked the school district to respond, received briefing from both sides along with several outside groups, and considered the case again at a later conference. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The students asked the Court to take up their case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Sixth Circuit's ruling in place, meaning the school district's decision to bar the sweatshirts stands and the students' lawsuit does not go forward. The denial is not a ruling that the school was right on the merits; it simply leaves the lower court's judgment as the final word in this case, without the Supreme Court deciding the free-speech question itself.
