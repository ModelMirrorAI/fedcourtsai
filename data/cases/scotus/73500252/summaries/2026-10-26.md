## In brief

Flagstar Bank asks the Court to decide whether federal banking law overrides state laws requiring interest on mortgage escrow accounts.

## What happened

California law requires lenders to pay interest on money held in mortgage escrow accounts. William Kivett and other borrowers sued Flagstar Bank for not paying this interest on their accounts. The district court ruled for the borrowers, awarding roughly $9 million, and ordered Flagstar to start paying interest going forward. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, relying on its earlier ruling that the National Bank Act does not override California's escrow-interest law. After the Supreme Court decided a related case, Cantero, and sent this case back for another look, the Ninth Circuit reconsidered but reached the same conclusion, over one judge's dissent. Similar disputes in other circuits have come out differently.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal banking law that lets national banks exercise their lending powers free of interfering state regulation blocks states from requiring banks to pay interest on mortgage escrow accounts. The question turns on how much a state interest requirement interferes with a bank's federally granted powers, and whether the lower court applied the right method for measuring that interference.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, and the Court has asked the Solicitor General to submit a brief giving the federal government's views on whether the case should be heard. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Flagstar, which lost below, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse. If the Court grants review and ultimately reverses, Flagstar would not have to pay interest on the escrow accounts, and the borrowers' judgment would be undone. If the Court affirms, the borrowers' award and the requirement that Flagstar pay interest going forward would stand. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling for the borrowers remains in place, but that denial would not mean the Court agrees with it.
