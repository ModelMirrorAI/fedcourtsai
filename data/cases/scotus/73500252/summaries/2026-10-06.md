## In brief

Flagstar Bank asks the Court to decide whether a federal banking law blocks California's requirement that banks pay interest on mortgage escrow accounts.

## What happened

William Kivett and other California borrowers sued Flagstar Bank, saying it failed to pay interest on their mortgage escrow accounts as required by California law. Flagstar argued the National Bank Act, a federal law governing national banks, overrides that state requirement. The district court ruled for the borrowers, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, relying on its own earlier precedent. After the Supreme Court decided a related case in 2024 setting out how courts should analyze such disputes, it sent this case back to the Ninth Circuit, which again ruled for the borrowers. Flagstar then asked the Supreme Court to take the case, noting that other appeals courts had reached different results on similar state laws.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether federal banking law prevents states from requiring national banks to pay interest on mortgage escrow accounts, or whether states may continue to impose that requirement without it counting as improper interference with federally granted banking powers.

## Where it stands

The case has been distributed for conference more than once, and most recently the Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views, a step the docket shows was taken in October 2026. The petition has not yet been granted or denied.

## What each outcome would mean

Flagstar, the bank, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether California's interest-on-escrow law is valid; granting review alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling favoring the borrowers, Kivett and the certified class, would remain in effect, requiring Flagstar to keep paying escrow interest. A grant that led to reversal would favor Flagstar, while an affirmance would favor the borrowers; a vacate would send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
