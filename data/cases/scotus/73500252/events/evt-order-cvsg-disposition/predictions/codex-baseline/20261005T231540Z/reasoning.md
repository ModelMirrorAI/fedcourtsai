## Why 68%, rather than the approximately 35% anchor

The additional evidence is unusually favorable to review even within the high/CVSG population:

- **An acknowledged post-remand conflict.** The QP and petition pp. 14–19 describe the Second Circuit finding preemption after Cantero while the First and Ninth Circuits do not. The opposition pp. 1–3 and 14–16 acknowledges conflicting outcomes rather than denying that they exist. It argues the conflict is too narrow and transient to warrant review. Agreement on the existence of the conflict is stronger evidence than the petitioner's characterization alone.
- **A developed, final vehicle.** Petition pp. 28–31 describes a final damages judgment and injunction resting on the preemption ruling. The opposition pp. 21–23 affirmatively endorses this case as a sound vehicle if the Court takes the issue, stressing the factual record and final judgment. That concession materially reduces ordinary preservation, interlocutory-posture, and unsuitable-record concerns. The petition also addresses the bank's former savings-bank status and relevant loan dates; these remain possible complications, not verified fatal defects.
- **A federal regulatory reason to hear from the United States.** Both briefs describe the OCC's new rule and its support for preemption. The snapshot's CVSG demonstrates actual Court interest in federal input. The agency's merits position is not the Solicitor General's cert recommendation, however, and the earlier recommendation to use Flagstar discussed by both parties dates to the prior round of litigation. Neither is treated as a current recommendation that has already arrived.
- **A clarification problem left open by the earlier decision.** The original Cantero decision, 602 U.S. 205, 219–221 (2024), requires comparison with the Court's preemption precedents rather than a categorical rule. The CourtListener excerpt confirms that framework without resolving the present petition. The parties disagree both about the proper application and about what the Ninth Circuit actually did. That dispute provides a plausible reason to return to the subject; it does not prove the bank is right on the merits.

There are substantial reasons not to go higher. The opposition pp. 10–16 argues that the June 2026 OCC rule, if valid, makes these cases largely retrospective and suggests awaiting a challenge to that rule. That is a serious importance/percolation objection. Its pp. 4–7 also describes evidence of ordinary compliance and a lack of demonstrated operational interference. I treat those assertions as advocacy supported by the opposition's account of the record, not as independently verified factual findings. They weaken any confident forecast of a merits win even if a conflict warrants review. The Court has recently supplied guidance on this subject and may prefer further development rather than another merits case. Finally, another pending petition may be selected as the lead vehicle.

The 68% estimate is a judgmental update from the measured 34.97% anchor for the admitted split and unusually favorable vehicle evidence, moderated by the new-rule objection and uncertainty about the United States' recommendation. It is not a fitted likelihood ratio or a statistical confidence interval. A GVR after a companion decision still counts as a grant of this petition, so lead-vehicle competition increases uncertainty about the route more than it mechanically reduces the grant-family probability. There remains a 32% complement for denial or other nongrant termination.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The 98% distribution increment reflects the expected return to conference after a requested government brief. It is not a claim that two more substantive relists are inevitable. The 0% CVSG increment is a convention for an already-satisfied condition that the harness masks. The 25% summary-route number is expressly conditional on a grant, with companion-case GVR risk doing most of the work; the 10% separate-writing number is expressly conditional on denial. No published transition or writing baseline was available, so those are judgmental probabilities.

The 0.63 significance score concerns the stakes if decided, not grant likelihood. The pre-decision record describes a multistate question affecting mortgage-escrow payments and the allocation of federal and state banking authority, with broader implications for application of Cantero. The opposition describes a judgment exceeding $9 million and a class exceeding 139,000 people. Those stakes justify a substantial score, but the technical subject and possible prospective displacement by the OCC rule keep it below a broad constitutional or system-wide crisis case.
