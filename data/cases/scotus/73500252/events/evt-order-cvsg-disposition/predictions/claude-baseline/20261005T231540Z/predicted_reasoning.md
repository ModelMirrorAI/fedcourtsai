# Forecast: Flagstar Bank, N.A. v. Kivett, No. 25-1350 (CVSG moment)

**State forecast from.** The docket shows two distributions (for the June 25 and September 28, 2026 conferences, the second preceded by a reschedule), a response requested after respondents waived, one industry amicus brief (Bank Policy Institute, ABA, Chamber, MBA) in support, and on October 5, 2026 an invitation to the Solicitor General to file a brief expressing the views of the United States. The CVSG is already on the docket, so nothing about a *further* CVSG is forecast.

**Disposition.** The Court will grant certiorari (P = 0.60), most likely plenary review of the question presented as written: whether the National Bank Act preempts state interest-on-escrow laws like California Civil Code § 2954.8(a). The question is already the one the Court framed in Cantero, and neither side proposes a reformulation; if the Court narrows anything it will be by framing the question around the Cantero methodology (whether a court of appeals may rest on pre-Cantero circuit precedent instead of the comparative analysis) rather than around California's statute specifically.

**The Solicitor General's brief.** The United States will recommend that the Court grant review in at least one of the three pending interest-on-escrow petitions, and will say the Ninth Circuit erred in treating Lusnak as surviving Cantero. The OCC adopted a final preemption rule for these laws in May 2026 and filed in the Ninth Circuit below calling the question exceptionally important; the SG's 2023 CVSG brief in the prior round already found this case a usable vehicle and urged the Court to take Flagstar rather than Cantero if it took one. Expect the brief between December 2026 and May 2027; the earlier window would put the petition on a January conference and the case on the April 2027 calendar, the later one on a June conference for argument in OT2027.

**Relists.** After the SG files, the petition will be redistributed at least once more (P = 0.95), and most likely exactly once or twice: a single conference after the SG brief is the modal path for a CVSG petition, with one relist plausible if the Court is choosing among the three vehicles. The only route to no further distribution is a settlement or withdrawal, which is unlikely with a $9 million class judgment, an injunction, and the industry's interest in a merits ruling.

**Vehicle choice among the three petitions.** The Court has three petitions on the same question: this one, the Cantero plaintiffs' petition from the Second Circuit's remand decision, and Citizens Bank's rehearing petition in Conti from the First Circuit. The likeliest grant is Flagstar as the lead vehicle, with Cantero held (or granted and consolidated) and Conti held: this case alone has a final money judgment, a published opinion with a dissent, a summary-judgment record, and both parties and the 2023 SG brief calling it the best vehicle. The alternative is the Court granting Cantero (as it did in 2023) and holding Flagstar, in which case Flagstar's eventual disposition follows the merits: a GVR if the bank prevails, a denial if the plaintiffs do.

**Summary disposition.** Conditional on a grant, P(the disposition rides in the cert order itself) = 0.25. That is mostly the held-then-GVR path above, with only a small share for an outright summary reversal of the Ninth Circuit for declining to apply Cantero on remand; the Court invited the SG's views rather than summarily reversing, which signals it is contemplating plenary review.

**If denied.** A denial most likely follows an SG brief that treats the OCC's prospective rule as having mooted the forward-looking importance and recommends letting the retrospective split lie. Conditional on denial, P(a noted dissent or statement respecting denial) = 0.25: Justice Kavanaugh, as Cantero's author, or Justice Alito would be the likeliest to write that the Ninth Circuit disregarded the Court's remand instruction, but a denial that follows the SG's recommendation usually draws no writing.

**If granted, the likely ground (conditional only).** The Court would hold that Cantero's comparative analysis is mandatory and that a court of appeals may not rest a preemption holding on pre-Cantero circuit precedent that never performed it, and would probably reach the merits and hold California's law preempted, resolving the split in the Second Circuit's favor.
