## Why substantially above the anchor

The strongest upward adjustment is a developed, acknowledged conflict about essentially the same recurring federal question. The petition, pp. 14–19, identifies the Second Circuit's post-remand conclusion that New York's interest-on-escrow requirement is preempted, against the First and Ninth Circuits' contrary results. The opposition, pp. 12–16, disputes the conflict's importance rather than denying its existence. This is stronger than a petitioner's unsupported assertion of a split. That the disagreement persists after the Court supplied a common framework makes additional review plausible.

The question presented directly asks whether the National Bank Act preempts state requirements such as California Civil Code section 2954.8(a). The final money judgment and prospective injunction give the issue concrete consequences. Both parties identify this case as a suitable vehicle if the Court takes the question: petition pp. 28–31; opposition pp. 21–23. The opposition's description of an evidentiary record also offers a reason to choose this case rather than a pleading-stage competitor. I treat the historical Solicitor General vehicle preference described in those briefs as useful but dated, not as a current recommendation. The response request and industry amicus filing provide additional, limited support without establishing four votes to grant.

I do not adopt the petition's strongest claim that the Ninth Circuit simply refused any comparative analysis. The supplied opinion actually compares Franklin National and Anderson, describes the precedents as inconclusive, and returns to Lusnak as binding circuit precedent (appendix 15a–18a). The meaningful dispute is the adequacy of that analysis and the role of retained precedent, not an absence of analysis. The retrieved Cantero opinion, 602 U.S. 205, 219–221 (2024), requires an assessment of interference informed by the Court's banking-preemption precedents; it does not itself decide the ultimate escrow-law question. This makes a fresh plenary resolution more plausible than an immediate summary reversal for obvious defiance.

## Why not higher

The opposition's strongest cert objection is that the new OCC rule described in its pp. 10–16 may narrow the practical need for retrospective judicial resolution. Its validity and future consequences are not adjudicated facts in this record. Even so, the prospect of future litigation about the rule is a credible reason to await a different vehicle. The opposition also describes substantial industry compliance and disputes proof of operational interference; I treat those as advocacy supported by its account of the record, not independently established economic findings. They weaken the petition's assertion that immediate intervention is indispensable.

Competing petitions create uncertainty about **this docket's** route. Taking another case can yield a hold and later GVR here, which still counts in my 0.64, but can also leave this petition denied after a ruling that does not require vacatur. The prior Conti denial and pending rehearing are disclosed in the provisioned opposition; they are context about another petition, not knowledge of this cell's disposition. I neither know nor retrieved the target outcome. Recent precedent, regulatory change, and vehicle competition prevent treating the acknowledged split as nearly certain review. The increase from 17.2% to 64% is a substantive judgment, not a fitted likelihood ratio or a measured rate for this precise fact pattern.
