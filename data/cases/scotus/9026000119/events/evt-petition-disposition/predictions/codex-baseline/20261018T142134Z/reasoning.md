Its discussion also recognizes actual-conflict cases and the need for factual findings. Here, extending that principle to territorial ethics regulation is an additional step, not a demonstrated disagreement over the same controlling federal holding. 2. **The appendix weakens the petition's account of summary disqualification.** The trial court held evidentiary hearings, identified the transit authority as an organizational client, and found the office had advised on activity later prosecuted without effective screening. 3. **Preservation and procedural posture complicate review.** The trial court declined several late-raised arguments, including separation of powers (Appendix D, 34a–35a). Its express deadline and warning weaken the petition's inadequate-notice framing. Failure to secure counsel then produced the appealed dismissal (Appendix A). I treat these as review obstacles, not as a conclusively established jurisdictional bar.
4. **There is meaningful counterweight.** Disqualifying the territory's principal prosecutorial office affects public-corruption enforcement; the federal Organic Act supplies more than a purely local-law hook. The petition alleges recurrence in another prosecution. Those considerations keep the forecast above an ordinary weak private petition, but do not eliminate the concrete vehicle problems.
