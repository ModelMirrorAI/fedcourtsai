# Predicted reasoning — Guam v. Ybanez, No. 26-119 (evt-petition-disposition)

Forecast of what the Court will do with this petition, from a docket showing
zero distributions: the petition was docketed July 27, 2026, and the response
is due August 26, 2026.

**Disposition: denied.** I expect a straight denial without argument, most
likely on an October 2026 order list.

**Distribution and relists.** The docket shows **no distributions yet** — the
state I am forecasting from. The respondents (five individual criminal
defendants who won below) will most likely waive a response or file a short
brief in opposition; either way the petition gets distributed for the
end-of-summer long conference (late September 2026) or one of the first
October conferences. So the petition will almost certainly be distributed at
least once (my `relist-increment` is 0.97 — the only realistic misses are a
pre-conference dismissal or withdrawal, and neither fits a government
petitioner suing its own supreme court's order). Past that first conference I
expect **no relist**: roughly a 15–20% chance of a second distribution, mostly
covering the scenario where a Justice interested in territorial questions asks
for the record or writes. Central case: distributed once, denied within one to
two weeks of that conference.

**CVSG: no.** The United States is not a party and no order invites its views.
A CVSG here would require the Court to treat the Organic Act
separation-of-powers question as certworthy enough to develop — possible,
since the office of the Guam Attorney General is a congressional creation
(48 U.S.C. § 1421g(d)), but the petition's execution gives the Court little to
work with, and the paid-segment CVSG rate is ~1.3%. I put the increment at
0.02.

**Which QP, if taken.** The QP as filed is argumentative ("should be extended
… to avoid unconstitutional dismissals") and would not be taken as written.
If the Court took the case it would reformulate — something like *whether a
territorial court's disqualification of the entire elected Attorney General's
office, followed by dismissal of the People's appeal for want of substitute
counsel, violates the Due Process Clause or exceeds the court's authority
under the Organic Act of Guam*. The Organic Act framing is the only one with
a realistic path: it is the ground on which the Court granted *Limtiaco v.
Camacho*, 549 U.S. 483 (2007), the last petition it took from the Supreme
Court of Guam, and it avoids the doctrinal dead end of a government entity
asserting Fourteenth Amendment rights.

**Summary disposition: no.** There is no intervening decision of this Court to
GVR against, and the asserted error rests on circuit-court authority
(*U.S. v. Bolden*), not on any settled holding of this Court that the Guam
Supreme Court defied — so a summary reversal has nothing to latch onto. P(GVR
or summary reversal) is under 1% combined.

**Separate writings at denial.** Most likely none (~90%+). The residual is a
short statement or dissent respecting denial from a Justice who has written on
territorial questions — Justice Gorsuch is the obvious candidate — framed
around the anomaly of a prosecution extinguished because the only lawful
prosecutor was disqualified and no substitute could exist.

**Conditional merits view.** If certiorari were granted, the likely outcome
would favor Guam on the narrow procedural ground (vacating the dismissal of
the appeal), because the alternative blesses a structure in which an exclusive
prosecuting authority can be judicially removed with no replacement mechanism
— but that is conditional on a grant I put at 7%.
