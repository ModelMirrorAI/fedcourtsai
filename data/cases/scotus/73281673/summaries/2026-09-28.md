## In brief

A man convicted based on evidence from a warrantless police viewing of a flagged video asks the Court to review whether that violated his Fourth Amendment rights.

## What happened

Snapchat's automated scanning flagged a 16-second video in Michael Gasper's account as suspected child sexual abuse material and reported it to a national clearinghouse, which forwarded it to Wisconsin authorities. A state analyst and then a local detective each viewed the video without a warrant before obtaining a warrant to search Gasper's home and devices, where more such material was found. Wisconsin was charged accordingly. A trial court suppressed the evidence, but the Wisconsin Supreme Court held that police viewing the flagged video did not exceed the scope of Snapchat's private search and so needed no warrant, and it sent the case back to the trial court.

## What the Court is being asked

Gasper's petition raises three issues: whether he had a reasonable expectation of privacy in material he uploaded to a cloud-based account from his phone; whether police viewing a flagged file that no human had yet examined went beyond what the private company's automated scan had already revealed, so that a warrant was required; and whether, even if a warrant was required, the evidence should still not be excluded because police relied in good faith on existing law. Wisconsin's brief in opposition also argues the Court lacks authority to hear the case yet, because Gasper's criminal case is not finished.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for the conference of September 28, 2026, after full briefing including Wisconsin's brief in opposition and an amicus brief; the docket does not yet show any order granting or denying it.

## What each outcome would mean

Gasper, who lost below, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the questions itself, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies the petition, the Wisconsin Supreme Court's ruling stands, leaving in place its conclusion that police did not need a warrant, and the case would continue in the Wisconsin trial court on remand. Wisconsin's brief also argues the Court lacks jurisdiction at this stage because no final conviction exists yet; if the Court agrees, it could dismiss the petition without reaching the substance of Gasper's questions.
