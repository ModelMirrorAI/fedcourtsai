## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review Michael Gasper's challenge to the warrantless police viewing of a video Snapchat had flagged as child sexual abuse material.

## What happened

Snapchat's automated scanning system flagged a video in Gasper's account as child sexual abuse material and reported it to the National Center for Missing and Exploited Children, which forwarded it to Wisconsin authorities without anyone viewing it first. A state analyst and then a local detective each viewed the video without a warrant, leading to a search warrant for Gasper's home and phone, where more such material was found. Wisconsin charged Gasper with possession and exploitation offenses. The trial court suppressed the evidence, but the Wisconsin Court of Appeals reversed, and the Wisconsin Supreme Court affirmed on the separate ground that police viewing the video did not exceed the scope of Snapchat's private search.

## What the Court is being asked

Gasper's petition raises three issues: whether people have a constitutionally protected privacy interest in material they upload to a cloud-based account, whether officers needed a warrant to view a file that a private company's automated scan had already flagged as matching known illegal material, and whether, even if a warrant was required, the evidence should still be allowed because officers relied in good faith on existing practice. Wisconsin responds that the Court lacks jurisdiction because Gasper has not yet been convicted and the case was sent back for more proceedings, and that the case is a poor vehicle for resolving these issues.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after Wisconsin filed a brief in opposition and an advocacy group filed a supporting brief. The Supreme Court denied the petition, ending this request for review.

## What each outcome would mean

Gasper, who lost below, asked the Court to take up his case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Wisconsin Supreme Court's decision in place, meaning the video and related evidence remain admissible under the private search doctrine as that court applied it, and Gasper's case returns to the Wisconsin trial court for further proceedings, including unresolved suppression arguments. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Wisconsin court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling standing.
