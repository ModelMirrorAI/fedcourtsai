## In brief

The Court declined to review a Wisconsin man's challenge to police viewing, without a warrant, of a flagged online video showing child sexual abuse material.

## What happened

Snapchat's automated scanning tool flagged a video in Michael Gasper's account as suspected child sexual abuse material and reported it, without any person at Snapchat viewing it, to a national clearinghouse, which forwarded it to Wisconsin authorities. A state analyst and then a local detective each viewed the video without a warrant before obtaining a warrant to search Gasper's home and phone, where more such material was found. Gasper was charged, and a trial court suppressed the evidence, but the Wisconsin Court of Appeals and then the Wisconsin Supreme Court ruled against him, the state supreme court holding that the officer's viewing did not exceed the scope of the platform's own search.

## What the Court is being asked

Gasper asks the Court to decide whether he had a privacy interest in material he uploaded to his cloud account, whether officers needed a warrant to view the flagged video given that a computer program, not a person, had screened it first, and whether, if a warrant was required, the evidence should still be allowed in because officers were relying in good faith on existing law. Wisconsin responds that the case is not over below and presents a poor opportunity to settle these questions.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, after the Court asked Wisconsin to respond and received briefing from both sides plus an outside group. The Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Gasper, the inmate seeking review, asked the Court to take up his case after losing in the Wisconsin Supreme Court. By denying the petition, the Court leaves that ruling in place: the video and the evidence found afterward remain admissible, and Gasper's criminal case returns to the Wisconsin trial court for further proceedings, including arguments he has not yet had decided. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Wisconsin Supreme Court's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision standing.
