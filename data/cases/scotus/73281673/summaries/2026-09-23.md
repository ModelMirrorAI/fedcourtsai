## What happened

Snapchat's automated scanning tool flagged a 16-second video in Michael Gasper's account as likely child sexual abuse material and reported it to a national clearinghouse, which forwarded it to Wisconsin authorities. A state analyst and then a local detective each viewed the video without a warrant before police obtained a warrant to search Gasper's home and phone, where they found more such material. Wisconsin charged Gasper with possession and exploitation offenses. A trial court suppressed the evidence, but Wisconsin's court of appeals and then the Wisconsin Supreme Court reversed, holding that because Snapchat's scan already made it virtually certain the video was what it appeared to be, the detective's viewing did not go beyond that private search and so needed no warrant. The case was sent back to the trial court for further proceedings.

## What the Court is being asked

Gasper's petition raises three issues: whether he had a privacy interest in material he uploaded to a cloud-based account, whether officers exceeded the scope of a company's private screening when they viewed a flagged file without a warrant, and, if not, whether officers could rely in good faith on existing law anyway. Wisconsin responds that the Court cannot even take up these questions yet because the state court's ruling was not final — Gasper has not been convicted, and other suppression arguments are still pending in the trial court. Wisconsin also argues the case is a poor vehicle for resolving the private-search question because a win for Gasper would not exclude the evidence underlying his charges, and that the state court's reasoning was correct regardless.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, most recently for the September 28, 2026 conference, after the state waived and then was asked to respond, and after both sides and an amicus filed briefs; the Court has not yet acted on whether to hear the case.
