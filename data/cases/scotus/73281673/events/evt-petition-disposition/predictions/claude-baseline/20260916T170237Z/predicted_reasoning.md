# Forecast: Gasper v. Wisconsin, No. 25-1191 (evt-petition-disposition)

**Bottom line.** The Court will deny certiorari, most likely at or shortly
after the September 28, 2026 long conference. P(any grant) = 0.08.

**State the docket is being forecast from.** Two distribution entries so far:
the first (for the May 21, 2026 conference) was superseded when the Court
called for a response on May 8 after Wisconsin had waived; the second is the
current distribution for the September 28 long conference. No relist in the
substantive sense has yet occurred, no CVSG is on the docket, and one amicus
brief (Project for Privacy & Surveillance Accountability) supports the
petition.

**Relists.** Probability 0.30 that the petition is distributed at least once
more after September 28. A call for a response after a waiver means at least
one chambers took a first look and found the private-search question worth
examining, and a petition that has drawn a CFR and an amicus is relisted more
often than the ordinary long-conference petition. But the brief in opposition
supplies a clean, familiar reason to stop: the Wisconsin Supreme Court's ruling
is interlocutory, the case was remanded for further suppression litigation,
and Florida v. Thomas, 532 U.S. 774 (2001), dismissed a near-identical posture
for want of a final judgment. Most likely the petition is denied at the first
conference; the minority path is one or two relists, either for a Justice to
prepare a short statement or to hold the petition against a cleaner vehicle on
the same question (the Solicitor General's decision whether to seek review of
United States v. Lowers, 170 F.4th 134 (4th Cir. 2026), is the obvious
candidate). If a relist occurs, expect one or two, not a long string.

**CVSG.** Probability 0.05. The federal interest is real: NCMEC's CyberTipline
is a federal statutory scheme (18 U.S.C. § 2258A) and the Department of Justice
litigates the same question in every circuit. But the Court almost never calls
for the SG's views on a state criminal defendant's petition with a
jurisdictional defect on its face, and here it would be asking the SG about a
case the Court cannot reach if the finality objection is correct. If the Court
wants the SG's views on hash-match private searches it will get them in a
federal case.

**Which question the Court would take, if it takes one** (context only, not
scored). Only Question 2, the private-search question the Wisconsin Supreme
Court actually decided, and in a reformulated form: whether a law enforcement
officer's warrantless viewing of a file that an electronic service provider
flagged solely by hash match, without any human at the provider having viewed
it, exceeds the scope of the private search under United States v. Jacobsen.
Questions 1 and 3 (a categorical Riley/Carpenter expectation of privacy in
cloud-stored phone data; the good-faith exception) were not passed on below and
would be left behind.

**Summary disposition versus plenary review.** Conditional on any grant, 0.25
that the disposition rides in the cert order rather than going to argument.
The only candidate for a GVR is Chatrie v. United States (2026), which the
Seventh Circuit has already flagged as bearing on whether agreeing to a
provider's terms of service forfeits an expectation of privacy. Chatrie does
not speak to the private-search ground the Wisconsin court rested on, and a
GVR requires the same § 1257 finality a plenary grant does, so the GVR route
is a minority of an already-small grant probability. A summary reversal is not
plausible: the state court sided with the larger set of circuits.

**Dissent or statement respecting denial.** Conditional on denial, 0.12 that
any Justice writes. The split is acknowledged and deepening (the Second,
Fourth, and Ninth Circuits against the Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh, with
Wisconsin and other state courts on the second side), and Justices Gorsuch and
Sotomayor have both written on digital privacy and on NCMEC's role. A short
statement noting the conflict and the vehicle problems is conceivable, but the
usual practice on a petition with a jurisdictional defect is a silent denial,
and the question will keep arriving in federal cases where a statement would do
more work. Expect no writing.

**If granted (conditional only).** The likely ground would be the meaning of
Jacobsen's "virtual certainty" test as applied to a hash match no human has
viewed. Given the current Court's writing on the third-party doctrine, the
private-search question would be closely contested, with the outcome turning on
whether the majority treats a hash value as equivalent to human inspection of
the file (the Reddick/Miller/Brillhart view) or as a label describing content
the officer then examines for the first time (the Wilson/Maher/Lowers view).
