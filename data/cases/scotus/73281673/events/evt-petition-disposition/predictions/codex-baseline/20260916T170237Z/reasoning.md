# Rationale for the probabilities

I used the case-level `record/snapshots/2026-09-15.json`, the event definition, `context.json`, and the provisioned questions presented, petition text, and brief in opposition. Context freezes Term 2025, `sal-v4`, band `elevated`, two distributions, and no CVSG. I retain that band rather than reclassifying from my assessment of the docket. Wisconsin is the respondent, not the petitioner; this is a private petitioner's case.

The matching committed `metrics/statpack.md` table supplies the anchor. Pooling the **bracketed reached rates** for elevated petitions over every displayed Term strictly before 2025 gives approximately **17.24%**, with weighted resolved denominator **2,810**. These are committed-pack estimates available in this checkout, not a fresh corpus-wide measurement; the pack metadata consulted did not supply a build timestamp.

The paid-segment terminal relist cuts show substantially higher grant-side rates after multiple relists, but their terminal buckets do not directly estimate the probability of another distribution. I use those cuts as qualitative context, not as conditional hazards or independent multipliers.

## Why only 5% for any grant

**The legal conflict is real on the parties' own presentations.** The petition, pp. 8–10 and 21–28, argues that automated identification does not permit a government officer to make the first human inspection without a warrant. Wisconsin expressly acknowledges conflicting appellate decisions in its BIO, pp. 18–19: Reddick and Miller on one side, Wilson and Maher on the other, with Lowers joining the latter side. I rely on the filings' descriptions, not an independently retrieved reading of those decisions. This conceded conflict, the Court's request for a response after Wisconsin waived, and a filed privacy-organization amicus all support attention above that of an ordinary unsupported error-correction petition.

**Finality is the strongest downward adjustment.** The petition's jurisdictional statement, p. 1, calls the January 14 ruling final but does not work through the interlocutory exceptions. The appended Wisconsin majority opinion, pp. 35a–36a, expressly remands for further proceedings. The BIO, pp. 9–15, says Gasper has not been convicted or sentenced, identifies remaining suppression issues, and develops a 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a) objection through Cox Broadcasting and Florida v. Thomas. This looks like a serious obstacle to taking this petition now, rather than a superficial objection to a clean split vehicle. I do not present the State's jurisdictional position as a ruling by the Supreme Court, and I leave some probability for an exception or a response not contained in the provisioned record.

**The remedial and record obstacles are cumulative.** The BIO, pp. 16–23, argues that the evidence supporting the charges would remain admissible even if the initial video viewing violated the Fourth Amendment. It attributes that conclusion, through different rationales, even to Wisconsin justices rejecting the majority's private-search analysis. The petition does challenge good faith and argues, at pp. 25–26, that the CyberTip alone is insufficient probable cause; thus it does not wholly ignore the derivative-evidence issue. Nevertheless, its treatment does not convincingly dispose of the State's alternative-basis argument. The BIO also identifies an excluded privacy affidavit and lack of admissible evidence of subjective privacy. Petition pp. 11–12 instead rely on the affidavit as an offer of proof and claim categorical protection from Riley and Carpenter. That dispute creates an additional vehicle issue; I do not assume that all provider terms automatically eliminate Fourth Amendment privacy.

The appended majority, pp. 33a–35a, leaves reasonable expectation of privacy and good faith undecided. Questions 1 and 3 therefore make the petition broader, not cleaner. A substantial split can warrant eventual Supreme Court review without making this interlocutory petition the likely vehicle. Those obstacles outweigh the upward signals and support a **5% probability of any grant**, including GVR or summary reversal, rather than the roughly 17% band anchor.

## Other judgments and limitations

The second distribution followed a requested and filed response; it is not persuasive evidence of repeated completed conference review. I therefore assign 18% to any further distribution and only 2% to a CVSG. These are subjective forecasts, not published conditional rates. Conditional summary disposition is 12% because a grant aimed at resolving the acknowledged split would more naturally be plenary. Conditional separate writing on denial is 8%: the underlying issue can attract attention, but the threshold problems give little reason to insist on this vehicle. The stakes score of 0.68 reflects the issue's nationwide privacy and investigative significance, not its grant probability.

`documents.json` reports a nonempty, untruncated BIO and nonempty but truncated petition. The petition's main argument and majority decision are available; the supplied text ends within the appendix around p. 55a, before later separate opinions and lower-court materials. My account of those omitted passages therefore rests on the BIO's characterizations. No reply or amicus text was provisioned; the snapshot supplies the amicus filing's existence only.

A CourtListener MCP lookup of Florida v. Thomas failed with HTTP 429. Two general web searches and an attempted official U.S. Reports PDF opening returned no usable content. I degraded to the provisioned filings and committed base rates, without a CourtListener REST fallback or a query for this petition's outcome. I neither know nor encountered this petition's disposition. The dated snapshot remains the factual baseline; I make no claim to having refreshed its current docket state.
