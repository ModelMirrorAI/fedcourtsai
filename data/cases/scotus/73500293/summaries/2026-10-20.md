## In brief

The Supreme Court affirmed, by an equally divided result due to a lack of quorum, a lower court ruling on whether copyright termination rights extend worldwide.

## What happened

Cyril Vetter co-wrote "Double Shot (Of My Baby's Love)" in 1962 and assigned worldwide rights to a publisher. Decades later he used a federal copyright law provision to terminate that grant and reclaim his rights. A dispute arose over whether reclaiming the rights gave him back only the U.S. copyright or the rights worldwide. The district court and the Fifth Circuit both ruled that Vetter recovered the full worldwide rights. BMG Rights Management and other music companies, who later acquired the opposing party's interest, asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether, when an author legally takes back rights previously signed away, that reversion covers only the copyright recognized under U.S. law or also extends to the copyright interests recognized under other countries' laws.

## Where it stands

The case was fully briefed and distributed for the Court's October 16, 2026 conference. Rather than deciding the question, the Court announced on October 19, 2026 that because it lacked a quorum, and the remaining justices believed the case could not be heard in the next term, the judgment below was affirmed automatically under a federal statute governing such situations.

## What each outcome would mean

BMG Rights Management and the other music companies, as petitioners, asked the Court to reverse the lower ruling. Because the Court lacked a quorum, the law required it to affirm the Fifth Circuit's decision automatically, with the same effect as if an equally divided Court had ruled. That leaves the Fifth Circuit's judgment in place: Vetter keeps the worldwide rights he reclaimed, and BMG and the other petitioners do not gain the foreign rights they sought. The order does not reflect the Court's own view on the merits of the legal question.
