## In brief

Music publishers asked the Supreme Court to decide if a songwriter's copyright termination reclaims only U.S. rights or worldwide rights; the Court affirmed the lower ruling.

## What happened

Cyril Vetter and Donald Smith wrote "Double Shot (Of My Baby's Love)" in 1962 and assigned worldwide copyright interests to a publisher. Decades later, Vetter used a federal copyright law provision to terminate part of that assignment, and separately acquired renewal rights from Smith's heirs. Vetter sued Resnik Music Group, which had acquired the publisher's interest, seeking a declaration that he owned the song worldwide. The district court and the Fifth Circuit both ruled for Vetter, holding that his termination recaptured rights globally, not just in the United States. Music publishing companies, who later bought Resnik's interest, then asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether an author who takes back rights under a federal copyright law provision, after earlier assigning those rights worldwide, gets back only the rights tied to the United States or all the rights originally granted worldwide. The publishers say the law limits what an author recovers to domestic rights, while Vetter argues the statute's terms do not separate domestic from foreign rights that trace back to the same original U.S. grant.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 16, 2026 conference. On October 19, 2026, the Court announced that because it lacked a quorum of qualified justices and the remaining justices determined the case could not be heard and decided in the next term, the judgment below was automatically affirmed under a federal statute governing such situations, with the same effect as an affirmance by an equally divided Court.

## What each outcome would mean

BMG Rights Management and other music publishers, as petitioners, asked the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling in Vetter's favor. Because the Court lacked a quorum, it could not decide the merits; instead, the law required automatic affirmance of the Fifth Circuit's judgment, leaving Vetter's worldwide ownership ruling in place. This outcome carries the same effect as a tie vote: it resolves this case for these parties but does not establish a nationwide rule on the legal question, since the Court issued no opinion on the merits.
