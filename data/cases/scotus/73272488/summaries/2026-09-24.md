## What happened

James Abrams was convicted after a jury trial in the Middle District of Pennsylvania on 48 counts, including wire fraud, mail fraud, and aggravated identity theft, related to a scheme to defraud investors in a clean-energy startup. At trial, his lawyer made only a brief, general motion for acquittal without spelling out specific problems with the government's evidence. The United States Court of Appeals for the Third Circuit ruled that this kind of general motion does not preserve a full, fresh review of whether the evidence was sufficient; instead, courts may only check for obvious, serious errors. Applying that stricter standard, the Third Circuit upheld Abrams's convictions. Abrams asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling, and the government waived its right to respond, though it was later asked to respond and has requested more time to do so.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is what a defendant must do, when asking a trial judge to throw out a case for lack of evidence, to preserve the right to a full, independent review of that evidence on appeal. Abrams argues that a simple, general request should be enough, and that requiring defendants to list every specific flaw in the government's proof is inconsistent with the rule's text and history and unfair to defendants. He also says federal appeals courts are deeply and permanently split on this question, with several courts requiring only a general motion and others, including the Third Circuit, demanding detailed objections.
