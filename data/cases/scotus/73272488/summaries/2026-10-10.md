## In brief

James Abrams asks the Court to decide what a defendant must say at trial to preserve a full appellate review of whether the evidence supported his conviction.

## What happened

James Abrams was convicted on 48 counts, including wire fraud and aggravated identity theft, arising from a scheme to defraud investors in a clean-energy startup. At trial, his lawyer moved for acquittal under a rule allowing such motions but did not specify which parts of the government's evidence were deficient. The Third Circuit Court of Appeals held that this general motion did not preserve detailed sufficiency arguments for the more favorable standard of review, so it reviewed his claims only for "plain error," a tougher standard, and affirmed his convictions. The petition says this conflicts with how most other circuits handle the same situation.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a defendant who makes a general request for acquittal, without spelling out each specific flaw in the government's evidence, preserves the right to have an appeals court review the evidence fully and independently, or whether the defendant must identify each specific problem at trial to get that fuller review rather than a more limited review reserved for unpreserved errors.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court later asked the government to respond. After several extensions, the government filed its brief opposing review on October 9, 2026. The case has not yet been scheduled for a conference since that filing, as the docket shows.

## What each outcome would mean

Abrams, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case and decide the legal question itself, without deciding in advance who is right. If the Court denies review, the Third Circuit's decision applying the stricter "plain error" standard to Abrams's case stands, and his convictions remain affirmed under that ruling.
