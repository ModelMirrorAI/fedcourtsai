## In brief

A man convicted of investment fraud asks the Court to decide what a routine courtroom motion must say to preserve a full appeal of the evidence against him.

## What happened

James Abrams was convicted on 48 counts, including wire fraud, mail fraud, aggravated identity theft, money laundering, and false statements, after a jury trial in which he was found to have used forged documents and false information to secure over $1 million in startup investments and then diverted the funds. At trial, his lawyer moved for acquittal under a rule allowing such motions when evidence is insufficient, but stated no specific grounds, saying only "I waive argument." The Third Circuit Court of Appeals held that this general motion did not preserve detailed sufficiency arguments for full review, applied a more limited "plain error" review instead, and affirmed his convictions.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a defendant who makes a bare, unspecific request for acquittal during trial preserves the right to a full, independent appellate review of whether the evidence supported the verdict, or whether the defendant must instead spell out each specific weakness in the government's case at trial to get that fuller review later. Abrams's petition says federal appeals courts are divided on this question, with most courts allowing general motions to preserve full review and a minority, including the Third Circuit, requiring specifics.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including the government's brief opposing review and Abrams's reply, after multiple extensions of the government's deadline to respond. The docket does not yet show the case distributed for a conference since briefing was completed.

## What each outcome would mean

Abrams is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse the Third Circuit's decision. If the Court grants review and ultimately sides with Abrams, his case would likely return to the lower court for fuller review of his sufficiency arguments. If the Court denies review, the Third Circuit's decision applying the stricter standard, and Abrams's convictions, would stand. The government argues denial is appropriate because the lower court found no error even under the stricter standard, meaning the outcome might not change regardless of which standard applied.
