## In brief

Pennsylvania asks the Court to review a ruling that struck down its requirement that mail-in voters date their ballot envelopes.

## What happened

Bette Eakin and several organizations sued Pennsylvania's county election boards, arguing that discarding mail ballots over missing or incorrect handwritten dates on return envelopes violates the Constitution. Republican party committees intervened to defend the requirement. A federal district court agreed with Eakin and ordered counties to stop discarding ballots for this reason. The Third Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, finding the requirement burdens the right to vote without sufficiently serving the state's interests. The full appeals court narrowly declined to rehear the case, over dissents. Pennsylvania's Attorney General, who had intervened on appeal to defend the law, now asks the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether requiring mail-in voters to handwrite a date on their ballot return envelope, with ballots lacking a valid date being discarded, violates voters' constitutional rights. Pennsylvania also argues that a Pennsylvania Supreme Court decision issued after the appeals ruling changes the legal landscape enough that the case should be sent back for another look rather than decided now.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including responses from various county election boards and the Eakin respondents opposing review, and a reply from Pennsylvania. The Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief expressing the federal government's views, and has not yet acted on the petition itself.

## What each outcome would mean

Pennsylvania, which lost below, is asking the Court to reverse that result. If the Court denies review, the Third Circuit's decision stands, meaning counties cannot discard mail ballots solely for missing or incorrect dates. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the constitutional question itself, deciding nothing about the outcome at this stage. The Court could instead grant, vacate, and remand the case for the lower court to reconsider in light of the intervening Pennsylvania Supreme Court ruling, which would set aside the current decision without resolving the dispute for either side.
