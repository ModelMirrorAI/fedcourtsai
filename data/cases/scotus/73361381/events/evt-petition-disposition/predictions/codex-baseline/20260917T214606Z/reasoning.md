## Prior and adjustment

The applicable starting point is the baseline band's bracketed reached rate in the sal-v4 table, not its lower terminal-band rate. Pooling all displayed Terms strictly before 2025, namely 2017 through 2024, gives approximately 5.12% over a denial-reweighted denominator of 11,580. I executed the weighted calculation using the displayed rounded percentages, so this is an approximate pool rather than an exact numerator reconstructed from the database. The same-Term 2025 row and later 2026 row are excluded. The whole modern discretionary-cert table gives about 2.82% for grants plus GVRs, but is not the paid private baseline risk-set anchor. The provisioned baseline classification is appropriate to retain; neither the state-court origin nor the constitutional issue makes the private petitioner a state petitioner. Source: metrics/statpack.md, modern-cert disposition and sal-v4 segment tables.

I adjust the 5.12% starting point down to P(any grant) = 0.8%. The petition presents a cognizable federal notice argument but does not establish a developed conflict among federal appellate courts or state courts of last resort on that question. Its examples of differing treatment of the Ohio transition rule are an Ohio intermediate appellate decision and a federal district court decision; the petition characterizes their application of limitations law, not conflicting holdings that due process requires codification. That is a weak demonstrated conflict on the question presented. Source: petition.txt, pp. 8-9.

The principal weakness is the petition's express concession both that the session law supplies clear retroactive intent and that its eight-year transition affords reasonable time. Its remaining claim is that a litigant should not have to consult uncodified law. The quoted transition provision runs eight years from the amendment's effective date or until the old period expires, whichever comes first; I use that quotation, rather than the petition's looser later description of eight years from accrual. The statement describes a 2010 loss and a March 11, 2024 complaint. An isolated reference to a 2014 complaint conflicts with that narrative, so I do not treat it as a separate procedural fact or infer a jurisdictional defect. Sources: petition.txt, pp. 2-3 and 6-8.

I checked the general precedent through CourtListener, not this case's history. Texaco, Inc. v. Short, 454 U.S. 516, 532-33 (1982), states that enactment and publication plus a reasonable opportunity to comply ordinarily provide legislative notice, and gives substantial deference to legislative grace periods. The retrieved majority passage does not decide Ohio's codification question. My inference is narrower: the petitioner must establish why otherwise published session law fails that general standard, despite conceding a reasonable transition. I do not equate an enacted session-law provision with nonbinding legislative history merely because the petition sometimes does so. This doctrinal obstacle and the limited demonstrated conflict weigh more heavily than the abstract importance of notice. Source: CourtListener majority opinion 9428577, passage retrieved for "enact and publish"; petition.txt, pp. 3-8.

I have not independently verified the petition's characterization of the two alleged conflicting decisions, preservation of the federal issue, or any alternative ground for the judgment. I therefore do not assert waiver, an independent state-law ground, or a timeliness bar. Missing opposition and lower opinions widen uncertainty rather than justify an invented vehicle defect. The low probability is a judgment from the available petition and doctrine, not an empirical pro se-petitioner rate.
