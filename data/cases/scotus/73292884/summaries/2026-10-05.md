## In brief

The Court declined to hear a nonprofit's challenge to New Mexico's campaign-finance donor-disclosure law as applied to its legislative scorecard mailings.

## What happened

Rio Grande Foundation, an issue-advocacy nonprofit, wanted to mail a "Freedom Index" scoring New Mexico legislators' votes close to an election but held back because doing so would trigger donor-disclosure requirements under the state's Campaign Reporting Act. It sued New Mexico's Secretary of State, arguing the law's disclosure rules violated the First Amendment as applied to groups that do not expressly campaign for or against candidates. A federal district court upheld the law, and a divided Tenth Circuit panel affirmed, rejecting Rio Grande's reading of the statute and finding its mailing an obvious attempt to influence voters. The full Tenth Circuit declined to rehear the case, with two judges dissenting.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a state's interest in informing voters is enough to justify requiring donor disclosure from groups that mention candidates in ads but do not explicitly urge votes for or against them, whether such disclosure rules can be properly limited in scope without requiring donors to flag their money for a specific ad, and whether these disclosure rules should face the toughest level of constitutional review rather than a somewhat less demanding one.

## Where it stands

The Court considered the petition at conferences in June and September 2026, during which it requested a response from the state, and denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Rio Grande Foundation asked the Court to take up the case and overturn the Tenth Circuit's ruling, which favored the state. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Tenth Circuit's decision in place: New Mexico's disclosure law, as applied to Rio Grande Foundation's mailings, stands, and the organization must comply with its donor-disclosure requirements if it proceeds with similar mailings. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed.
