## In brief

A lawyer whose seized bank funds were returned asks the Court to decide whether he still counts as having won, entitling him to attorney's fees.

## What happened

The government seized millions of dollars from Richard Stuart Ross's bank trust account, suspecting the funds were tied to a fraud scheme. Ross contested part of the seizure. The government eventually dismissed its forfeiture claim to $1.21 million without prejudice and returned that money to Ross, while other funds he had not claimed were forfeited by default. Ross then sought attorney's fees under a federal law letting a claimant who "substantially prevails" in a forfeiture case recover fees. The district court denied fees, and the Second Circuit affirmed, holding Ross had not prevailed because the dismissal was not a court ruling in his favor and the government could still refile. The appeals court did void a related finding the government had conceded was wrong.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether someone who gets the government to drop a forfeiture case and return his property counts as having "substantially prevailed" for purposes of recovering attorney's fees, even without a court judgment declaring him the winner. It is also asked whether losing the chance to seek such fees, because a case was dismissed without prejudice rather than with prejudice, counts as the kind of legal harm that should lead courts to dismiss cases with prejudice instead.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond, but the Court later asked for a response, which is now due November 9, 2026, after an extension. The case has not yet been scheduled for a conference since that request was made.

## What each outcome would mean

Ross, the claimant, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide what "substantially prevails" means under this fee law; a grant would not itself decide who is right. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Ross without attorney's fees and leaving in place that court's rule requiring a judgment, not just a returned asset, before a forfeiture claimant can recover fees.
