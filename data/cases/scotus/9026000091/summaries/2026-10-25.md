## In brief

Richard Ross asks the Court to decide whether he counts as having prevailed in a forfeiture case the government dropped, entitling him to attorney's fees.

## What happened

Federal agents seized millions from Ross's law-firm trust account, suspecting the funds were tied to a wire-fraud scheme. Ross contested the seizure of $1.21 million he claimed was unrelated. After discovery was stayed for a criminal investigation, the government moved to dismiss its forfeiture claim to that $1.21 million without prejudice and returned the money, while still forfeiting the rest by default. Ross sought attorney's fees under a federal forfeiture law, arguing he had "substantially prevailed." The district court and the Second Circuit disagreed, holding a without-prejudice dismissal does not make him a prevailing party, though the appeals court threw out a related finding favorable to the government on a separate point.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether someone who gets the government to voluntarily drop a forfeiture case and return the seized property counts as having "substantially prevailed" under the fee-shifting law, even without a final court ruling in his favor. A second question asks whether losing the chance to claim such fees, because a case was dismissed without prejudice rather than with prejudice, counts as a legal harm that should have led the lower court to dismiss the case in a way that preserved his fee claim.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the government initially waived its right to respond, but the Court then asked for a response, which is now due in November 2026. No action on the petition itself has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Ross is asking the Court to take up and reverse the Second Circuit's ruling, which went against him. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide what "substantially prevails" means under this fee-shifting law, potentially leading to a ruling for either side. If it denies the petition, the Second Circuit's decision stands, leaving Ross without attorney's fees despite recovering his $1.21 million. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with the lower court's reasoning, only that the case goes no further.
