## In brief

Richard Ross asks the Court to decide whether he counts as having "substantially prevailed" against the government under a forfeiture-fees law, after it dropped its case and returned his money.

## What happened

Federal agents seized millions of dollars from Ross's attorney trust account, believing it held proceeds of a wire-fraud scheme against another company. Ross contested the forfeiture of $1.21 million he said was unrelated, mostly proceeds from his home sale. After a discovery stay tied to a criminal probe, the government moved to dismiss its claim to that money without prejudice and returned it to Ross. He sought attorney's fees under a law requiring the government to pay fees to a claimant who "substantially prevails." The district court denied fees, and the Second Circuit affirmed, holding a without-prejudice dismissal does not make Ross a prevailing party; it also vacated an unrelated certificate the government conceded was improperly granted.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether someone who gets the government to voluntarily drop a forfeiture case and give back seized property counts as having "substantially prevailed," entitling them to attorney's fees, even without a court ruling finally deciding the case in their favor. A related question is whether losing the chance to claim those fees, because the case was dismissed without prejudice rather than with prejudice, counts as a legal harm courts must guard against when approving such dismissals.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference. The government later asked the Court to consider the case anyway and was asked to respond, but then received an extension, now due November 9, 2026, so the case has not yet been scheduled for another conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Ross, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on what "substantially prevails" means under this fee law and could rule for either side, or could send the case back without deciding it. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's decision stands, leaving Ross without attorney's fees despite recovering his seized funds.
