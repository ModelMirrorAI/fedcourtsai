## In brief

A man whose seized funds were returned after the government dropped its forfeiture case asks the Court to say he still counts as having won, entitling him to attorney's fees.

## What happened

Federal agents seized millions from Richard Stuart Ross's bank trust account, suspecting the money was linked to a fraud scheme, and the government sued to forfeit it. Ross contested the forfeiture of part of the money; courts entered a default forfeiture judgment on the uncontested portion. The government later moved to dismiss its forfeiture claim to the contested $1.21 million without prejudice and returned those funds to Ross. The district court denied Ross attorney's fees, finding he had not "substantially prevailed," and the Second Circuit affirmed that denial while also vacating an unrelated certificate the government conceded was improperly granted.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether someone who gets the government to drop a forfeiture case and return his property, without a court ruling in his favor, counts as having "substantially prevailed" under the federal law that awards attorney's fees in such cases. A related question is whether losing the chance at those fees because the case was dismissed without prejudice, rather than with prejudice, counts as the kind of harm that should have led the court to dismiss the case on different terms.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference; the government waived its right to respond but was later asked to respond, and that response's deadline has twice been extended, now to December 7, 2026. No conference has yet considered the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Ross, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up and reverse that ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether Ross's recovery of his money counts as "substantially prevailing," deciding nothing by the grant itself. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling against Ross's fee claim stands, leaving him without the attorney's fees he sought despite getting his money back.
