I raise P(any grant) to 0.24 for three case-specific reasons. First, the Court affirmatively requested a response after the United States waived one. That is materially more informative than a petition merely being distributed. Second, eight recorded amicus briefs, including former forfeiture prosecutors and organizations concerned with property rights and litigation costs, suggest attention beyond this claimant. Third, the petition presents a recurring federal fee-recovery question and identifies differing circuit approaches to the related Rule 41(a)(2) issue. The allegations of complete recovery of the claimed property and preservation of alternative fee theories make the question reasonably concrete. These considerations justify a substantial upward adjustment, but they are not independent measured likelihood ratios.

The principal reasons to stop well below 50% come from the petition itself. Its first question challenges a substantially shared circuit interpretation rather than presenting a clean disagreement on the same statutory issue. Its sharpest opposing authority on the second question, the Ninth Circuit's Ito decision, is unpublished; the petition acknowledges disagreement about its persuasive force even among district courts. Its Eleventh Circuit comparison rests on an assumption about prejudice for a meritorious claimant and a further showing of likely entitlement. Those distinctions weaken the claimed square conflict. See petition printed pages 23–26. The requested response can clarify these weaknesses without producing four votes for review.

The petition's descriptions of Hardt, CRST Van, and Lackey support its advocacy but do not establish that existing precedent compels reversal. Indeed, the QP section characterizes the preclusive-judgment issue as left open. I treat the asserted conflict with those decisions as an argument, not a verified holding about CAFRA. The lower-court account in the petition also makes the timing and legal effect of the limitations period potentially relevant; I do not assume an expired limitations period retroactively supplies the missing judicial relief. No opposition or independently retrieved lower-court opinion was available to test those points. Sources: provisioned petition, printed pages 7–18 and 21–26; provisioned QP; snapshot proceedings.

The stakes score of 0.48 reflects meaningful national consequences for recovering litigation costs after civil forfeiture and potentially for other fee statutes, while recognizing that this petition does not directly ask the Court to invalidate civil forfeiture or decide entitlement to the already-returned principal. It is separate from grant likelihood.
