## Why denial is the central forecast

The application describes five alleged forms of Florida protocol maladministration: omitted anesthetic, insufficient anesthetic, expired anesthetic, incorrect quantities of the remaining drugs, and unauthorized drugs (application pp. 5–14). Its strongest point is the asserted pattern, not a single paperwork error. It alleges corrected drug logs, restricted discovery, and toxicology evidence supporting a risk of awareness during paralysis. Its pre-warrant filing on September 28, 2026 also supplies a serious answer to the appellate majority's delay rationale (pp. 1–5, 14–17). These are real reasons not to set the stay probability near zero.

Nevertheless, the applicant's own account describes an evidentiary hearing and unfavorable findings in both lower courts. It reports that the courts credited testimony that anesthetic was administered, treated inventory discrepancies as recordkeeping errors, found the toxicology comparisons unreliable, and relied on receipts and lot numbers showing unexpired stock (pp. 6–12). The application disputes those findings, but this posture asks the Court to revisit a contested evidentiary assessment on an emergency timetable. The filing identifies no developed inter-circuit conflict, and an error about the timing equities would not independently establish a sufficient likelihood of success on the underlying constitutional claim. Judge Jill Pryor's concurrence did not join the appellate discussion of the remaining stay factors; the application does not describe her as dissenting from the denial of relief (p. 14 n.3).

General precedent reinforces the distinction between serious allegations and entitlement to a stay. In *Hill v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 573, 583–84 (2006), the Court explained that a cognizable section 1983 action does not automatically stop an execution: the applicant must satisfy equitable requirements, including likelihood of success, and timing matters. I verified that discussion through CourtListener opinion 145647. The *Baze v. Rees* plurality, 553 U.S. 35, 50, 61 (2008), distinguishes an isolated mishap from a substantial risk of serious harm and requires a demonstrated severe-pain risk for a stay on the grounds considered there; I checked the lead opinion, CourtListener 9435238. Guardado alleges something broader than an isolated mishap, so that distinction does not dispose of his claim by itself. But the lower courts' reported findings substantially weaken his showing that the alleged systemic failures establish the necessary prospective risk. I do not treat this application as an adjudication of the medical facts, nor assume that a correctly followed protocol eliminates every constitutional concern.

The 6% estimate represents a low but meaningful chance that restricted discovery and the cumulative operational evidence persuade enough Justices to preserve the case for fuller review. It is judgmental, not the result of a fitted model or a published capital-stay subgroup rate. The principal uncertainty is how the unprovisioned hearing record and opposition bear on those allegations. Irreversibility weighs heavily for Guardado but does not replace the merits showing.
