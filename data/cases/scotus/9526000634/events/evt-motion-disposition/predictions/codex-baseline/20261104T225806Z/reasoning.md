## Why relief is unlikely

The largest obstacle is merits likelihood rather than the reality of irreparable harm. The application describes a district-court evidentiary hearing followed by adverse findings, with the Eleventh Circuit also refusing a stay. In the application's own account, the lower courts credited testimony that etomidate was administered, postmortem tests detecting it, receipts and lot numbers supporting a January 2027 expiration date, and assurance that the drugs set aside for this execution were unexpired. Those assertions are contested, but they give the Court a record-based route to deny relief without endorsing poor recordkeeping or deciding that the alleged pain would be acceptable. See application pp. 6–11.

The remaining disputes concern inferences from postmortem drug concentrations, variation among prisoners and sampling methods, altered expiration entries, missing or inconsistent inventory entries, and possible unauthorized lidocaine use. The application identifies disturbing evidence, but it does not show that the lower courts disagreed on a controlling national rule. Its practical request is largely to revisit evidentiary assessments and allow additional discovery under an imminent execution deadline. A fact-intensive disagreement is less persuasive as a basis for extraordinary Supreme Court intervention than a clearly established legal error. I do not independently verify the State's account or accept that recordkeeping errors were harmless; the adverse findings are predictive obstacles, not my factual findings.

The general legal context supports separating a cognizable civil-rights claim from entitlement to emergency relief. In *Hill v. McDonough*, 547 U.S. 573, 583–85 (2006), the Court explained that bringing a method-of-execution claim does not itself entitle a prisoner to a stay: merits likelihood, equitable considerations, and the State's enforcement interest remain relevant. I verified the pertinent passage through CourtListener opinion 145647. The *Baze v. Rees* plurality, 553 U.S. 35, 50 (2008), distinguished isolated mishaps from a substantial risk of serious harm; I checked the passage in lead opinion 9435238. Guardado's allegation of repeated systemic failures attempts to clear that distinction, but proving those allegations against contrary findings remains difficult. Neither precedent decides Guardado's application, and I do not assume a protocol-maladministration claim necessarily fails for lack of an alternative execution method.

## Why the probability is not negligible

The strongest counterweight is the timing-and-discovery argument. According to application pp. 1–5 and 14–17, the civil-rights complaint preceded the death warrant, was filed shortly after clemency proceedings ended, and was forced into a compressed hearing with limited, heavily redacted disclosure. That weakens an uncomplicated characterization of the suit as deliberately last-minute. Altered drug records and disputed expert evidence make a short stay for meaningful review conceivable. Execution would permanently foreclose relief for this applicant, so the harm is unusually grave.

But correcting the delay analysis alone need not change the outcome if merits likelihood independently fails. The application's account of Judge Jill Pryor's separate concurrence does not establish a vote to grant a stay: it specifically says she did not join the remaining-factors discussion, not that she rejected the merits result. Without the actual opinion or a response, I cannot sharpen that distinction further. I assign the modest 8% allowance mainly to a possible procedural/discovery intervention, not to an expected wholesale rejection of Florida's execution protocol.

## Procedural and writing probabilities

From the frozen zero-response, zero-referral, zero-amicus state, I assign an 80% chance of a response request and a 90% chance of referral to the full Court. The impending execution and substantive constitutional claim make expedited adversarial consideration and full-Court handling plausible even when relief is unlikely. These are judgmental estimates, not rates extracted from the pack. Referral can occur without a separately recorded request, so the referral estimate can exceed the request estimate.

The short interval before the stated execution time limits amicus participation: I assign an 18% chance of at least one new counted entry or distinct unmatched submitted amicus filer, with zero additional entries the modal forecast. These claims concern increments, not an inference that filings already exist outside the snapshot.

An unsigned denial without an extended explanation is the modal writing forecast. A written objection is possible because of the irreversible consequence and curtailed discovery; I place the largest independent authorship probabilities on Sotomayor (30%) and Jackson (22%), with Kagan at 5% and the other Justices at 1–2%. These are low-confidence author predictions, not predictions that joining another Justice's dissent counts as writing, and not a firm vote-count forecast.

The stakes score is 0.64: immediate life-and-death consequences plus potential effects on Florida's administration of executions, moderated by the narrow factual posture. It is not a grant-likelihood score.
