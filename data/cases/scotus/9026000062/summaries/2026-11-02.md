## In brief

Custodia Bank asks the Court to decide whether a federal law requires Federal Reserve Banks to grant master accounts to eligible nonmember banks like itself.

## What happened

Custodia, a Wyoming-chartered bank serving digital asset companies, applied for a master account with the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City, which gives a bank direct access to the Federal Reserve's payment system. The Reserve Bank denied the application, citing risks from Custodia's crypto-focused business. Custodia sued the Reserve Bank and the Federal Reserve Board, arguing federal law requires approval for any eligible institution regardless of risk. The district court and the Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals both ruled against Custodia, holding that Reserve Banks have discretion to deny such applications. One Tenth Circuit judge dissented, and the full court later declined to rehear the case over three dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a 1980 banking law, which says Federal Reserve services "shall be available" to nonmember banks, strips Reserve Banks of discretion to deny deposit accounts to individual eligible applicants, or whether that discretion survives from an older, separate provision allowing Reserve Banks to decide whether to accept deposits.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including responses from both the Federal Reserve Board and the Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City opposing review and a reply from Custodia. The case was distributed for the Court's November 13, 2026 conference, where the justices will decide whether to take it up.

## What each outcome would mean

Custodia, which lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to reverse that result. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether Reserve Banks have this discretion, without yet deciding who is right. If the Court denies review, the Tenth Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Federal Reserve Banks free to deny master accounts to eligible nonmember institutions based on risk assessments, and Custodia's loss becomes final.
