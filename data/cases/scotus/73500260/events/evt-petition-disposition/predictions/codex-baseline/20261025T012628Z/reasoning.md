I move upward because the Court affirmatively requested a response after waiver, South Carolina filed an amicus brief, and the petition articulates a recurring federal-jurisdiction question with a claimed inter-circuit disagreement. The requested response is more informative than the routine extensions. It does not imply four votes to grant. The question concerns how specifically a court should characterize the important state interest under Younger when a State regulates transactions occurring outside its borders. It is not a direct request to invalidate Pennsylvania's usury rules.

The main downward adjustments concern the strength of the split and this vehicle. The petition juxtaposes the Third, Fifth, and Seventh Circuits with the Fourth Circuit's Harper and the Tenth Circuit's tribal-gaming decision. The latter arises in a distinctive federal/tribal setting, rather than the same consumer-credit enforcement posture. More importantly, general legal-context retrieval found the Fourth Circuit's **Air Evac EMS, Inc. v. McVey**, No. 21-1301 (June 10, 2022), which describes Harper as giving way only in unusual circumstances and states that NOPSI moved away from case-by-case balancing of state and federal interests. That does not disprove every aspect of the asserted conflict, but makes the petition's broad two-camp account less persuasive as evidence of a square, mature split. This is older precedent about another dispute, not information about this petition's outcome.

The appended Third Circuit decision is expressly nonprecedential. It applies the quasi-criminal-enforcement category, finds a state forum available for constitutional objections, and relies both on generic consumer-protection interests and an earlier decision involving these lenders. Those details allow the Court to view this case as an application of existing abstention rules rather than an urgent doctrinal conflict. The prior decision is described here only as it appears in the provisioned materials; I did not look up its later treatment.

**Sprint Communications, Inc. v. Jacobs**, 571 U.S. 69, 72–73, 78–82 (2013), confirms both the narrowness of Younger and the continuing inclusion of qualifying civil enforcement proceedings. It therefore supplies arguments to both sides, not a clear intervening command for summary reversal. I cannot assess the respondent's eventual framing, factual objections, or vehicle objections without an opposition. Taken together, these considerations justify a material increase above the roughly 5% anchor but not a grant as the modal outcome.
