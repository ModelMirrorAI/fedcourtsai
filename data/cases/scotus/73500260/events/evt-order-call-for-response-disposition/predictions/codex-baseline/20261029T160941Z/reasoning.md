## Anchor and adjustment

The committed statpack's sal-v4 table matches the frozen band. Pooling every displayed Term strictly before 2025, OT2017 through OT2024, gives **607 grant-family outcomes / 11,599 denial-reweighted resolved petitions that reached baseline = 5.2332%**. These are the bracketed risk-set denominators, not terminal-baseline rates, and the petitioners are private companies: a state respondent and a state amicus do not make the petitioner a sovereign. I exclude the 2025 and 2026 rows. The calculation uses the matching `prefix_weighted_resolved` and `prefix_est_grant_rate` fields in `metrics/statpack.json` and agrees with the displayed table after rounding.

For scale only, the modern-cert disposition table yields 1,282 grants or GVRs among 44,777 resolved petitions, about 2.86%, but that all-petition mixture is not this paid private petition's anchor. The paid-segment terminal cuts are also descriptive, not transition probabilities: no relist has a 1.8% grant-family share (n=10,203 resolved); one relist 13.7% (n=2,538); two 41.2% (n=495); three or more 37.4% (n=484). The CVSG bucket is 34.8% (n=164), versus 6.4% without one (n=13,556). One terminal amicus entry corresponds to 15.1% (n=1,162). These all-Term marginal cuts overlap and cannot be multiplied or treated as a response-request-conditioned rate. The statpack publishes no such response-request rate.

The adjustment to 18% is judgmental. The Court's affirmative request after a waiver is meaningful evidence of interest, though not four votes for review. The petition identifies a concrete conflict between the Third/Fifth/Seventh Circuits' generic-interest approach and Fourth Circuit cases requiring greater attention to interstate interests, with a Tenth Circuit Indian-law analogue (petition pp. 26-31). South Carolina's filed amicus signals that the concern extends beyond these lenders. The question concerns access to a federal forum for challenges to cross-border enforcement; those stakes are broader than the immediate financial dispute.

There are substantial offsets. The Third Circuit opinion is expressly nonprecedential (App. 2a). Its actual reasoning is more fact-bound than the QP's framing: it relies on earlier TitleMax litigation sustaining Pennsylvania's interest, the connection to more than 5,000 Pennsylvania borrowers, and the availability of constitutional review in the state process (App. 6a-8a). It also rejects bad faith and distinguishes prospective relief; one subpoena claim separately encounters ripeness problems (App. 9a-11a). That last ground does not defeat every claim, but illustrates vehicle complexity. The asserted split draws on different regulatory settings and older precedents, and the Tenth Circuit's distinctive Indian-law context weakens the claim of identical cases receiving opposite answers. These are reasons to discount the petition's assertion that the conflict is clean, not reasons to decide the constitutional merits against the petitioners.

The petition reports that an administrative hearing examiner recommended dismissal in April 2026, subject to further agency review (pp. 14-15). That is not a final termination or proof of mootness, but the changing administrative posture creates vehicle risk. The petition also identifies a potentially cleaner related TMX case and asks alternatively for a hold; it does not establish any later Supreme Court action in that litigation. Those considerations justify a meaningful conditional summary-route probability without treating a companion grant as a fact.
