## In brief

Insurers ask the Court to review a ruling that Louisiana law, not federal law, decides whether they can force a policyholder into arbitration.

## What happened

One Lakeside Plaza sued its insurers after they allegedly failed to properly pay hurricane damage claims. Eight domestic insurers asked the district court to compel arbitration under their policy, relying on an international treaty covering arbitration agreements that involve foreign parties; two foreign insurers originally named in the suit had already been dismissed. The district court refused, holding that Louisiana law bars arbitration clauses in insurance policies and that the insurers could not use the legal doctrine of equitable estoppel to get around that bar. The Fifth Circuit affirmed, relying on its recent decision in a similar case, Town of Vinton, and the full court declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether federal rules or state law control when a company that did not sign an arbitration agreement can still rely on it, through equitable estoppel, in cases involving this international arbitration treaty. The petitioners also ask the Court to simply hold this case until it resolves the same question in the related Town of Vinton case, and then decide this one the same way.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for November 6, 2026; the docket does not yet show a decision on whether the Court will hear the case. Briefing is complete, including the insurers' reply.

## What each outcome would mean

Indian Harbor and the other insurers are asking the Court to take up the case and ultimately reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling, which favored One Lakeside. If the Court grants review, it would decide the legal question itself, with no party assured of winning simply from that decision; the petitioners have specifically asked the Court to hold this case until it rules in Town of Vinton and then decide this one consistently. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the ruling that state law governs and that the insurers cannot compel arbitration here.
