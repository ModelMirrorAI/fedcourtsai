Feb. 2, 2026) (App. 1a–33a), the Third Circuit panel (Ambro, J., joined by McKee and Restrepo, JJ.) held that the Equal Access to Justice Act's fee-shifting sovereign-immunity waiver in "any civil action (other than cases sounding in tort)," 28 U.S.C. § 2412(d)(1)(A), unambiguously encompasses 28 U.S.C. § 2241 habeas corpus petitions challenging civil immigration detention, and affirmed EAJA fee awards to Michelin (`$15,841.60`) and Abioye (`$18,224.58`).    - On June 29, 2026, the Supreme Court granted certiorari in *Montoya Palacios v. Liggins*, No. 25-1223 (`scotus/73253921`, arising from the Fourth Circuit), to resolve the 4–2 circuit split on the exact question presented here: whether 28 U.S.C. § 2412(d)(1)(A) waives the United States' sovereign immunity for habeas petitions challenging immigration detention.
   - In `petition.txt` (at 5–6), the Solicitor General asks the Court strictly to **hold** this petition pending *Montoya Palacios* and then dispose of it as appropriate in light of that decision. (The Solicitor General filed a parallel hold petition on the same day from the Tenth Circuit's decision in *Daley v. Ceja*, docketed as *Baltazar v. Daley*, No. 26-146.)

   - Nor is there any alternative ground below that would insulate the Third Circuit's judgment if the government prevails in *Montoya Palacios*: in *Michelin*, the EAJA sovereign-immunity question was the sole issue on appeal, and in *Abioye*, the Third Circuit's secondary holding that the government's detention litigation position lacked "substantial justification" is a statutory defense inside § 2412(d)(1)(A) that becomes moot if sovereign immunity bars EAJA fees altogether.

2. **Substantive Merits of *Montoya Palacios* and Probability of a Resulting GVR (`0.58`):**
   - Because the Court is ~96% likely to hold No. 26-145 pending *Montoya Palacios*, the probability that No. 26-145 ends in a GVR (`granted = 1`, `predicted_disposition = "gvr"`) is essentially the probability that the federal government prevails in *Montoya Palacios* (or that the *Montoya Palacios* opinion otherwise vacates/modifies the standard applied by the Third Circuit).
   - On one hand, *Montoya Palacios* was granted from a Fourth Circuit decision favoring the government, and the Supreme Court disturbs the judgment below in roughly 70% of argued merits cases (`metrics/statpack.md`). Moreover, the textual argument for the detainees—that § 2412(d)(1)(A) covers "any civil action (other than cases sounding in tort)" and that habeas petitions are classified as civil actions under 28 U.S.C. § 2241—persuaded the Second, Third, Ninth, and Tenth Circuits.
   - On the other hand, *Montoya Palacios* was granted as the first clean vehicle to resolve an entrenched 4–2 circuit split on recurring federal fee liability, and the government's sovereign-immunity argument aligns closely with the current Court's strict sovereign-immunity clear-statement jurisprudence (*Ardestani v. INS*, 502 U.S. 129, 137 (1991); *FAA v. Cooper*, 566 U.S. 284, 290 (2012); *Lac du Flambeau Band v. Coughlin*, 599 U.S. 382, 388 (2023)). Under that standard, the government need not show that "any civil action" unambiguously excludes habeas petitions; it need only show that excluding "hybrid" habeas proceedings is a plausible reading of the text—a reading reinforced by the fact that even the circuits favoring detainees concede that criminal § 2241 and § 2254 habeas petitions are *not* "civil actions" under EAJA, requiring those circuits to split the single statutory phrase "any civil action" according to the underlying detention context.
   - Balancing the general grant-to-reverse tendency in *Montoya Palacios* against the strength of the sovereign-immunity clear-statement canon before the current Court, I estimate a ~57% probability that the government prevails in *Montoya Palacios* (plus a ~3% probability of a GVR even under a modified/intermediate ruling), yielding `P(grant/GVR in No. 26-145) = 0.96 × 0.60 ≈ 0.58`.
