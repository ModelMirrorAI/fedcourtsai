## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review whether immigrants who win habeas cases over their detention can collect attorney's fees from the government.

## What happened

Two men detained by immigration authorities, Adolph Michelin and Adewumi Abioye, separately won court orders requiring bond hearings after being held for over a year without one. Each then won an award of attorney's fees from the district courts under a federal law called the Equal Access to Justice Act, which lets prevailing parties collect fees from the government in civil cases unless the government's position was substantially justified. The government appealed, and the Third Circuit Court of Appeals, consolidating the two cases, ruled that habeas cases challenging immigration detention count as "civil actions" under the fee law, and upheld the fee award in Abioye's case. The government asked for rehearing before the full appeals court, which was denied over several judges' dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The government asks the Court to decide whether a federal law letting people collect attorney's fees from the government in civil lawsuits also applies to habeas cases—court challenges to unlawful detention—brought by immigrants contesting their detention. The government argues the law's waiver of its immunity from such fee awards does not clearly cover this type of case, while Michelin and Abioye argue it does.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, after which the Court asked the other side to respond; that response's deadline has since been extended to December 4, 2026. The government's petition itself asks the Court to hold the case until it decides a related case, Montoya Palacios v. Liggins, which already presents the same question.

## What each outcome would mean

The government is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Third Circuit's decision favoring Michelin and Abioye. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the fee-shifting law covers these habeas cases, deciding nothing yet on its own. If it denies the petition, the Third Circuit's ruling favoring Michelin and Abioye stands, and the fee award to Abioye remains in place. The government has also suggested the Court could instead simply hold the petition until it rules in a similar pending case.
