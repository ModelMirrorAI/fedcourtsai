## Anchor and adjustments

The frozen sal-v8 **baseline** band's prior-Term reached-rate anchor is **0.051587928582714486**, with a ten-Term configured lookback. I use that frozen anchor, rather than the much lower rate for petitions that ultimately end in the baseline band or the incomplete 2026 row. The prior-Term rows shown in `metrics/statpack.md` are the relevant historical comparison; the selected private-petitioner population is more appropriate than the whole cert docket.

The following are cross-checks, not independent multipliers. The paid-segment terminal no-relist bucket has approximately 1.8% grants including GVRs (10,204 resolved); the one-relist bucket has 13.5% (2,532 resolved). These are terminal trajectories, not the forward probability that this petition will be distributed again. The paid-segment no-CVSG bucket has about 6.4% grants including GVRs (13,550 resolved), versus 34.8% with a CVSG (164 resolved). The one-amicus bucket has 14.9% grants including GVRs (1,159 resolved; 1,209 total). That last comparison is marginal, measured at disposition, and potentially correlated with other attention signals; it does not establish an independent amicus effect. I count the joint November 2 filing once, not once per organization.

My principal upward adjustment is the developed statutory conflict described in the petition, pp. 15–21. The Fourth Circuit's Trauernicht ruling limits monetary relief to the participant's account, whereas the petition identifies seven circuits permitting broader recovery. The appendix independently confirms that the Ninth Circuit explicitly rejected Capital Group's restrictive reading of LaRue and joined other circuits, App. 21a–23a. The question is not just whether one set of investments was imprudent: it concerns who may seek recovery for losses to other participants' accounts under ERISA. A national rule would affect many retirement plans and repeated disputes over representative remedies and arbitration.

The requested response adds a further, moderate positive signal. The Court chose to obtain an adversarial presentation after a waiver. It is not a grant vote, and there is no committed table here measuring the grant rate conditional on a called-for response. The magnitude of my adjustment is judgment, not a lookup from the relist table. The single docketed amicus filing and experienced appellate representation provide modest additional support, but not enough to make review more likely than denial.

Several considerations restrain the forecast. This is an appeal from refusal to compel arbitration rather than a final judgment awarding plan-wide damages. The Fourth Circuit decision arose in a class-certification setting (petition, pp. 16–18), so the asserted conflict must be evaluated across different procedural vehicles. More importantly, the Ninth Circuit opinion also interprets the contractual representative-action waiver as barring claims belonging to the plan, App. 18a–20a, and discusses equitable remedies affecting the whole plan. A ruling limited to monetary recovery may not dispose cleanly of all those contractual and remedial issues. I treat this as a substantial vehicle risk, not a finding that there is a definitively unreviewable independent ground.

The panel dissent does not independently establish disagreement over the petition's precise statutory question. Judge VanDyke focused on delegation and the meaning of the waiver, and did not decide the scope-of-monetary-relief question (petition, pp. 13–14; appendix, App. 24a onward). The majority considered the delegation argument forfeited, App. 17a–18a n.2. Those features make the case less clean than a simple divided-panel statutory split.

The September 18 supplemental brief reports a stay of the Ninth Circuit's mandate and quotes the respondent's counsel acknowledging the conflict in another appeal. These are useful party-reported developments, but the stay was consensual and the other brief is not this case's opposition. I do not adopt petitioners' inference that consenting to a stay concedes certworthiness, or treat the lower court's stay standard as the Supreme Court's forecast. The missing opposition leaves the most important uncertainty unresolved: whether the conflict and vehicle survive the respondent's strongest account. Taken together, the conflict and response request support a substantial increase from 5.16%, while those limitations keep my grant estimate at 24%, rather than near even odds.

## Other probabilities and stakes

**Further distribution: 0.97.** After the requested opposition or its deadline, another distribution is the natural next procedural step. That is an increment from the snapshot's count of one, even if it is not a substantive post-conference relist. I expect one additional distribution on the denial path, with another possible if review receives sustained consideration.

**New CVSG: 0.12.** ERISA implicates federal regulatory interests and the remedial question could benefit from the government's views. Nonetheless, private parties, an identified split, and an already requested opposition make ordinary handling more likely. If a CVSG occurs, I expect it after the opposition is considered, in early 2027 rather than before the present response deadline.

**Summary disposition conditional on grant: 0.04.** The provisioned record identifies no controlling intervening decision or lead-case order supporting a GVR. Resolving a disputed statutory interpretation embedded in an arbitration appeal would ordinarily call for briefing and argument. The low summary probability is conditional, not an unconditional 4% chance of a GVR.

**Separate denial writing conditional on denial: 0.05.** An unexplained denial is the clear modal outcome. The per-Justice order-writing probabilities are small, independent marginals, not estimates of cert votes. Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, and Kavanaugh are the relatively more plausible authors if a writing appears; none is individually likely. The 0.05 existence claim includes noted dissents as well as written statements and is not the sum of the writer marginals.

The **0.58 stakes score** reflects nationwide retirement-account recovery and arbitration consequences, not the likelihood of a grant. The question is economically significant but technically focused.
