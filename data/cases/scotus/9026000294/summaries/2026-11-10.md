## In brief

Terrorism victims ask the Court to decide whether courts, not the State Department, must independently determine if an entity qualifies for foreign sovereign immunity.

## What happened

Petitioners, victims of al-Qaeda's 1998 embassy bombings in Kenya and Tanzania, won a multibillion-dollar default judgment against the Taliban and sought to attach $3.5 billion in blocked funds held by Da Afghanistan Bank (DAB) to satisfy it. The district court denied confirmation of the attachment, deferring to a brief Justice Department letter calling DAB immune. A divided Second Circuit panel affirmed, holding it was bound by a State Department website and the Justice Department's litigation letter to treat Afghanistan as a "foreign state" entitled to immunity under a federal immunity statute. Rehearing en banc was denied over five dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, under the federal law governing when foreign governments and their agencies are immune from U.S. court jurisdiction and asset seizure, courts must work out for themselves whether an entity counts as a foreign state, or whether they must instead accept the State Department's informal statements and the Justice Department's litigation filings as conclusive on that question.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in August 2026, with an amicus brief filed in October. It was distributed for a November 6, 2026 conference and then redistributed for a November 13, 2026 conference; the docket shows no action on it yet.

## What each outcome would mean

The petitioners, terrorism victims holding an unpaid judgment against the Taliban, are asking the Court to take up their case and reverse the Second Circuit's ruling. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments and decide the question itself, resolving nothing yet. If it denies the petition, the Second Circuit's decision stands, leaving the DAB funds immune from attachment and the petitioners unable to use them to satisfy their judgment.
