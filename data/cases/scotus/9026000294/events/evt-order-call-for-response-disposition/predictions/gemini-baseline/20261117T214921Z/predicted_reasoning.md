# Predicted Court Action — *James Owens, et al. v. The Taliban, et al.* (No. 26-294)

## Procedural Trajectory: Relists, Response Window, and CVSG

The docket currently records **two** distributions (for the Conferences of November 6, 2026 and November 13, 2026), an invitation to the Solicitor General (CVSG) entered on November 16, 2026, and a call for a response entered on November 17, 2026 (due December 17, 2026).

1. **Further distributions (`relist-increment`):** Because both a CVSG and a response call are pending, the petition will almost certainly be distributed at least once more before disposition. Neither the defaulted Taliban nor garnishee Federal Reserve Bank of New York (which appeared as a neutral stakeholder on the consolidated Second Circuit caption) is likely to file an adversarial brief in opposition on the merits by December 17, 2026, though the NY Fed may submit a formal waiver or brief stakeholder response. The Solicitor General's invited amicus brief—covering this petition alongside companion dockets No. 26-292 (*Havlish v. The Taliban*) and No. 26-293 (*Ashton v. The Taliban*)—is expected in May 2027 (for late-June 2027 conference consideration) or late summer/autumn 2027 (for early OT2027 consideration). Once the Solicitor General files and petitioners submit supplemental briefs, the petition will be redistributed for conference, likely drawing **one to two additional distributions** (reaching 3–4 total distributions) before final disposition—or longer only if held behind a standalone grant on the Terrorism Risk Insurance Act (TRIA) question in No. 26-292.
2. **CVSG (`cvsg-increment`):** The Court has already called for the views of the Solicitor General on November 16, 2026; no further CVSG order will issue.

## Expected Disposition and Scope of Any Grant

The Court will **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari after receiving the Solicitor General's brief recommending denial.

- **If granted (conditional scope and vehicle structure):** Should the Court grant plenary review in No. 26-294, it would either consolidate No. 26-294 with Nos. 26-292 and 26-293 or pair No. 26-294 (where all nine Justices sit, unlike Nos. 26-292 and 26-293 in which Justice Kagan is recused) with No. 26-292 so both the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act (FSIA) and TRIA questions are before the Court, and appoint an amicus curiae to defend the judgment below if no respondent appears on the merits. Rather than taking petitioners' broad rhetoric framing Executive Branch websites as impermissible *Chevron*-style deference, the Court would either take the petition's Question Presented as a vehicle to address the narrower statutory issue under **28 U.S.C. §§ 1603(b) and 1609**—whether Da Afghanistan Bank (DAB) currently qualifies as an "agency or instrumentality of a foreign state" when a non-recognized terrorist organization exercises de facto control over it—or affirm presidential exclusivity over state recognition under *Zivotofsky v. Kerry*, 576 U.S. 1 (2015), while vacating and remanding on § 1603(b) instrumentality status.
- **Summary disposition vs. plenary review (`summary-disposition-route`):** Plenary review is the dominant route if the Court grants review. A summary reversal on a $3.5 billion central-bank immunity question is highly unlikely; the primary summary-disposition scenario is a **grant, vacate, and remand (GVR)** if the Court grants plenary review solely on the TRIA § 201(a) blocking-date question in No. 26-292 (*Havlish*), holds No. 26-294, and later vacates and remands the unified Second Circuit judgment for further consideration in light of *Havlish*, or if an intervening shift in U.S. diplomatic recognition or sanctions licensing alters the status of DAB's reserves.

## Separate Writings at the Certiorari Stage

Upon denial of certiorari, **most likely no Justice writes separately**, as the denial will follow a detailed Solicitor General brief emphasizing *Zivotofsky* recognition exclusivity, the protection of foreign central-bank reserves at the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, and the sui generis posture of post-2021 Afghanistan. There is, however, a meaningful minority chance (~13% conditional on denial) of a **dissent from the denial of certiorari or statement respecting denial**—most plausibly authored by **Justice Gorsuch**, **Justice Thomas**, or **Justice Alito**—criticizing the Second Circuit's reliance on defunct pre-Taliban Afghan banking law to treat a Taliban-controlled bank as an instrumentality of a recognized foreign sovereign under 28 U.S.C. § 1603(b).
