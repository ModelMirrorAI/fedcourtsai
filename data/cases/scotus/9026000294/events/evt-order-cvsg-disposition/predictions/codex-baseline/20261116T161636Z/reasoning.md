## Case-specific adjustments

The petition's question concerns whether courts must independently apply the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act, rather than treat executive materials as dispositive of foreign-state status. The petition, pp. 10–17 and 23–25, describes a divided published Second Circuit decision, five judges dissenting from denial of rehearing, conflicts with three other circuits, and consequences for terrorism judgments and approximately $3.5 billion in frozen assets. Those are substantial reasons for review. The actual CVSG confirms attention to federal interests rather than merely a sympathetic narrative. Together they move me moderately above the band anchor.

But the asserted split is not obviously a square conflict on identical facts. I checked the Third Circuit's opinion in *OI European Group B.V. v. Bolivarian Republic of Venezuela*, 73 F.4th 157 (3d Cir. 2023), through CourtListener, opinion 9407593, especially its discussion of sovereign continuity and the FSIA's allocation of immunity decisions to courts. It concerns a recognized state's oil-company alter ego and conduct of rival governments. That supports independent judicial analysis without necessarily deciding whether Afghanistan remains a state or DAB its instrumentality after regime change. The distinction between recognition of a state, recognition of its government, and statutory immunity could make this petition's stark question overstate the conflict. This is my inference from that precedent and the petition, not a verified holding of the unavailable opinion below.

There are also vehicle and federal-interest uncertainties. The petition, pp. 9–11 and 23, reports a prior government submission favoring immunity, nonappearance by the Taliban and DAB, and attachment proceedings followed by a default judgment. An invitation for government views can help the Court understand diplomatic consequences before denying review; it is not itself evidence that the government will recommend a grant. I do not guess its future recommendation. There is no provisioned brief in opposition, no separately staged appendix, and no decision-below text. The petition's appellate appendix references cannot substitute for reading the underlying opinions.

The related snapshots for **26-292 (Havlish)** and **26-293 (Ashton)** concern the same lower-court judgment, share the Faiq brief, and received CVSGs on the same day. Their relation is **`vided-amicus`**, with one shared brief each, not a Court-designated `Vide` link. The substantive match makes them genuine alternative vehicles, but I do not treat three simultaneous CVSGs as three independent favorable signals. Another petition could carry plenary review while this one is held and later summarily disposed of, or denied. This competition offsets part of the upward adjustment for importance and judicial disagreement. The resulting 39% is a modest, judgmental increase of about four percentage points, not a fitted likelihood calculation.
