## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to revive a land exchange with a fertilizer company that an appeals court blocked as unlawful.

## What happened

In 1900, Congress ratified an agreement under which the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes ceded part of their Fort Hall homeland, while reserving the Tribes' right to hunt, fish, and graze there until the land left public ownership. That law said the ceded land could be disposed of only under certain older categories of land law. In 2020 the Bureau of Land Management transferred part of that land to J.R. Simplot Company, a fertilizer producer, to expand a waste storage site, relying instead on a general 1976 public-lands law. The Tribes sued, and a federal district court ruled the transfer unlawful. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, agreeing that the 1900 law's listed categories were exclusive and that the 1976 law did not override them. The government and Simplot are asking the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a later, general public-lands law allows the government to transfer land that an older, specific law said could be disposed of only under certain named categories of land law, none of which include the newer statute.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including an opposition brief and a reply, and amicus briefs were filed supporting the petitioners. The case was distributed for the Court's conference of December 4, 2026, and no decision on whether to hear it has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, joined by Simplot, is asking the Court to take up the case and overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling that favored the Tribes. If the Court grants review, it will decide the legal question itself, with no party yet favored by that step alone. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision blocking the land transfer stands, leaving the Tribes' win in place. The case also reflects a district court ruling that struck down the transfer on three additional grounds the Ninth Circuit did not address, which would remain regardless of how this specific question is resolved.
