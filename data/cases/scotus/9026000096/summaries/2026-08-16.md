## What happened

In 1900, Congress ratified an agreement under which the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes ceded land in Idaho to the United States, providing that the land could later be sold or transferred only under certain listed 1900-era land laws. In 2020, the Department of the Interior approved exchanging part of that land with the J.R. Simplot Company, which operates a nearby fertilizer plant, relying instead on a newer 1976 law that broadly lets the government dispose of federal land. The Tribes sued, arguing the 1900 law's limits still control. The district court and, later, a divided Ninth Circuit panel agreed with the Tribes and blocked the exchange; the full Ninth Circuit declined to rehear the case, over several judges' dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The government is asking the Court to decide whether a modern, general-purpose federal law letting officials sell or trade government land can be used for land that came from a tribe long ago, even though the old law approving that original transfer said the land could only be disposed of under a specific, narrower set of laws that no longer function today. The government says the two laws work together; the Tribes' side, reflected in the lower court rulings, says the older law's limits still control unless Congress says otherwise.

## Where it stands
