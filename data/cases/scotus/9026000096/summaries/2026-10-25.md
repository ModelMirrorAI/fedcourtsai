## In brief

The federal government and a mining company ask the Court to let a 1976 land law override an 1900 statute restricting transfers of certain tribal lands.

## What happened

In 1900, Congress ratified an agreement under which the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes ceded Fort Hall lands to the United States, while reserving the Tribes' hunting, fishing, and grazing rights there and limiting future disposal of the ceded lands to four specific categories of federal land law. In 2020, the Bureau of Land Management transferred some of these lands to J.R. Simplot Co. to expand a fertilizer-waste storage site, relying on a general 1976 public-lands law instead. The Tribes sued, and the district court ruled the transfer unlawful on several grounds, including the 1900 statute. The Ninth Circuit affirmed on that ground alone, and denied rehearing en banc over several dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal agency may use a general, modern land-disposal law to transfer land away from the public domain when an older, specific statute says that land may be transferred only under certain named categories of law, none of which is the modern statute.

## Where it stands

The petitions were filed in July 2026, and after extensions, the Tribes filed a brief opposing review in October 2026. The docket shows no action yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The Department of the Interior and Simplot, who sought Supreme Court review, are asking the Court to overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which favored the Tribes. If the Court grants review and later reverses, the 1976 law would be available to authorize the land transfer to Simplot. If it affirms, the transfer would remain blocked under the 1900 statute's restrictions. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision favoring the Tribes stands, though the case would continue in the lower courts on other issues the Ninth Circuit did not reach.
