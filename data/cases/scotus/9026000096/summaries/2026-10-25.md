## In brief

The federal government and a fertilizer company ask the Court to let a land exchange on former tribal land proceed despite an older law restricting such transfers.

## What happened

In 1900, Congress ratified an agreement under which the Shoshone-Bannock Tribes ceded land but could keep using it for hunting, fishing, and grazing unless the government transferred it under certain named 19th-century land laws. In 2020, the Bureau of Land Management gave 713 acres of that land to J.R. Simplot Company, which wanted to expand a fertilizer-waste disposal site, in return for other land. The agency relied on a general 1976 public-lands law rather than the 1900 law. The Tribes sued, and a district court ruled the exchange unlawful on several grounds. The Ninth Circuit agreed on one ground and did not address the others; it also denied rehearing by the full court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal agency could rely on its general, later-enacted authority over public lands to transfer this land, even though an older, specific law says the land may only be transferred under a narrower, listed set of land laws that do not include the general one.

## Where it stands

The petitions were filed, and after extensions the Tribes filed a brief opposing review on October 21, 2026. The docket does not yet show any further action, such as a conference date or a decision on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The Department of the Interior and Simplot, the petitioners, are asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which favored the Tribes. If the Court grants review and later reverses, the agency's reliance on the general law would be upheld, potentially allowing the exchange to proceed, though the Tribes note other unresolved grounds for blocking it. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision invalidating the exchange stands. A grant without a decision would mean the Court will hear full arguments before resolving the question.
