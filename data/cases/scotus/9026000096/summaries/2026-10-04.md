## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to revive a land exchange with a fertilizer company, after a lower court blocked it using a 1900 law covering Shoshone-Bannock tribal land.

## What happened

In 2020 the Bureau of Land Management approved exchanging federal land, once part of the Fort Hall Reservation, for land owned by the J.R. Simplot Company, allowing Simplot to expand fertilizer-plant operations. The Shoshone-Bannock Tribes sued, arguing the exchange was unlawful. The district court agreed, and a divided Ninth Circuit panel affirmed, holding that an 1900 law governing the land's earlier transfer limited disposal to specific now-defunct methods, excluding the general land-management law the government relied on. The full Ninth Circuit declined to rehear the case over several judges' dissents.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a modern law letting the government sell or exchange federal land generally can still be used for land that was obtained from a tribe long ago, when the older law approving that original transfer said the land could only be disposed of under certain specific, now-defunct methods.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in July 2026. The respondent's deadline to reply has been extended twice, most recently to October 21, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on whether to hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

The Department of the Interior, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will decide the question itself, without yet favoring either side. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, keeping the exchange blocked and leaving the government, by its own account, without a clear way to dispose of the affected land under current law. A grant would not itself decide who wins; it would set the case for full briefing and argument.
