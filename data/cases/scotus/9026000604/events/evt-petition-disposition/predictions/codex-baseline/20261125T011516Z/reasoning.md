I put the chance of any grant at **9%**. Eta preserved the search issue in his guilty plea, and the customs officer testified that the purpose of reading his phones was to help the FBI investigate fraud. The officer specifically disclaimed a search for contraband or an immigration purpose. That makes the conflict with the Ninth Circuit’s rule in *Cano* concrete. The Seventh Circuit expressly held that officers need no case-specific border justification for a routine manual search. A national rule would matter wherever travelers carry private communications across the border. See the question presented, petition pp. 9–15 and 26–31, and appendix pp. 6a–7a, 13a–17a and 43a.

The principal obstacle is the Seventh Circuit’s second holding. Even if the search violated the Fourth Amendment, the panel would admit the evidence because the agents had corroborated grounds to suspect Eta of fraud and acted in objective good faith under the law at the time. The district court also found inevitable discovery, although the panel did not adopt that ground. Eta has a substantial answer in *Chatrie*, where the Court decided a digital privacy issue and left good faith for reconsideration on remand. Here, however, he presents only the constitutional question. The panel also found a territorial interest in the transnational fraud itself. Some of the decisions Eta cites involved forensic examinations, while his preserved challenge concerns the officers scrolling through the phones by hand. Those differences make the claimed one–two–six circuit division a poor count of courts that would actually suppress this evidence. See petition pp. 10–12 & n.2 and 32–35, and appendix pp. 12a–19a and 35a.
