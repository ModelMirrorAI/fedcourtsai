## 2. Case-Specific Adjustments

Starting from the `2.69%–5.18%` baseline range, two opposing sets of case-specific features pull in opposite directions and net out to **4.5%** (`0.045`):

### Upward pressures
1. **Active related petition with a CFR (*Anibowei v. Blanche*, No. 26-208):** Corpus retrieval confirms that *Anibowei v. Blanche*, No. 26-208 (docketed August 19, 2026, from the Fifth Circuit), presenting whether the Fourth Amendment permits warrantless border cell-phone searches, drew **10 cert-stage amicus briefs** (including Cato Institute, Constitutional Accountability Center, Rutherford Institute, ABA, Fourth Amendment Scholars, and Project for Privacy & Surveillance Accountability), received a **Call for Response on September 29, 2026** (`Pet. 34`), and remains open (`disposition: null` as of November 22, 2026). Petitioner explicitly asks the Court (`Pet. 34–35`) either to grant and consolidate *Eta* with *Anibowei* (analogizing to *Riley v. California* and *United States v. Wurie*, 573 U.S. 373 (2014)) or, *"at a minimum, hold this one for Anibowei."* This creates a concrete **hold-then-GVR** pathway (~2.7%) if *Anibowei* is granted and resolved favorably to travelers, plus a modest **CFR-and-companion-grant** pathway (~1.8%).
2. **Elite Supreme Court counsel and clean factual concession on purpose:** Petitioner is represented by Jones Day's Supreme Court practice (Brendan D. Duffy; former Assistant to the Solicitor General Anthony J. Dick is noted on the Seventh Circuit panel opinion), appealing a published Seventh Circuit decision (`180 F.4th 936`) in which the government expressly conceded below (`Pet.App. 38a–39a, 43a`) that CBP conducted the secondary inspection not to look for contraband or verify admissibility, but "to assist the FBI" in a fraud investigation.
3. **Genuine circuit divergence on border-nexus limits and post-*Chatrie* salience:** Nine circuits have addressed border electronic-device searches, dividing between *United States v. Cano*, 934 F.3d 1002 (9th Cir. 2019) (digital contraband only), *Alasaad v. Mayorkas*, 988 F.3d 8 (1st Cir. 2021) and *United States v. Aigbekaen*, 943 F.3d 713 (4th Cir. 2019) (border-nexus requirement for nonroutine searches), and six circuits requiring neither (`CA2`, `CA5`, `CA7`, `CA8`, `CA10`, `CA11`). The Court also demonstrated continued engagement with digital Fourth Amendment questions last Term in *Chatrie v. United States*, No. 25-112 (decided June 29, 2026).

### Downward pressures (decisive against plenary review)
1. **Solicitor General waiver and superiority of *Anibowei* as the merits vehicle:** The Solicitor General waived a response in *Eta* on November 17, 2026, and zero amici have filed here, whereas the civil-liberties bar rallied 10 amicus briefs behind *Anibowei* (No. 26-208). Unlike *Chatrie* (where geofence warrants arise almost exclusively in criminal prosecutions, leaving no civil vehicle), *Anibowei* is a **civil** action seeking return/expungement of copied phone data, completely free of exclusionary-rule obstacles.
2. **Airtight `Davis` good-faith alternative holding on a February 2018 search (`Pet.App. 17a–19a`):** Judge St. Eve squarely held in Part II.B that *"Even if Eta prevailed on the Fourth Amendment merits, we would nonetheless affirm because the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule applies."* In February 2018 — before *Cano* (2019) or *Aigbekaen* (2019) had been decided — binding Supreme Court precedent (*Ramsey*, *Montoya de Hernandez*, *Flores-Montano*) and Seventh Circuit precedent (*Yang*) authorized suspicionless routine border searches, no circuit required more than reasonable suspicion even for forensic searches, and the district court found after a four-witness evidentiary hearing that agents possessed ample corroborated reasonable suspicion (two informants, bank flows exceeding $1 million, and >1,000 messages/day with an indicted co-conspirator). Because `Davis v. United States`, 564 U.S. 229 (2011), looks to objectively reasonable reliance on precedent *at the time of the 2018 search*, even a prospective Fourth Amendment victory in *Anibowei* would likely leave Eta's wire-fraud conviction intact, making *Eta* unattractive both as a plenary vehicle and even as a hold candidate.
3. **Manual-search posture and transnational-crime finding blunt the split (`Pet. 11 n.2`; `Pet.App. 6a–7a, 15a`):** All nine circuits (`9–0`) reject a categorical **warrant** requirement at the border. On the narrower **purpose-nexus** divide, CBP officer Coder conducted a **manual** scroll search of Eta's phones and obtained a judicial probable-cause **warrant** two days later before searching the forensic extractions (`Pet.App. 6a–7a, 16a–17a, 22a n.1`), whereas the First and Fourth Circuits attach heightened suspicion/nexus rules to *forensic/nonroutine* searches. Moreover, the Seventh Circuit explicitly held in the alternative (`Pet.App. 15a`) that Eta's Nigerian cyber-fraud and cross-border money-laundering enterprise was an ongoing **transnational** crime, satisfying the First and Fourth Circuits' border-nexus standard (*United States v. Nkongho*, 107 F.4th 373, 381 (4th Cir. 2024); *Alasaad*, 988 F.3d at 19). That leaves tension at most with *Cano* — and in June 2021 the Court denied *both* the Solicitor General's own petition in *United States v. Cano*, No. 20-1043, and the civil companion petition in *Merchant v. Mayorkas*, No. 20-1505.
