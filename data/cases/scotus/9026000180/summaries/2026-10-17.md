## In brief

A father asks the Court to revive his civil rights lawsuit against University of California doctors and others after his son underwent gender-related surgery he says he never approved.

## What happened

After Edward Hudacko's divorce, a state family court gave his ex-wife, Christine Underhill, authority over their son's medical care, but required Hudacko's written consent for "any gender identity related surgery." Hudacko alleges that university doctors and others arranged a hormone-implant procedure on his son without his knowledge or consent, and that he learned of it only later through an insurance bill. He sued under federal civil rights law. The district court dismissed his claims, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding the defendants had qualified immunity—legal protection for officials unless they violated clearly established law—because Hudacko had not pointed to prior court rulings with similar facts.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in deciding if a right was "clearly established" for purposes of this immunity, courts may look only to prior judicial rulings, or whether they may also consider other sources, such as statutes, professional standards, or information the officials actually had. The Court is also asked whether all government officials should get the same immunity test, or whether courts should first ask whether similar officials traditionally had immunity under historical common-law practice.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference. The Court then asked the respondents to file a response, due by a later deadline that has since been extended to November 18, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Hudacko, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the legal questions about qualified immunity, without yet deciding who should win. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling dismissing Hudacko's claims on qualified immunity grounds stands, ending his federal civil rights lawsuit against the university defendants and others named in the case.
