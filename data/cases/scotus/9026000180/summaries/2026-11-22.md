## In brief

A father whose parental rights were limited by a custody order asks the Court to revive his civil rights lawsuit over his son's medical treatment.

## What happened

Edward Hudacko and Christine Underhill divorced, and a California family court gave Underhill sole authority over their son's medical decisions, except that the son could not have "gender identity related surgery" without Hudacko's consent. The son later received a hormone-suppressing implant arranged by Underhill and UCSF doctors. Hudacko sued the doctors, university officials, Underhill, and two attorneys, claiming this violated his parental rights. A federal district court dismissed his claims, finding the officials entitled to qualified immunity, a legal protection shielding officials from damages suits unless they violated clearly established law. The Ninth Circuit affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, in deciding if an official's conduct violated clearly established law, courts may only look to prior court rulings with similar facts, or whether they may also consider other sources like state orders, professional standards, or an official's own knowledge. The Court is also asked whether it should change the general rule for this kind of immunity by first asking whether officials like these historically received immunity under common law.

## Where it stands

The case was distributed for the Court's October 9, 2026 conference, after which the Court requested a response from the respondents, who had initially waived their right to respond. The respondents have since filed briefs opposing the petition, and the case now awaits the Court's decision on whether to hear it.

## What each outcome would mean

Hudacko, the father, is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which favored the Regents of the University of California and the other defendants. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions about qualified immunity; this would not by itself determine who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, leaving the dismissal of Hudacko's lawsuit in place and the defendants protected from his claims.
