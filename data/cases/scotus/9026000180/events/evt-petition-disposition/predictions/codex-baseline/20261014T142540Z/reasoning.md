I raise the forecast to **P(any grant) = 0.16**. The request for a response is the strongest case-specific positive signal: the Court requested adversarial briefing rather than disposing of the petition on waivers. Five amicus entries and a question reaching qualified immunity beyond this family dispute further distinguish it from an ordinary baseline petition. Neither the response request nor the amicus count is itself a CVSG or a merits endorsement. The adjustment is judgmental; the pack supplies no response-request-conditioned rate that would justify a precise multiplier.

The first QP alleges disagreement over whether clearly established law can be shown through sources other than judicial precedent, including officials' professional knowledge. The second asks for a historically grounded threshold inquiry into whether the official's category enjoyed immunity. I give the first question appreciably more grant potential than the second.

Important offsets keep denial the modal disposition:

- The attached panel disposition is expressly unpublished and nonprecedential (appendix 1a). Its reasoning at 4a-5a is tied to a particular custody order: the other parent had medical decision-making authority, with an exception for gender-identity-related surgery. The panel found the implant's coverage by that exception unclear and identified no clearly established parental right under the particular circumstances. This is not a published holding expressly resolving every source-of-law question in the petition's broad split formulation.
- Whether the procedure meets a medical definition of surgery and whether performing it violated clearly established federal constitutional law are related but distinct propositions. The petition argues that the doctors' alleged knowledge bridges them; the panel's narrower account leaves room for vehicle objections even if nonjudicial materials can matter in some immunity cases. I do not assume the Court will accept the petition's characterization of the disagreement.
- Petition page 36 expressly acknowledges that the second question was not raised below and invokes futility. I cannot resolve that preservation argument without opposition briefing, but it is a substantial obstacle to using this case to revise the architecture of qualified immunity.
- Other parties' state-action and conspiracy issues, discussed in the attached memorandum, complicate the litigation, although the public medical defendants provide an identifiable focus. I do not treat those independent issues as necessarily defeating relief against every defendant.

For general doctrinal context only, I retrieved and read *Taylor v. Riojas*, 592 U.S. 7 (2020), CourtListener opinion 4582848. The per curiam opinion permits an obvious-violation analysis without a factually identical precedent; it does not establish that medical training or a disputed custody-order classification alone establishes a federal right. This makes narrower corrective intervention conceivable while also weakening the need for the Court to announce the petition's broad proposition from scratch. It is an analogy, not evidence about Hudacko's outcome. I retrieved no subsequent history or disposition of this case and do not independently know its outcome.

**Separate writing given denial, 0.25:** The petition directly appeals to concerns about parental rights and Justice Thomas's historical critique of immunity. Those arguments make a recorded objection plausible, but denial without separate writing remains more likely. This probability concerns aggregated existence, not any individual Justice's vote.

**Stakes, 0.72:** The potential scope of a rule about sources of clearly established law or immunity for public medical personnel is large, and the parental-consent setting is salient. The unusual custody order and procedural vehicle constrain the likely reach. This is a stakes assessment, not a second grant probability.
