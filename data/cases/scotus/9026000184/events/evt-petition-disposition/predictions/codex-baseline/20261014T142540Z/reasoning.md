## Anchor and adjustment

The applicable anchor is the **bracketed reached rate for baseline under sal-v4**, not the terminal baseline rate and not a government-petitioner rate merely because the respondent is a state officer. Pooling every displayed prior Term, 2017 through 2025, using `metrics/statpack.json`'s exact `prefix_est_grant_rate` and `prefix_weighted_resolved` fields gives **643 / 12,877 = 4.9934%**. I exclude Term 2026. These are the committed pack's denial-reweighted historical estimates, not a newly queried live-corpus count. The pack does not expose a corpus-wide refresh timestamp in the inspected material, so I make no claim that these estimates describe an independently refreshed corpus today.

I raise that roughly 5% anchor to **22% P(any grant)** for three case-specific reasons:

- **Affirmative attention after waiver.** The Court requested an opposition one day after distribution. This is stronger evidence of interest than a routine first distribution, but does not establish four votes for review.
- **A substantial First Amendment disagreement.** Petition QP 2 and the introduction allege a conflict with *Junger v. Daley*, 209 F.3d 481, 484–85 (6th Cir. 2000). The Third Circuit's own retained opinion, Appendix A at 27a–34a, criticizes Junger's categorical reasoning and requires contextual indicia of expression. I independently read Junger through CourtListener, opinion 768337, dated April 4, 2000: it recognizes source code as protected expression and treats functionality in the regulation analysis. This supports a genuine difference in doctrinal approach, rather than merely accepting the petition's characterization.
- **A separate institutional conflict.** The petition's first question and Volume II, Appendix K at 296a–299a, show a serious dispute over the effect of the Fifth Circuit's transfer ruling and return request. That adds a supervisory reason to consider the case, although it is not automatically a broad, clean conflict over the same rule in ordinary cases.

The upward adjustment is bounded by substantial vehicle problems. Appendix A at 35a–38a identifies **two pleading deficiencies**: inadequate allegations connecting particular files to the statute's prohibition, and inadequate allegations of expressive use. It also states that plaintiffs declined an opportunity to amend. A decision favoring petitioners on the general coverage rule may therefore leave an additional pleading obstacle. Junger concerned a professor's encryption source code, whereas the Third Circuit says this record does not adequately identify the nature and use of the CAD/CAM files. That distinction could let the Court await a cleaner case without resolving the full constitutional boundary.

On transfer, Appendix A at 12a–14a treats the Fifth Circuit ruling as arising after the transferred claims had left that circuit's jurisdiction, distinguishes a return request from a binding order, and stresses discretion. Petitioners contest that account. The exceptional procedural history both attracts attention and makes the case less suitable for a general venue rule. I do not count the two questions as independent chances of grant or equate their importance with likely review. The missing BIO is my largest remaining informational limitation. The 22% figure is a judgmental adjustment, not an empirically fitted response-request or circuit-split rate.
