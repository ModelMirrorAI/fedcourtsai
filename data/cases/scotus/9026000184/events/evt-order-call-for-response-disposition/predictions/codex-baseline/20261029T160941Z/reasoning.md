## Why 20%, rather than the 5.1% anchor

The response request is a real positive attention signal following a waiver, but the threshold for requesting a response is much lower than the four-vote threshold for review. I increase the prior for that attention and, more importantly, for a substantive conflict visible in the actual opinion rather than merely asserted in the petition. The resulting 20% is a judgmental forecast, not a measured response-request conditional frequency or a fitted multiplier.

**The strongest upward evidence is question 2.** Petition pages 24–26 and Appendix A pages 27a–34a show that the Third Circuit engages Junger v. Daley, 209 F.3d 481 (6th Cir. 2000), rejects automatic coverage based on code's capacity to communicate, and requires contextual indicia of expressive use. This is more concrete than routine disagreement with an adverse application of settled law. Petitioners also invoke Universal City Studios, Inc. v. Corley, 273 F.3d 429 (2d Cir. 2001). Importantly, Appendix A's own discussion describes Corley as distinguishing programmer-to-programmer communication from functional machine instructions. That makes the asserted conflict with Corley less categorical than the petition's framing, while leaving a substantial tension with Junger. A general-source search did not yield a separately opened Junger opinion, and the attempted CourtListener citation lookup was throttled; my account of Junger therefore rests on the provisioned petition and lower opinion, not a claim to have independently read it.

**Vehicle weaknesses keep denial clearly favored.** Appendix A pages 34a–38a identifies two pleading deficiencies: uncertainty whether the particular files are actually reached by the statute, and inadequate factual allegations of expressiveness. The panel also says petitioners declined an opportunity to amend. Even correcting an overly restrictive coverage standard might leave a fact-specific applicability or pleading obstacle. CAD/CAM files used for manufacturing are not identical to the educational encryption source code described in Junger. Those distinctions allow the Court to await a cleaner case despite the broader constitutional stakes.

**The transfer question adds some grant potential but is not a second independent clean split.** Petition pages 17–23 describe a striking same-litigation conflict. Appendix A pages 12a–16a, however, treats the transferred and retained actions as distinct after severance, emphasizes limits on the Fifth Circuit's jurisdiction, characterizes the Texas communication as a request rather than a binding command, and reviews the New Jersey court's discretionary transfer analysis. Those points weaken a simple account in which one court ignored an unquestionably binding mandate. I checked the general law-of-the-case background against Christianson v. Colt Industries Operating Corp., 486 U.S. 800, 816–819 (1988), in the official U.S. Reports scan. Its transfer principles make the administrative concern substantial but do not by themselves settle this unusual post-severance posture. This is not a prediction that the lower court's venue analysis is necessarily correct.

Balancing an identifiable constitutional tension and affirmative Court attention against serious vehicle problems produces **P(any grant)=0.20**, with denial the modal disposition. An illustrative allocation consistent with that total is approximately 17.6% plenary grant, 2.4% cert-order grant route, 79% denial, and 1% dismissal/withdrawal or another nongrant exit; these allocations are judgmental, not empirical frequencies.
