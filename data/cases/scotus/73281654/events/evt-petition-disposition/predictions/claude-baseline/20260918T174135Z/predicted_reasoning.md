# Predicted reasoning — Winnemucca Indian Colony v. United States, No. 25-1170

**Disposition: denied**, most likely on the order list following the September 28, 2026 conference (the first conference at which the petition will actually be considered), or after at most one further relist or reschedule.

## Relists

The docket shows **two distributions** (May 12 for the May 28 conference; September 9 for the September 28 conference), but the first was overtaken by the Court's May 27 call for a response after the Solicitor General waived, so the petition has never yet been considered at conference. It sits, in substance, at its first conference. I expect it to be **denied at or shortly after September 28 without a further distribution**. A single further distribution (a reschedule at the long conference, or a one-conference relist while a chamber decides whether to write) is the main alternative, roughly one chance in five; two or more further distributions would be surprising.

## Call for the views of the Solicitor General

**None.** The United States is the respondent and has already filed its brief in opposition through the Solicitor General; a CVSG is not available on this docket.

## Which question the Court would take, if it granted

If the Court took the case at all it would reformulate. The petition's three questions are overlapping and partly framed as pure error correction ("did the Federal Circuit err"). The only question with a cert-worthy shape is the one the Solicitor General restated: whether the Winters reserved-water-rights doctrine, alone or read into 25 C.F.R. 152.22, is a "substantive source of law" imposing a specific, money-mandating duty on the United States to prevent a third party's upstream diversion of water from a reservation. The Court would take that single question and drop the petition's second and third framings. It would not take the case to revisit Arizona v. Navajo Nation.

## Summary disposition

**Unlikely.** No intervening decision of this Court bears on the Tucker Act threshold or on Winters, so there is nothing to GVR in light of, and the Federal Circuit's published opinion is a straightforward application of Navajo Nation that offers no target for a per curiam reversal. If the Court granted, it would almost certainly set the case for briefing and argument rather than dispose of it in the cert order.

## Dissent from denial

**Probably none.** The Navajo Nation dissenters (Gorsuch, Sotomayor, Kagan, Jackson) are the natural source of any writing, and the call for a response suggests at least one chamber looked closely. But the vehicle is poor in ways that do not turn on the Winters question: the Court of Federal Claims dismissed the same claim on two independent jurisdictional grounds (28 U.S.C. 1500 and the six-year limitations period) that the Federal Circuit did not reach, and the underlying facts are an intractable intra-tribal governance dispute. A Justice inclined to write about tribal trust duties after Navajo Nation would wait for a cleaner case. A short statement respecting denial by Justice Gorsuch is the only realistic writing, and I do not expect it.

## Conditional merits note

Were review granted, the likely result would be affirmance: Navajo Nation's requirement that a trust duty be located in the text of a treaty, statute, or regulation, not in common-law trust principles, maps directly onto the petitioner's reliance on a judge-made doctrine (Winters) and a land-conveyance regulation that does not mention water.
