## In brief

The Court declined to hear the Winnemucca Indian Colony's bid to revive its damages claim against the United States over an upstream water diversion.

## What happened

The Winnemucca Indian Colony sued the United States for money damages, arguing that during a long internal leadership dispute, federal officials failed to recognize any tribal government for about two decades and, as a result, failed to stop a third-party company from diverting a stream that had supplied water to part of the Colony's reservation. The Court of Federal Claims dismissed the claim, finding no law imposing a specific duty on the government to prevent such diversions, and also citing two independent jurisdictional bars: an overlap with earlier related litigation and an expired filing deadline. The Federal Circuit affirmed, agreeing the Colony had not identified a legal source imposing that duty.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether a longstanding legal doctrine recognizing water rights tied to tribal reservations, combined with a federal land-trust regulation, creates a specific legal duty requiring the government to pay damages when it fails to stop an outside party from diverting water before it reaches the reservation.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond but was later asked to respond; after briefing, including a brief in opposition and a reply, the petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court denied it on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Colony, which lost in the lower courts, asked the Court to take up its case. By denying the petition, the Court left the Federal Circuit's ruling in place, meaning the Colony's damages claim over the water diversion remains dismissed and the United States' win below stands. Had the Court instead granted the petition, it would have agreed to hear full arguments on whether such a duty exists, without yet deciding who was right.
