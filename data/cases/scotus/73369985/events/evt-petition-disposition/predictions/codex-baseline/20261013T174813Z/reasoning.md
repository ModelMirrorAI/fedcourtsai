## Why 0.58 rather than the anchor

I read the questions presented, the petition's introduction and principal grounds, the BIO's introduction and opposing grounds, the reply's principal responses, and the relevant panel and rehearing passages in the appendix. References below use the filings' printed pagination.

The strongest upward adjustment is **a plausible summary-correction vehicle on clearly established law**, not the mere assertion of a circuit split. QP 2 and the petition at pp. 26–33 challenge the use of generalized rights at the pleading stage despite extensive body-camera evidence. The panel's actual immunity analysis, App. 17a–19a, describes rights against shooting a nonthreatening, compliant, incapacitated person, but the central controversy is whether reaching for an accessible firearm after the initial shots fits that description. The petition and reply press the absence of a sufficiently similar precedent. That affords a narrower way to grant relief than deciding the entire Fourth Amendment dispute.

The depth of disagreement increases the chance that this is more than an ordinary unsuccessful police petition. The petition, pp. 11–14, reports eight judges dissenting from denial of rehearing across four opinions. The provisioned Thapar/Hermandorfer dissent particularly contests the notion that further discovery could cure the clearly-established-law problem. The snapshot also shows four supporting amicus filings, from police and municipal interests. These are evidence of sustained institutional attention, not proof that the officers' factual interpretation is correct.

For general precedent, I verified **Zorn v. Linton, No. 25-297 (March 23, 2026), per curiam, pp. 3–6**, through CourtListener. It granted certiorari and reversed an immunity denial for insufficiently specific precedent. This supports the availability of summary correction even without a clean categorical circuit split. Zorn involved a different use of force and summary judgment, however; it is an analogy, not controlling resolution of this petition. Its decision predates the petition and appears in the provisioned advocacy, so it is ordinary forward context.

There are important downward adjustments. The BIO, pp. 1–4 and 11–19, makes a credible case that the petition disputes interpretation of the videos and well-pleaded allegations rather than a legal rule. The panel itself, App. 5a–6a, says it considered the recordings and found them inconclusive at the relevant points. Its Barnes discussion, App. 8a–12a, expressly considers prior events and the changing threat rather than announcing a categorical time restriction. I verified **Barnes v. Felix, 605 U.S. 73, 79–81 (2025)** through CourtListener: the earlier context can favor either side. Thus the petition's portrayal of Barnes as compelling the officers' result overstates what that decision establishes.

The petition and reply offer cross-circuit factual analogies and identify a disagreement about use of video at the pleading stage, but this is not an uncontested clean split on the exact rule applied below. The Sixth Circuit did consider video here. Further, discovery might distinguish the surrender and threat narratives. The interlocutory posture therefore weakens the vehicle, although it is not a categorical bar where immunity from litigation is itself at stake. These objections keep the forecast near a contested grant/deny boundary rather than at high confidence. The increase from about 35% to 58% is a judgmental adjustment for the specific immunity-correction vehicle and strength of the dissents, not a fitted numerical model.
