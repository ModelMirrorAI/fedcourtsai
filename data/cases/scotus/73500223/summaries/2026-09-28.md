## In brief

Shopping center owners ask the Court to overturn a 40-year-old precedent and let them bar an activist's leafletting on their property.

## What happened

Majestic Realty and related companies own shopping centers in Redlands, California, that ban leafletting and petitioning. Alex Salazar, an activist, asked to distribute flyers there and was refused under that policy. He sued, arguing California's constitution protects his right to leaflet on privately owned property open to the public. Relying on the California Supreme Court's PruneYard decision, which the U.S. Supreme Court had upheld in 1980, a California appeals court agreed and issued a preliminary injunction against the centers' ban. California's Supreme Court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a state violates property owners' rights against uncompensated takings, and their right not to be forced to convey messages, when it requires owners of privately owned commercial property open to the public to allow unwanted leafletting or similar expressive activity there. The owners argue newer rulings on property and speech rights have made the older precedent unworkable; Salazar answers that those rulings distinguished rather than undercut it, and that the case is a poor vehicle for revisiting it because it arises from an early, preliminary stage of the lawsuit.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including several amicus briefs supporting the shopping centers, and was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026. The docket shows no action since that distribution.

## What each outcome would mean

Majestic Realty and the other shopping center owners are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide whether the owners' rights were violated, deciding nothing at this stage about who wins. If the Court denies review, the California appeals court's preliminary ruling favoring Salazar stands, and the lawsuit continues in the lower courts without the Supreme Court weighing in; a denial would not mean the Court agrees with that ruling, only that it lets it stand for now.
