## In brief

The Court declined to hear shopping center owners' bid to use a leafletting dispute to overturn a 40-year-old precedent allowing protected speech on private commercial property.

## What happened

Majestic Realty Co. and related entities own shopping centers in Redlands, California, that ban leafletting and similar activity. Alex Salazar, an activist, asked to distribute leaflets about men's child-support obligations; the centers refused under their policy. Salazar sued, arguing California's constitution protects such speech on privately owned shopping centers open to the public. A trial court denied his request for a preliminary injunction, but the California Court of Appeal reversed, holding Salazar was likely to succeed under a California Supreme Court precedent, later affirmed by the U.S. Supreme Court, that permits this kind of speech on shopping-center property. The state's highest court denied further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The centers ask the Court to decide whether a state can require owners of commercial property open to the public to allow unwanted leafletting and similar activity without violating the owners' right to exclude others from their land or their right not to be forced to host speech they disagree with. They argue newer rulings about physical invasions of property and compelled speech have undermined the older precedent allowing such access. Salazar answers that the older precedent still controls and that the case is a poor vehicle for revisiting it.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and the Court denied it on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Majestic Realty and the other property owners asked the Court to take up the case. By denying the petition, the Court leaves in place the California Court of Appeal's decision, which found Salazar likely to succeed in his claim that the centers must allow his leafletting activity. The owners' challenge to that preliminary ruling ends at this stage, though the underlying lawsuit, which was still at the preliminary-injunction phase, may continue in the lower courts.
