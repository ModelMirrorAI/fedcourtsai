## Why this petition can attract review

The petition raises a concrete dispute about when performance begins under the military-commission pretrial-agreement withdrawal rule and whether the government's position could satisfy the demanding mandamus standard. The claimed conflict with Dean is substantial enough not to dismiss as a generic assertion of error. The petition emphasizes identical regulatory language, pre-execution factual stipulations, and conduct following execution; it also relies on the military tribunals' disagreement with the D.C. Circuit and Judge Wilkins's dissent. See petition pp. 17–23, 24–34.

I checked Dean's majority opinion directly through CourtListener, opinion 9500279. At 67 M.J. 227–28, it treats performance as extending beyond entry of a guilty plea and identifies several acts, including a factual stipulation and a later amended witness list. That supports the petition's contention that the precedent matters. It does not independently settle whether the specific stipulation timing and non-questioning of a witness here compel the same result. The government's effort to distinguish the post-agreement witness-list filing has a real factual foundation in Dean.

The stakes are unusually high: both briefs connect enforceability of the agreements to whether death remains an available punishment in the September 11 prosecutions. This is a reason for greater potential institutional attention, not a prediction that significance alone supplies four votes. See petition pp. 34–36; opposition pp. 3–8, 18–19.

## Why denial remains more likely

The opposition provides substantial cert-vehicle answers. It argues that the stipulation preceded the government's acceptance; that not questioning a witness was not an express contractual promise; that waiver depended on later judicial acceptance; and that the judge preserved the opportunity for questioning. It also disputes detrimental reliance and says some additional promises were not raised below. These are the government's positions, not independently established facts beyond the record supplied. See opposition pp. 8–13.

On the alleged conflict, the government accepts that performance of promises other than a guilty plea can bar withdrawal but distinguishes Dean's actual post-execution performance. It argues that the courts-martial rule has been replaced and that the D.C. Circuit has exclusive appellate jurisdiction over the remaining military-commission rule, reducing the prospect of an ongoing, broadly recurring inter-circuit conflict. It also distinguishes disagreement about applying the mandamus standard from disagreement about the standard itself. See opposition pp. 13–18. Those arguments create a plausible path to treating the petition as a consequential but narrow dispute rather than a necessary plenary intervention.

Starting from about 17.2%, I move upward for the serious military-court conflict argument, mandamus posture, and extraordinary consequences. I move downward for the limited prospective reach of the precise rule, the government's specific vehicle objections, and the absence of confirmed conference consideration behind the elevated distribution count. These opposing qualitative adjustments leave my forecast near, but slightly above, the anchor at 18%. This is a judgmental forecast, not a fitted model or a claim that the adjustments are empirically estimated. The largest uncertainty is whether the Court sees the mandamus issue as a systemic supervisory problem rather than case-specific contract interpretation.
