## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a Guantanamo detainee's bid to revive a plea agreement that would have spared him the death penalty.

## What happened

Walid bin 'Atash is charged before a military commission with helping plan the September 11 attacks. In 2024 a military official accepted a pretrial agreement under which bin 'Atash would plead guilty in exchange for the government not seeking the death penalty. Two days later the Secretary of Defense withdrew from the agreement. A military judge and the Court of Military Commission Review both ruled that bin 'Atash had already begun performing promises in the agreement, so the withdrawal came too late. The United States Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit disagreed, ruling for the government and clearing the way for the case to proceed without the plea deal. One judge dissented. Bin 'Atash then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether it was clear and indisputable error to find that bin 'Atash had not yet started carrying out his side of the plea agreement, given that he had signed a detailed factual confession and taken other steps the government treated as required by the deal, before the government could still rely on an extraordinary order from the appeals court to undo the agreement.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for several conferences, including ones in September and October 2026, and the Supreme Court denied it on October 19, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Bin 'Atash, who asked the Court to hear the case, sought to overturn the D.C. Circuit's ruling that favored the government. Because the Court denied review, the D.C. Circuit's decision stands: the Secretary of Defense's withdrawal from the plea agreement is treated as valid, bin 'Atash's guilty plea and the death-penalty waiver do not take effect, and his case continues before the military commission without that agreement in place. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the D.C. Circuit's reasoning, only that it left that ruling undisturbed.
