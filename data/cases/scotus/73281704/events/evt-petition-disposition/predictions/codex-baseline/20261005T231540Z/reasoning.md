## Reasons for adjusting below the anchor

**The first question is consequential and supported by an identifiable disagreement.** The petition's QP1 asks whether a circuit split can establish debatability for a certificate of appealability despite contrary home-circuit law. Its discussion of Hamilton, Mitchell, and Lambright presents more than an assertion that Clark lost an important case. It also acknowledges that its Third Circuit example is less direct and the Tenth Circuit examples are unpublished. The petition's own analysis therefore supports a disagreement without making the advertised 3-3 split equally strong in all six circuits. See petition pp. 8-20. That keeps the grant probability appreciably above an ordinary unselected private petition's rate.

**The asserted predicate split is much less clean in this vehicle.** QP2 asks whether severe financial sanctions can qualify for collateral review under section 2255. The opposition argues that the Second Circuit has reserved a hypothetical exception rather than granted relief for circumstances like Clark's; it distinguishes the Sixth Circuit decisions as ineffective-assistance cases. See opposition pp. 8-12. I checked the historical Second Circuit opinion in *United States v. Rutigliano*, 887 F.3d 98, 105-07, 111 (2d Cir. 2018), through CourtListener. Its rejection of the restitution challenges, and its distinction between payment terms and amount alone, support a narrower characterization than a square split over Clark's own claim. This does not eliminate doctrinal tension, but it weakens the contention that another circuit would resolve his case differently. The petition emphasizes nearly $488 million in obligations and their practical burdens; magnitude alone does not answer the Second Circuit's stated inquiry.

**Independent obstacles reduce the payoff from resolving QP1 here.** The opposition pp. 13-14 argues that Clark failed to challenge the financial components directly, then voluntarily abandoned his appeal. That is the government's asserted procedural-default obstacle, not a separate holding I attribute to the orders below. The petition pp. 5-7 explains the attempted supplemental briefing and counsel's advice but does not remove the resulting vehicle dispute. In addition, the opposition pp. 6-7 describes the appellate order as applying both the procedural and underlying-constitutional components of Slack's COA inquiry. Resolving how a circuit split bears on one component may not establish the other. I therefore do not treat a potentially persuasive criticism of the COA standard as sufficient to produce review in this case.

**A related petition creates a possible alternative route, not evidence of a known outcome.** The petition p. 8 and opposition p. 13 identify *Berry v. United States*, No. 25-7026, as presenting the COA issue. I rely only on those filed descriptions. I did not search Berry's current status or Clark's disposition. A cleaner related vehicle could lead to a hold and later reconsideration here, but it could also make this petition unnecessary as the lead case.

The adjustment from 17.2% to 12% balances a real procedural issue and observed renewed distribution against the disputed underlying split, the default objection, and uncertain satisfaction of the constitutional-claim component. The numerical adjustment is judgmental, not a fitted likelihood ratio.
