## In brief

A man ordered to pay roughly half a billion dollars in restitution and forfeiture asks the Court to revive his challenge to those penalties.

## What happened

Fred Davis Clark, Jr. was convicted of bank fraud and related crimes, sentenced to 40 years in prison, and ordered to pay about $179 million in restitution and $309 million in forfeiture. His prison sentence was later commuted to time served, but the financial penalties stood. Clark dropped his direct appeal and instead filed a motion in federal district court challenging the restitution and forfeiture orders. The district court denied relief, following circuit precedent that such noncustodial penalties cannot be challenged this way. The court of appeals denied Clark permission to appeal further and denied reconsideration.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things. First, whether a disagreement among federal appeals courts over a legal question, by itself, can show that reasonable judges could debate a case's outcome, even when the court handling the case has its own contrary precedent, for purposes of allowing an appeal to proceed. Second, whether the law allowing prisoners to challenge their sentences can be used to challenge purely financial penalties, like restitution or forfeiture, when those penalties are severe enough that they restrict a person's freedom in practical ways.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for the October 9, 2026 conference, after full briefing including the government's opposition and Clark's reply. The docket does not yet show a decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Clark, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on these questions and decide them; granting review decides nothing yet about who wins. If the Court denies review, the lower courts' rulings stand, leaving Clark unable to pursue his challenge to the restitution and forfeiture orders, and the government's position in opposition prevails by default. Clark's reply brief also asks the Court to pair his case with a related one, Berry v. United States, or to hold his case while deciding Berry first.
