## In brief

A man ordered to pay hundreds of millions in restitution and forfeiture asks the Court to revive his bid to challenge those penalties after his prison sentence was commuted.

## What happened

Fred Davis Clark, Jr. was convicted of bank fraud and related crimes and ordered to serve 40 years in prison, pay about $179 million in restitution, and forfeit about $309 million. His prison sentence was later commuted, but the financial penalties were left in place. Clark dismissed his direct appeal without challenging those penalties, then filed a motion asking the district court to vacate them. The district court denied the motion, ruling that this type of claim cannot be raised this way under circuit precedent, and also denied him permission to appeal. The court of appeals agreed and declined to reconsider.

## What the Court is being asked

Clark raises two questions. First, whether an appeals court must let a prisoner appeal when other circuits disagree with the rule that bound his case, even if his own circuit's precedent is settled. Second, whether a court can consider a challenge to financial penalties like restitution and forfeiture, through the kind of petition normally used to challenge imprisonment, when those penalties are large enough to restrict someone's freedom even without jail time.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including the government's opposition and Clark's reply. It was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and again for the conference on October 9, 2026, with no action yet recorded on the docket.

## What each outcome would mean

Clark, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case and reverse the lower courts. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on one or both questions and decide them, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the lower courts' rulings stand: Clark would not be allowed to appeal, and his financial penalties would remain in place without further review under this claim.
