## In brief

Federal officials ask the Court to review a ruling that let a former inmate's damages lawsuit over prison medical care proceed against a prison doctor.

## What happened

Jordan Watkins, held at a federal facility, alleged that after hernia surgery he suffered severe pain and swelling that prison medical staff, including Dr. Brij Mohan, dismissed and left inadequately treated. Watkins sued Mohan for violating his constitutional rights and separately sued the United States for negligence. A federal district court dismissed his claims, reasoning his constitutional claim required extending an existing damages remedy to a new situation. The Seventh Circuit reversed, holding Watkins's claim fit within a damages remedy the Supreme Court recognized in 1980 for prisoners' medical-care claims. One judge dissented in part. Rehearing was denied, and Mohan and the federal government now ask the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether prisoners may still sue individual federal officials for money damages over inadequate medical care, or whether such suits are now barred because they differ enough from the 1980 case that first allowed them — due to differences like the type of injury, the existence of an internal grievance process, or the inmate's status as a pretrial detainee versus convicted prisoner.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for two conferences, most recently one dated after the extension the Court granted for the reply brief; the docket does not show any grant, denial, or other action taken on the petition since that most recent conference.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government and Dr. Mohan are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether Watkins's claim can proceed, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Seventh Circuit's decision allowing Watkins's lawsuit against Mohan to continue would stand, and the case would return to the lower courts for further proceedings. Denial would not mean the Supreme Court agreed the Seventh Circuit was right, only that its ruling remains in effect.
