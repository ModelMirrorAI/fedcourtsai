## In brief

A federal prisoner asks the Court to decide whether credits he earned for completing prison programs can shorten his supervised release.

## What happened

Raul Rivera-Perez earned more than 700 days of credit under the First Step Act for completing recidivism-reduction programming in prison. The Bureau of Prisons applied some credits to move him into prerelease custody early but treated the rest as unusable. He filed a habeas petition arguing the remaining credits should shorten his term of supervised release, the period of monitored freedom that follows imprisonment. The district court agreed. A divided Second Circuit panel reversed, holding the credits cannot reduce supervised release, only accelerate its start, and ordered the case dismissed as moot. The Second Circuit's ruling agreed with the Sixth Circuit but conflicted with the Ninth Circuit, which allows credits to shorten supervised release.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law letting prisoners earn credits for completing rehabilitation programs allows those credits to shorten the time a person must spend on supervised release afterward, rather than only moving up when that supervised period begins.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the November 6, 2026 conference. Before that conference, the Court asked for a response from the government, which had originally waived its right to respond; that response is now due December 14, 2026 after an extension, and an outside group has filed a brief supporting the petition. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Rivera-Perez, who lost in the court of appeals, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the legal question itself, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's decision stands, meaning Rivera-Perez and others in that circuit and the Sixth Circuit could not use leftover credits to shorten supervised release, while prisoners in the Ninth Circuit still could under that circuit's contrary rule.
