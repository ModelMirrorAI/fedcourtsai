## In brief

A federal prisoner asks the Court to decide whether credits he earned for completing prison programs can shorten his supervised release.

## What happened

Raul Rivera-Perez earned more than 700 days of credit under the First Step Act for completing recidivism-reduction programming while imprisoned for a drug conspiracy conviction. The Bureau of Prisons applied some credits to move him into prerelease custody but left others unused. He filed a habeas petition arguing the remaining credits should shorten his term of supervised release. A federal district court agreed and ordered the credits applied. A divided Second Circuit panel reversed, holding the law allows credits only to accelerate the start of prerelease custody or supervised release, not to shorten either, and sent the case back with instructions to dismiss it as moot. Rivera-Perez now asks the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The case turns on how to read a provision of the First Step Act describing how earned time credits are applied toward prerelease custody or supervised release. The Court is asked whether those credits can be used to shorten the length of time a prisoner spends on supervised release after leaving prison, or whether they can only move up the date that supervised release begins.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, after the government waived its right to respond and the Court later asked for a response anyway. The deadline for that response has since been extended to December 14, 2026, so the petition remains pending and has not yet been acted on.

## What each outcome would mean

Rivera-Perez, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Second Circuit's decision. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the question itself, resolving a disagreement among federal appeals courts over how credits may be used; a grant alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, meaning Rivera-Perez's unused credits could not be applied to shorten his supervised release term under that circuit's approach.
