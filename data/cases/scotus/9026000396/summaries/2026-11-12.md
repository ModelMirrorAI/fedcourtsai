## In brief

A federal prisoner asks the Court to decide whether credits he earned under a prison-reform law can shorten his supervised release term.

## What happened

Raul Rivera-Perez earned more than 700 days of time credit under the First Step Act for completing prison programming, but the Bureau of Prisons applied only 365 days toward moving up his release to prerelease custody, leaving the rest unused. He filed a habeas petition arguing the remaining credits should reduce his term of supervised release that follows imprisonment. A district court agreed and ordered the credits applied. A divided Second Circuit panel reversed, holding the law allows credits only to start supervised release earlier, not to shorten it, and sent the case back with instructions to dismiss it as moot. Rivera-Perez now asks the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide what a provision of the First Step Act means when it says earned time credits "shall be applied toward" time in supervised release: whether that language lets a prisoner use leftover credits to shorten the actual length of supervised release, or only to move up the date supervised release begins.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, after which the Court asked for a response, due November 12, 2026; that deadline has since been extended to December 14, 2026. The docket shows no action yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Rivera-Perez, the petitioner, asks the Court to take up the case and reverse the Second Circuit, whose ruling against him currently stands. If the Court grants review, it will decide the question itself after full briefing and argument, deciding nothing about who is right at this stage. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's decision stands, meaning Rivera-Perez and similarly situated prisoners in that circuit cannot use leftover time credits to shorten supervised release terms, while prisoners in the Ninth Circuit, which allows this under its own ruling, could.
