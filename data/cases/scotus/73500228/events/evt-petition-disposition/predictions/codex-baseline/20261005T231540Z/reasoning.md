## Why 32%, above the anchor but below even odds

The upward case is substantial. The petition identifies two recurring constraints on single-incident municipal failure-to-train liability, rather than merely asking whether these particular officers behaved badly. It alleges a conflict with the Second, Sixth, and Eighth Circuits on the clearly-established-right issue and a further disagreement about complete versus deficient training (petition pp. 25-30). A divided panel and a 10-7 denial of rehearing en banc sharpen the institutional disagreement (petition pp. 13-18). Three institutional amicus filings and survival of the initial conference strengthen the signal that this is more than an ordinary unsuccessful civil-rights pleading appeal. I do not count the relist twice: it is already reflected in the elevated anchor.

The doctrinal concern is real but not an automatic merits answer. The parties both rely on Connick v. Thompson, 563 U.S. 51, 63-68 (2011). A targeted CourtListener lookup of that opinion confirmed its narrow treatment of single-incident liability and its rejection of the proposition that helpful additional training alone establishes municipal liability. That supports taking the City's argument seriously; it does not establish that the Court must accept the City's proposed categorical rules in this different police-training setting.

The strongest downward evidence is the BIO's vehicle objection, not simply its denial that a split exists. Respondent says the judges allowing the municipal claim understood the constitutional violation to be obvious, notwithstanding the separate qualified-immunity holding, and understood the complaint to allege no First Amendment training at all (BIO pp. 20-24, 29-31). If that reading is right, neither proposed question cleanly changes the result. The petition itself describes different two-judge coalitions on immunity and municipal liability, making a broad characterization of the panel's rule uncertain. The parties' disagreement about general training versus site-specific training risks turning the second question into complaint interpretation. The case is also at the pleading stage, leaving factual development rather than a final liability determination ahead.

The companion petition compounds that uncertainty. Respondent asks the Court to address qualified immunity and obvious violations there, not expand this City's petition into the whole dispute (BIO pp. 14-15, 32-33). Attention to the litigation may therefore support a different petition or a different issue. I do not translate that attention one-for-one into a grant here. Balancing these factors yields 0.32, an approximately fifteen-percentage-point upward judgmental adjustment from the reached-band anchor, not a fitted statistical estimate or a prediction of a merits win.
