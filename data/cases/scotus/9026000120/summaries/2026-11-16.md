## In brief

The Court declined to hear an advocacy group's challenge to Colorado legislators' immunity from a lawsuit over speech rules at legislative hearings.

## What happened

Gays Against Groomers and other petitioners sued several Colorado state legislators after committee chairs limited their testimony at hearings on a bill about legal name changes for transgender people, citing rules against "misgendering" and "deadnaming" witnesses. Petitioners said this violated their First Amendment rights. A federal district court dismissed the suit, finding the legislators protected by legislative immunity, a doctrine shielding lawmakers from suits over their legislative acts. The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, with one judge concurring only in part. Petitioners then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether legislators sued in their official roles, rather than personally, can still claim legislative immunity when someone seeks a court order rather than money damages. It also asks whether immunity covers not just adopting hearing rules but also enforcing them against specific speakers.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, once in November for a meeting that was rescheduled, and again for a later November conference. The Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Gays Against Groomers and the other petitioners asked the Court to take up the case and overturn the Tenth Circuit's ruling. The denial leaves that ruling in place, meaning the Colorado legislators named in the suit, including Lorena Garcia and others, keep the legislative immunity the lower courts recognized, and the petitioners' lawsuit over the hearing rules remains dismissed. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Tenth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that court's decision as the final word in this case.
