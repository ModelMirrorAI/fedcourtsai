## In brief

Gun sellers ask the Court to hold that magazines holding more than ten rounds are constitutionally protected arms that Washington cannot ban.

## What happened

Gator's Custom Guns and its owner, Walter Wentz, kept selling large-capacity magazines after Washington banned their manufacture and sale in 2022. The state sued them for violating its consumer protection law, and they argued the ban violated the Second Amendment. A trial court agreed and struck down the law, but the Washington Supreme Court reversed, ruling that such magazines are not "arms" protected by the Second Amendment at all, and that even if they were, they are not commonly used for self-defense. Gator's then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether magazines that hold more than ten rounds count as "arms" that are presumptively protected under the part of the Constitution covering the right to keep and bear weapons, or whether they can be treated as mere accessories outside that protection entirely.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for many conferences since September 2025, most recently for June 2026, and the docket shows no decision yet. The parties have filed several supplemental briefs pointing to new appeals court rulings on the same issue, the most recent filed in August 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Gator's and Wentz are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Washington Supreme Court. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether such magazines are constitutionally protected, which could lead to reversing, affirming, or vacating the state court's decision; a reversal would favor Gator's, while an affirmance would leave Washington's ban in place. If the Court denies review, the Washington Supreme Court's ruling upholding the ban stands, and Gator's loses without the Court deciding the constitutional question itself.
