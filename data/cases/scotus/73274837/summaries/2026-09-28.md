## In brief

A gun store and its owner ask the Court to decide whether high-capacity ammunition magazines count as "arms" protected by the Second Amendment.

## What happened

Washington State banned the manufacture, distribution, and sale of magazines holding more than ten rounds. Gator's Custom Guns and its owner, Walter Wentz, kept selling such magazines anyway. When the state sued to enforce the law, Gator's argued the ban was unconstitutional. A trial court agreed, but the Washington Supreme Court reversed, ruling that these magazines are not "arms" under the Second Amendment and that Washington's restriction does not interfere with anyone's right to armed self-defense. Gator's then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether magazines that hold more than ten rounds are the kind of weapon the Second Amendment presumptively protects, or whether states may treat them as accessories outside that protection. Washington argues that even if the Court took the case, the one-sided evidentiary record would still doom Gator's claim.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for many conferences since September 2025, with the parties filing supplemental briefs about related cases along the way, but the docket does not record any grant, denial, or other action on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Gator's Custom Guns and Wentz are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether these magazines receive Second Amendment protection, deciding nothing until it rules. If the Court denies review, the Washington Supreme Court's decision upholding the law stands, leaving Washington's ban in effect and Gator's facing the state's enforcement action on remand.
