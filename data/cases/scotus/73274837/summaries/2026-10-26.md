## In brief

A gun shop asks the Court to revive its claim that Washington's ban on large-capacity ammunition magazines violates the right to bear arms.

## What happened

Washington bans the manufacture, sale, and distribution of ammunition magazines holding more than ten rounds. Gator's Custom Guns and its owner, Walter Wentz, sold thousands of these magazines after the law took effect. When the state sued to enforce the ban, Gator's argued the law violated the Second Amendment. A trial court agreed and struck the law down, but the Washington Supreme Court reversed, ruling that such magazines are not themselves "arms" protected by the Second Amendment and, even if they were, are not commonly used for self-defense. Two justices dissented. Gator's then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether magazines that hold more than ten rounds count as "arms" entitled to constitutional protection in the first place, a threshold question that determines whether courts must go on to ask if a gun restriction fits the country's historical tradition of firearms regulation.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for many conferences since September 2025 without any recorded grant or denial. Both sides have filed supplemental briefs updating the Court on other courts' rulings on similar magazine bans, most recently in August 2026, and the docket shows no action on the petition since.

## What each outcome would mean

Gator's, the gun shop, is asking the Court to take up and reverse the Washington Supreme Court's ruling, which the state defends. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether these magazines are constitutionally protected arms, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Washington Supreme Court's decision upholding the ban stands, and Gator's loses this challenge, leaving the state free to pursue its enforcement action over Gator's past sales.
