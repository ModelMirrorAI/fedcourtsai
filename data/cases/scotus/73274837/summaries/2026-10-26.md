## In brief

A gun store asks the Court to decide if large-capacity ammunition magazines count as "arms" protected by the Second Amendment, after Washington's top court upheld a state sales ban.

## What happened

Washington banned the manufacture and sale of magazines holding more than ten rounds. Gator's Custom Guns kept selling them anyway, and the state sued. Gator's argued the ban violated the Second Amendment. A trial court agreed, but the Washington Supreme Court reversed, ruling that such magazines are not "arms" protected by the Constitution and, separately, that Gator's had not shown they are commonly used for self-defense. Gator's asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that ruling. Washington opposes review, arguing every appeals court to consider similar bans has upheld them and that Gator's presented no supporting evidence below.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether magazines that hold more than ten rounds are themselves "arms" covered by the right to keep and bear arms, such that a government must justify restricting them under the historical-tradition test the Court has set for gun laws, or whether they are merely unprotected accessories that a state may freely regulate.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for dozens of conferences since September 2025 without the docket recording a grant, denial, or other disposition as of the snapshot date. The parties have also filed supplemental briefs flagging related developments in other magazine-ban cases.

## What each outcome would mean

Gator's Custom Guns, the gun store, is asking the Court to take up and reverse the Washington Supreme Court's ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full briefing and argument on whether these magazines are constitutionally protected arms, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Washington Supreme Court's decision upholding the ban stands, and Washington's restriction on selling these magazines remains in effect. A grant would not by itself change the outcome below; only a later merits decision would do that.
