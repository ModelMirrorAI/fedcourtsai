## 2. Disposition of the Application (`interim-disposition`: `0.85`, `predicted_disposition: "granted"`)

The Court will **grant** the Solicitor General's application for an unqualified stay of the October 2, 2026 preliminary injunction (and October 14, 2026 memorandum opinion) of the U.S. District Court for the Western District of Texas, pending the filing and disposition of a petition for a writ of certiorari. The Court will decline the Solicitor General's alternative invitation to construe the application as a petition for certiorari before judgment and summarily reverse on the emergency papers, following its consistent OT2025–OT2026 practice of granting stays pending certiorari rather than summary merits dispositions at the interim stage.

If the Court accompanies the stay order with a brief *per curiam* explanation or if separate writings address the merits, the reasoning will center on three points:

1. **Exclusive Supreme Court Review and Jurisdiction-Stripping under IIRIRA § 102(c)(2) and *Dalton v. Specter*:**
   Congress in IIRIRA § 102(c)(2)(A) barred district courts from entertaining any non-constitutional cause of action or claim arising from the Secretary of Homeland Security's exercise of waiver authority under § 102(c)(1), and in § 102(c)(2)(C) channeled all appellate review of interlocutory or final orders directly to the Supreme Court on writ of certiorari. Under *Dalton v. Specter*, 511 U.S. 462, 471–74 (1994), respondents' contention that the Secretary exceeded the statutory bounds of IIRIRA § 102(a) and § 102(c)(1) is a statutory *ultra vires* claim—not a constitutional claim under the Presentment Clauses or the Take Care Clause. A plaintiff cannot evade § 102(c)(2)(A)'s jurisdiction-stripping bar by recasting an alleged statutory violation as an unconstitutional executive repeal of statutes.

2. **Statutory Authority under IIRIRA § 102(a) and § 102(c)(1) and Inapplicability of the Major Questions Doctrine:**
   Even assuming a court may verify as a threshold predicate that the challenged waivers concern "barriers and roads under this section," the government is likely to succeed in showing that the Big Bend Sector projects fall squarely within § 102(a)'s mandate to install "additional physical barriers and roads ... in the vicinity of the United States border to deter illegal crossings in areas of high illegal entry." Every court of appeals to interpret § 102(a) has held that "additional" bears its ordinary meaning and is not restricted to the specific fencing segments enumerated in § 102(b) or to narrow urban "gap-filling." Nor does the Major Questions Doctrine justify narrowing § 102(a): Congress in § 102(c)(1) explicitly granted the Secretary "sole discretion" to waive "all legal requirements" "[n]otwithstanding any other provision of law," and in the July 2025 One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Pub. L. No. 119-21, § 90001(1)(A), 139 Stat. 357) appropriated $46.5 billion specifically for border-barrier construction.

3. **Remedial Overbreadth and Equities:**
   Under *Trump v. CASA, Inc.*, 606 U.S. 831 (2025), the district court's sector-wide injunction barring reliance on the four waivers across 517 miles of the international border—including road maintenance and pre-construction activities far removed from the specific locations in Ruidosa, Mr. Miller's ranch, and park sites visited by respondents' members—is overbroad. The balance of equities and irreparable harm weigh decisively in favor of staying an injunction that halts congressionally funded border-security infrastructure.
