## In brief

A Catholic organization asks the Supreme Court to rule that denying it permission to build a religious shrine on its property violated a federal religious land-use law.

## What happened

Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist owns a Catholic church in Park Hills, Kentucky, built before the city's zoning code existed. It leased, then acquired, adjacent land to build a grotto honoring the Virgin Mary. A local zoning board granted the permits needed, but neighbors Joel and Elizabeth Frederic sued, arguing the board lacked authority under state law because the property was not on a street zoned for churches. Kentucky's courts agreed the permits were unlawful under state law. The Missionaries argued that barring the grotto violated a federal law protecting religious land use. The Kentucky Supreme Court held the law was not violated, reasoning the group could build a smaller shrine elsewhere and had known the zoning rule barred its plan.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things: first, whether blocking a religious group from building a religious structure on its own property counts as a serious enough interference with religious practice to trigger the federal law's protections, given that the group could have built something smaller in a different spot and knew the zoning rule when it applied; second, whether the zoning rule improperly treats houses of worship worse than comparable nonreligious uses of land, since some nonreligious buildings face the same street-location rule while others do not.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review limited to the first question, and the case has been argued and now awaits decision. Briefing and amicus filings are complete, and the case was argued on January 20, 2027, according to the docket's scheduling entry, though the record here extends only through late November 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Missionaries, as petitioner, are asking the Court to overturn the Kentucky Supreme Court's ruling that the zoning restriction did not substantially burden their religious exercise. If the Court reverses or vacates, the case would return to lower courts for further proceedings applying a different legal standard, without the Court itself deciding who ultimately wins. If the Court affirms, the Kentucky Supreme Court's decision stands, leaving in place the rule that the Frederics' challenge to the permits succeeds and the grotto cannot be built as proposed.
