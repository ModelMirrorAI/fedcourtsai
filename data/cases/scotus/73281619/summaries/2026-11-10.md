## In brief

A Catholic organization asks the Supreme Court to decide whether denying it a permit to build a shrine on land next to its church violated a federal religious-liberty law.

## What happened

Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist wanted to build a grotto honoring the Virgin Mary on land next to its church in Park Hills, Kentucky. A local zoning board granted the permit and variances needed, but neighbors Joel and Elizabeth Frederic sued, arguing the board lacked authority because the site was not next to an "arterial street" as the zoning code required. Kentucky's courts agreed the board exceeded its authority. The Kentucky Supreme Court also rejected the organization's argument that denying the permit violated a federal law protecting religious land use, holding there was no substantial burden on religious exercise and no unequal treatment of religious institutions.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two questions about a federal law protecting religious institutions from burdensome or unequal zoning rules. First, whether completely blocking a religious group from building a religious structure on its chosen property counts as a serious enough burden on religious exercise to trigger the law's protections, even if the group could build something smaller elsewhere. Second, whether a zoning rule that applies an arterial-street requirement to religious buildings, while exempting some other uses like parks and golf courses, treats religious institutions unequally compared to those other uses.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference twice, and the Court asked for and received a response from the Fredericks after initially receiving a waiver of their right to respond. After full briefing, including many outside groups weighing in, the Court granted the petition but limited review to the first question about substantial burden. The case has been scheduled for argument in January 2027.

## What each outcome would mean

Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist, the petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the Kentucky Supreme Court's ruling against it. If the Court affirms that ruling, the finding that denying the permit did not substantially burden the organization's religious exercise would stand, and the Fredericks' legal victory blocking the grotto would remain in place. If the Court reverses, it would mean the denial did impose a substantial burden, which could require further proceedings on whether the zoning restriction can still be justified. Vacating would send the case back without deciding the substantial-burden question either way.
