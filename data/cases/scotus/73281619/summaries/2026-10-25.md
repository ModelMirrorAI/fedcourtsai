## In brief

A Catholic organization asked the Court to revive its claim that a zoning denial for a religious shrine violated federal religious land-use law.

## What happened

Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist wanted to build a grotto honoring the Virgin Mary on land next to its church in Park Hills, Kentucky. The local zoning board granted a permit and variances, but neighbors Joel and Elizabeth Frederic sued, arguing the board lacked authority because the property was not on an "arterial street" as the zoning code required. Kentucky's courts agreed the board exceeded its authority. The Missionaries argued that denying the permit violated a federal law protecting religious land use, but the Kentucky Supreme Court rejected that argument, holding the denial did not substantially burden the group's religious exercise and did not treat it unequally compared to nonreligious uses.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a rule blocking a religious group from building a religious structure on its own land counts as a serious burden on its religious exercise under federal law, when the burden can be avoided by not asking at all or when a smaller alternative elsewhere might be feasible. A second question, about whether the zoning rule treated religious and nonreligious uses unequally, was not taken up.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition limited to the first question presented, concerning the substantial-burden provision. The case has not yet been briefed or argued on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

The Missionaries, as petitioner, are asking the Court to overturn the Kentucky Supreme Court's ruling in favor of the Fredericks. If the Court affirms, the zoning denial stands and the Missionaries cannot build the grotto as planned. If the Court reverses, it would hold that the denial did impose a substantial burden, which would send the case back for further proceedings on whether the zoning rule can survive the stricter legal test that follows. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Kentucky ruling and return the case for another look without deciding the issue for either side.
