## In brief

A Catholic organization asked the Court to decide whether a zoning denial that blocked construction of a religious shrine violates a federal religious-land-use law.

## What happened

Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist wanted to build a grotto honoring the Virgin Mary next to its church in Park Hills, Kentucky, on adjoining land it later acquired. A local board granted the needed permit and variances, but neighbors Joel and Elizabeth Frederic sued, arguing the board lacked authority under state zoning law. Kentucky's courts agreed the board exceeded its authority, and the state supreme court also held that denying the permit did not violate the federal Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act, reasoning the group could build a smaller grotto elsewhere and had known the zoning rule barred its request before applying.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether denying a permit to build a religious structure in a chosen location counts as placing a serious obstacle on religious practice under the federal law, even when a smaller version might be built elsewhere or the religious group knew of the restriction beforehand. The Court is also asked whether a zoning rule that applies different requirements to houses of worship than to some other kinds of buildings treats religious groups unequally under that same law.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition on October 1, 2026, agreeing to hear the case, but limited review to the first question about what counts as a serious obstacle to religious exercise; it did not take up the second question about equal treatment. The case has not yet been briefed or argued on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up the case after losing in the Kentucky Supreme Court. Because the Court granted review only on the first question, the case will now proceed to full briefing and argument on that issue. If the Court ultimately rules for Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist, it would mean the zoning denial counted as a serious burden on its religious exercise, favoring its position in the dispute with the Fredericks. If the Court rules for the Fredericks, the Kentucky Supreme Court's conclusion that no such burden existed would stand. The Court could also send the case back for further proceedings without deciding the question for either side.
