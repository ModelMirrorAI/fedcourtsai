## Why 30%, rather than the 17.2% anchor

The upward adjustment reflects more than religious subject matter. The Court called for a response despite a waiver; the unusually broad amicus participation includes a state coalition; and the questions concern recurring interpretations of two federal statutory protections rather than simply whether this applicant should receive a zoning variance. Petition pp. 10–28 argues that alternatives and advance knowledge of zoning restrictions should not defeat a substantial-burden claim when the particular religious exercise is prohibited. Petition pp. 28–36 identifies disagreements over equal-terms comparators, regulatory purposes versus objective zoning criteria, and allocation of burdens. Those are plausible issues for plenary clarification.

The substantial-burden question has a concrete connection to the facts: the institution wants a Marian grotto on an adjacent parcel; the parties disagree about the significance of a smaller shrine elsewhere and knowledge of the arterial-street restriction. The equal-terms argument points to named secular categories exempt from that restriction. These are more specific grounds for attention than a bare assertion of religious discrimination. Their potential reach supports the stakes score of 0.62, independently of grant likelihood.

Important counterweights keep the probability well below one-half:

- **Outcome-determinative conflict is contested.** The opposition, especially pp. 11–23, argues that the supposedly competing substantial-burden tests converge on feasible alternatives and self-imposed burdens. On equal terms, pp. 29–35 argues that most circuits require similarly situated comparators and that the distinctions would not change this result. I do not adopt either party's circuit count as an independently established current fact.
- **Invited-error risk is concrete.** BIO pp. 27–28 says petitioner affirmatively requested Livingston's framework in Kentucky, then attacked its factors after losing. A reviewing court could distinguish acceptance of a framework from misapplication of it, but the record described is less clean than the petition's categorical question suggests.
- **Preservation and evidentiary development matter.** BIO pp. 35–36 says equal terms was first raised, incompletely, in the Kentucky Supreme Court, without a factual comparator record. The petition counters that the ordinance itself presents a legal question and that the relevant issue was decided. I treat this as a substantial vehicle dispute, not a proved waiver.
- **An additional statutory-coverage objection complicates QP1.** BIO pp. 27–28 contends that the board lacked authority to make an individualized assessment and therefore invokes both RLUIPA's coverage condition and adequate-and-independent-state-ground terminology. I do not treat that characterization as an established jurisdictional bar: whether federal law overrides a state restriction remains a federal-law question. Nevertheless, it offers a possible antecedent dispute before the Court could reach the broad substantial-burden issue.
- **The second distribution is partly procedural.** A response request and briefing can explain the new conference setting. That deserves less weight than a sequence of unexplained post-briefing relists.

As a narrow precedent check, CourtListener located Livingston Christian Schools v. Genoa Charter Township, 858 F.3d 996 (6th Cir. 2017), and supplied passages discussing Holt at approximately 1002. Livingston expressly considered Holt while distinguishing the land-use inquiry from the prison setting. Thus the petition's Holt argument is not simply that the governing appellate framework overlooked the Supreme Court authority. This supports taking the doctrinal dispute seriously without treating summary correction as inevitable. I did not use the prior as a matched statistical sample.

The net adjustment from roughly 17% to 30% is judgmental, not an estimated regression coefficient. Strong attention and nationally recurring statutory issues justify a substantial increase; the specific vehicle objections and uncertain strength of the split prevent a grant-favored forecast. Missing the reply leaves uncertainty about petitioner's strongest answers to those objections.
