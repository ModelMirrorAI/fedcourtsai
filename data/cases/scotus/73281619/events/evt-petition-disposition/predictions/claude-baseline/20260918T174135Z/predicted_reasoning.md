# Predicted reasoning — Missionaries of Saint John the Baptist, Inc. v. Frederic (No. 25-1131)

Cert-stage forecast at the `distribution` moment. The docket shows **two
distributions**: the first (May 12) for the May 28 conference produced a call
for a response on May 14 rather than a decision; the second (September 2) sets
the petition, now fully briefed, for the September 28 long conference. So the
Court has not yet actually voted on this petition once, and the state I forecast
from is "first substantive consideration, after a response the Court itself
asked for."

## Disposition

**The Court will most likely deny the petition, but a grant is a serious
possibility.** P(grant) = 0.30. If it denies, the denial comes on the October 5
order list or, more likely, after one or two relists.

What makes the grant case: the Court has never construed either land-use
provision; the substantial-burden and equal-terms splits are real and have been
acknowledged by circuit judges for years (Thapar's Tree of Life dissent); the
Third Circuit's published, divided Anash decision of July 30, 2026 sharpens the
substantial-burden conflict after the petition was filed; the Court called for
a response after the neighbors waived; and the cert-stage support (16 amicus
briefs, a 20-state coalition led by Kentucky's own Attorney General, a
cross-denominational set of religious groups) is the profile of a petition the
Court takes seriously.

What makes the deny case, and why I put it above even odds: the vehicle is
messy in ways the BIO exploits well. Petitioner asked the Kentucky Supreme
Court to adopt the very Livingston factors it now attacks (invited error); the
substantial-burden holding is partly a fact-bound "failure of proof" on
alternatives; the equal-terms argument was raised for the first time in the
state supreme court with no record on similarly-situated comparators; the
adverse parties are private neighbors rather than the zoning government; and
the BIO's argument that RLUIPA's "individualized assessments" hook does not
apply because the Board lacked authority under state law gives the Court a
threshold excuse. The Court also passed on four cleaner RLUIPA land-use
petitions in the last seven years (Tree of Life, Canaan Christian, New Harvest,
Spirit of Aloha) and on the RFRA substantial-burden question in Apache
Stronghold.

## Relists

**Roughly even odds of at least one further distribution** (P = 0.50). Two
paths produce one: a grant path in which the Court relists once before
granting (the modern pattern for most grants not announced straight off the
long conference), and a deny path in which a Justice holds the petition to
write a dissent from denial. Two paths produce none: a clean grant announced in
the post-long-conference grant list (around October 2), or a quiet denial on
October 5. If relisted, expect one or two relists, not a long string.

## CVSG

**Unlikely** (P = 0.10). No federal party is involved and the United States did
not file at the cert stage. A CVSG is not implausible because the Department of
Justice enforces RLUIPA's land-use provisions and the questions are pure
statutory construction, but the Court has not called for the SG's views in the
recent RLUIPA land-use denials, and having already called for a response it is
more likely to act than to seek further input.

## Which question presented, if granted

If it grants, the Court would most likely take **both questions as written**,
since the petition frames them as a pair of never-construed provisions and the
20-state amicus brief supports both. If it narrows, it would keep **QP 1**
(substantial burden), which was squarely litigated at three levels and where
Anash supplies a fresh published split, and leave QP 2 (equal terms) behind
because of the preservation and record problems. A reformulation adding the
BIO's threshold question (whether §2000cc(a)(2)(C) applies where the board
lacked state-law authority) is possible but unlikely.

## Summary disposition route

**Very unlikely** (P = 0.05 conditional on a grant). No intervening decision
bears on these questions. Grand v. City of University Heights (No. 25-965,
granted June 30, 2026) is a ripeness case about pre-enforcement challenges to
land-use enforcement threats and will not construe substantial burden or equal
terms, so a hold-and-GVR in its light is a remote scenario. A summary reversal
on a contested split is not the Court's practice. A grant here means plenary
review, argued in the winter or spring sittings of OT2026.

## Dissent from denial

**A real possibility if the Court denies** (P = 0.30). The likeliest author is
Justice Gorsuch, who dissented from the denial in Apache Stronghold on the
substantial-burden question and has repeatedly urged the Court to police lower
courts' narrowing of RLUIPA and RFRA, plausibly joined by Justice Thomas; a
statement respecting denial from Justice Kavanaugh or Justice Alito flagging
the split for a better vehicle is the other shape. Against that, the last four
RLUIPA land-use denials drew no separate writing at all, and the vehicle flaws
here give a Justice inclined to write a reason to wait instead.

## If granted (conditional only)

The likely ground would be that RLUIPA's substantial-burden inquiry, as in
Holt v. Hobbs, asks whether the government burdened the claimant's chosen
religious exercise, not whether an alternative exercise is available, with a
reversal or vacatur and remand for strict scrutiny; on equal terms, the Court
would likely hold that an ordinance drawing an express church-specific line
requires the government to justify its comparators. Merits votes are not
forecast at this stage.
