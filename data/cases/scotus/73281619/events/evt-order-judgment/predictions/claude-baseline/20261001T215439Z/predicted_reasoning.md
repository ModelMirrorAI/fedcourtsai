# Forecast: what the Court will do in No. 25-1131

**Judgment class: reversed.** The Court will reverse the Supreme Court of Kentucky and remand. The dispositive holding will be on Question 1 alone, as granted: a land-use regulation that prohibits a religious institution from building a religious structure on its own property imposes a "substantial burden" on religious exercise under 42 U.S.C. § 2000cc(a). The Court will hold that the Kentucky court's two grounds for finding no substantial burden, that petitioner could build a smaller shrine on the church lot and that petitioner knew the ordinance barred the grotto when it acquired the adjacent parcel, are not permissible bases for defeating the threshold showing. Because the state court never reached strict scrutiny, the Court will remand for the compelling-interest and least-restrictive-means inquiry rather than decide it. A vacatur-and-remand that announces the standard and leaves its application to Kentucky is the main alternative shape; it is less likely because Question 1 is framed as a yes-or-no question about an outright prohibition, and the Court took it as written.

**Vote lineup: 8 to 1 on the judgment.** Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett vote to disturb the judgment; Justice Jackson dissents. The dissent's ground will be the record, not the statute: that petitioner bore the burden of persuasion under § 2000cc-2(b), offered only conclusory statements that it had no alternative site, and that the judgment should stand on that failure of proof whatever the right legal standard is. A clean 9 to 0 judgment is the next most likely lineup, with the liberal Justices joining a narrow textual opinion as they did in Holt and in the RLUIPA and Title VII accommodation cases that followed it; a 6 to 3 split with Justices Sotomayor and Kagan joining the record-based dissent is the third.

**The ground.** The majority will reason from the statutory definition of "religious exercise," which reaches "any exercise of religion" and expressly includes "the use, building, or conversion of real property" for religious purposes (§ 2000cc-5(7)), and from Holt v. Hobbs, which rejected the "availability of alternative means" inquiry under the materially identical institutionalized-persons provision. It will treat the land-use and prisoner provisions as sharing one substantial-burden standard, hold that courts ask whether the government has burdened the specific exercise the claimant proposes rather than whether some modified exercise remains available, and hold that a complete prohibition on the proposed construction is a substantial burden. It will reject the "self-imposed burden" rationale on the ground that nothing in the text turns on the claimant's notice of the regulation, noting that the prisoner in Holt likewise knew the grooming policy before seeking an exemption. It will not adopt or endorse any circuit's multi-factor test; it will say that factors such as delay, expense, and arbitrariness may bear on other cases but cannot convert an outright prohibition into a non-burden.

**Breadth.** The rule will be categorical as to prohibitions: an outright bar on a religious institution's proposed religious use or construction of its own property is a substantial burden as a matter of law, and the alternative-location and self-imposed-burden factors are excluded as threshold defeaters. The opinion will leave open how partial restrictions (size caps, conditional denials, procedural delay) are measured, and will say nothing about the Equal Terms Provision, which the Court declined to take.

**Separate writings.** Justice Alito writes for the Court. Justice Gorsuch concurs, pressing the view he stated in dissent from denial in Apache Stronghold that preventing a religious exercise is necessarily a substantial burden on it, and perhaps addressing the invited-error point. Justice Sotomayor concurs to emphasize that the claimant still bears the burden of persuasion on sincerity and on the fact of a burden, and that the holding does not displace neutral zoning rules that leave the exercise possible. Authorship and writing roles are context-only forecasts; nothing in the record scores them.

**Procedural exits.** Respondents' two vehicle arguments remain live but will not carry the day. The adequate-and-independent-state-ground argument fails because the question whether § 2000cc(a)(2)(C)'s "individualized assessments" hook is satisfied is itself a question of federal law, and because the Kentucky court decided the RLUIPA defense on its federal merits with no plain statement that state law disposed of it. The invited-error argument (petitioner asked the Kentucky court to adopt Livingston) is the sharper DIG risk; the Court granted over it after calling for a response and reading the reply, so a dismissal as improvidently granted is possible but unlikely, on the order of one in twenty. An equally divided affirmance would need a recusal, and nothing on the record suggests one.

**Timing.** Briefing on the merits runs through the winter; argument in the February or March 2027 sitting; decision by the end of June 2027. The timing is unscored and stated to discipline the rest.
