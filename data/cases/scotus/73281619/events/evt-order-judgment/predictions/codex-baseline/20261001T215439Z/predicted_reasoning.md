# Forecast of the Court's judgment

The Court will **reverse** the Kentucky Supreme Court's judgment and remand for further proceedings on the RLUIPA substantial-burden defense. My probability that the judgment below is disturbed, including vacatur or a mixed judgment, is **0.80**. Reversal is my single most likely judgment class, not a prediction that the Court itself will issue a construction permit.

I predict a **6–3 judgment**: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, and Barrett in the majority; Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson dissenting. This is a forecast of all nine participating, not evidence of their votes. I make no authorship prediction.

## Ground

The majority will decide Question 1, the substantial-burden question on which review was granted, and leave Question 2's equal-terms dispute unresolved. It will hold that RLUIPA protects the sincerely proposed religious use itself, including construction on the claimant's property. A complete prohibition of that use cannot be reduced to an insubstantial inconvenience simply because the claimant could build something smaller elsewhere or knew of the restriction when acquiring the property.

The majority will apply the statutory definition of religious exercise in 42 U.S.C. § 2000cc-5(7)(B) and extend Holt v. Hobbs's protection of the particular exercise at issue to this land-use setting. Advance notice of a restriction will not operate as a categorical forfeiture of RLUIPA protection. The majority will reject respondents' attempt to treat the state-law limit on the board's authority as sufficient, by itself, to insulate the judgment from the federal statutory defense.

## Breadth and remand

The holding will extend to land-use decisions completely prohibiting a sincerely proposed religious use of the claimant's property, not just this grotto's dimensions or these adjacent parcels. It will nevertheless stop short of treating every setback, expense, or permit condition as a substantial burden, or granting religious organizations an automatic exemption from zoning. Establishing a substantial burden triggers the remaining statutory analysis; it does not settle compelling interests, least restrictive means, or all prerequisites to relief.

On remand, the lower courts will address remaining statutory-coverage and justification questions rather than regard the hypothetical smaller shrine and prior notice as sufficient to end the case. The Court will not adjudicate the ungranted equal-terms question to reach that result.

The dissent will emphasize the claimant's burden to prove substantial interference, the distinction between alternative religious practices and another location for the same practice, and the thin record concerning why a smaller grotto would be inadequate. A narrower opinion attracting additional Justices is a meaningful alternative to my specific lineup. A DIG is also a live procedural alternative because respondents argue that petitioner invited the standard it now attacks; an equally divided affirmance is remote because the provisioned record discloses no recusal.
