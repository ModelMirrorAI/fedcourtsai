## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a former TSA employee's bid to revive her disability-accommodation lawsuit against the federal government.

## What happened

Stephanie Redding worked for TSA as a Federal Air Marshal until medical conditions made her unable to meet flight requirements. TSA reassigned her to a lower-paying job at another agency and closed her accommodation case days later. Redding says TSA knew, but never told her, that accepting the reassignment would end her pending disability-retirement application, and that TSA closed her case before required training was finished. A federal district court dismissed her Rehabilitation Act lawsuit, finding she had not shown she could do her prior job's essential functions. The Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding the reassignment was "manifestly reasonable" and that this ended the inquiry.

## What the Court is being asked

Redding asked the Court to decide whether an employer meets its duty to work in good faith with a disabled employee seeking accommodation when it proposes a job move while hiding that accepting it will cost the employee a separate retirement benefit, and when it ends the accommodation process before required training and evaluation are complete. She also asked whether courts may treat an employee's participation as voluntary consent when the employer concealed facts material to that choice.

## Where it stands

The Solicitor General waived the government's right to respond to the petition. The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, and on October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Redding, as petitioner, asked the Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit's decision against her. The denial leaves that decision in place: the lawsuit remains dismissed, and the Fourth Circuit's ruling that TSA's reassignment was reasonable, ending further inquiry into the agency's conduct, stands as the final word in her case. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fourth Circuit; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed without deciding the underlying question.
