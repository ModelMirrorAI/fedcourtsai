## In brief

A former federal air marshal asks the Court to revive her disability-accommodation lawsuit against the Department of Homeland Security after the lower courts dismissed it.

## What happened

Stephanie Redding worked for the Transportation Security Administration until vision and other medical conditions made her unable to continue as a Federal Air Marshal. TSA and Redding pursued a reassignment to a training-center job while she also had a disability-retirement application pending with a separate federal agency. Redding says TSA knew accepting the reassignment would end her retirement application, never told her, and closed the accommodation process before she had even completed job training. A federal district court dismissed her Rehabilitation Act claim, finding she had not shown she could do her prior job even with accommodation. The Fourth Circuit affirmed, reasoning that the completed reassignment was itself a reasonable accommodation and that this ended the inquiry.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises questions about what an employer must do to satisfy its duty to work in good faith with a disabled employee toward an accommodation. Redding asks the Court to decide whether an employer meets that duty when it proposes a job change while hiding that accepting it will cancel a separate retirement benefit, and when it declares the process finished before the new job's required training is done. She also asks whether courts may treat an employee's participation as voluntary consent when the government withheld information relevant to that choice.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed in May 2026, the government waived its right to respond, and the case was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference. The docket does not yet show any action taken at that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Redding, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up her case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the accommodation and consent questions itself, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies review, the Fourth Circuit's decision affirming dismissal of Redding's lawsuit stands, and the case ends in DHS's favor. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with the Fourth Circuit's reasoning, only that the decision remains in place.
