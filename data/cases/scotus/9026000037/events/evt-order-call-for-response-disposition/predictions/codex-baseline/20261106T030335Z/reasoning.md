The question is what materials a court may use to decide whether offenses are the same for double-jeopardy purposes: abstract elements, charging allegations, or the prosecution's theory as manifested at trial.

I independently checked two cited authorities rather than treating the petition's entire split chart as established. United States v. Nash, 115 F.3d 1431, 1437 (9th Cir. 1997), rejects reliance on the way offenses are charged; United States v. Gamboa, 439 F.3d 796, 809 (8th Cir. 2006), calls for examining the specific indictment charges as well as statutory provisions. CourtListener's opinion excerpts confirm those opposing formulations. This supports a genuine methodological disagreement, but does not independently establish that the different statutes and procedural settings produce an outcome-determinative conflict here. The petition itself discusses both older and newer Eleventh Circuit formulations, another reason not to accept its three-way taxonomy without qualification.

The most important downward adjustment is vehicle quality. The bound-in opinion does more than compare trial evidence: appendix pp. 22a–25a discuss statutory structure, the 2003 amendment, the increase from nine to eighteen years, and whether the legislature authorized cumulative punishment for a sexual offense used to elevate kidnapping. It also discusses alternative jury instructions and the State's position about an incorporated sexual offense. Those details leave room to characterize the result as a narrow statutory-intent or necessarily-included-offense determination rather than a clean choice among three universal tests. The decision's reference to both federal and state constitutions, and its refusal to abandon precedent under state stare-decisis rules, add jurisdictional and framing friction. I do not assume either is a conclusive independent-state-ground bar: the petition expressly argues that the doctrines are interwoven and that the relevant challenge was raised below.

Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359, 368–69 (1983), independently checked through CourtListener, reinforces both sides of this assessment: federal double-jeopardy conclusions are reviewable, but the Court accepts a state court's construction of state statutes. Unlike Hunter's explicit cumulative-punishment authorization, this record contains a dispute about inferred legislative intent. That distinction makes summary correction less straightforward and leaves a substantial chance that the Court declines review even if some Justices dislike New Mexico's general methodology.

Taken together, these considerations yield 34%, approximately eleven percentage points above the state-class anchor, but well short of making a grant the most likely outcome. This is not a mechanically fitted adjustment. Lack of adversarial briefing is the largest remaining uncertainty.
