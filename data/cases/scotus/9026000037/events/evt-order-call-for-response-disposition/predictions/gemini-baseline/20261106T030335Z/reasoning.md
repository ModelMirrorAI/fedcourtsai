## 2. Case-Specific Adjustments from the `0.2333` Anchor

### A. State Legislative Intent, N.M. Const. art. II, § 15, and State Stare Decisis (Strong Downward Adjustment)

The Petition asks the Court to decide whether *Blockburger v. United States*, 284 U.S. 299 (1932), compares (a) only abstract statutory elements, (b) the elements as charged in the indictment, or (c) the evidence, closing arguments, and jury instructions at trial under New Mexico's "modified *Blockburger*" test (*Swafford v. State*, 1991-NMSC-043; *State v. Gutierrez*, 2011-NMSC-024; *State v. Begaye*, 2023-NMSC-015).

However, this case involves **cumulative punishments imposed in a single state criminal trial** under **New Mexico statutes**. That posture creates two intertwined obstacles to Supreme Court review:

1. **Under settled federal precedent, cumulative punishment in a single state trial is purely a question of state legislative intent:** In *Whalen v. United States*, 445 U.S. 684, 688, 691–92 (1980), *Missouri v. Hunter*, 459 U.S. 359, 366–68 (1983), and *Ohio v. Johnson*, 467 U.S. 493, 499 (1984)—reaffirmed just months before this petition in *Barrett v. United States*, 607 U.S. ___, slip op. at 8–10 (Jan. 14, 2026) (`cluster_id: 10774608`, `opinion_id: 11241193`)—this Court has held that in the concurrent-prosecution (multiple-punishment) context, the Fifth Amendment Double Jeopardy Clause does no more than prevent the sentencing court from prescribing greater punishment than the legislature intended, and *Blockburger* is merely a rule of statutory construction for discerning legislative intent. Critically, as *Ohio v. Johnson*, 467 U.S. at 499, held, federal courts "accept, as we must, the [state] Supreme Court's determination that the [state] Legislature did not intend cumulative punishment." Throughout its opinion, the New Mexico Supreme Court stated that its *Swafford* / modified-*Blockburger* test is the interpretive framework New Mexico uses to determine **"whether the Legislature intended for the unitary conduct to be punished as separate offenses"** when a New Mexico statute is "vague and unspecific or written in the alternative" (Pet. App. 14a–15a, 45a–46a).
2. **Coextensive invocation of the New Mexico Constitution and state stare decisis:** In Part II.B of its opinion (Pet. App. 38a–40a, ¶¶ 42–44), the New Mexico Supreme Court refused the State's request to abandon its double-description framework on two grounds:
   - First, the court held that the State failed to brief or satisfy New Mexico's four state-law stare decisis factors (*Padilla v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 2003-NMSC-011, ¶ 7), noting that the existing test is entirely workable because the prosecutor could have obtained separate convictions simply by drafting the jury instructions and presenting its closing argument more carefully (Pet. App. 38a–40a, ¶¶ 42–43).
   - Second, in Paragraph 44 (Pet. App. 40a), the court explicitly invoked **both** the federal and state constitutions: *"And we reiterate that the Legislature is free at any time to authorize multiple punishments for a particular crime or series of crimes. Absent such express language, we conclude the current test honors the mandate of the double jeopardy clause contained within both the United States and New Mexico Constitutions—that no person shall 'be twice put in jeopardy' for the same offense. U.S. Const. amend. V; N.M. Const. art. II, § 15."*
   Even if *Michigan v. Long*, 463 U.S. 1032 (1983), permits technical jurisdiction because the New Mexico Supreme Court treated federal and state double jeopardy principles as intertwined without a plain statement of exclusive state-law reliance, the BIO will forcefully argue that a decision by this Court on Fifth Amendment *Blockburger* methodology would be practically advisory: on remand, the New Mexico Supreme Court could immediately reaffirm the exact same result either as a matter of state statutory construction of `N.M. Stat. Ann. § 30-4-1` under *Ohio v. Johnson* or under Article II, Section 15 of the New Mexico Constitution.

### B. Compound Statutory Alternatives and General-Verdict Ambiguity (Moderate Downward Adjustment)

The record also makes *Neal* a poor vehicle for testing an "abstract statutory elements" rule because the vacatur of Count 2 (`CSP II`) turned on an **explicit alternative element in New Mexico's first-degree kidnapping statute** combined with a **general jury verdict**:

- Under `N.M. Stat. Ann. § 30-4-1(B)`, kidnapping is elevated from a second-degree felony to a first-degree felony only when the defendant does not free the victim in a safe place or inflicts **"physical injury or a sexual offense upon the victim."**
- Consistent with the statute, the Count 1 jury instruction required the State to prove that Respondent "inflicted physical injury or a sexual offense on [Victim]" (Pet. App. 17a, 47a).
- Even under this Court's federal *Blockburger* cases—*Whalen v. United States*, 445 U.S. at 694, *Harris v. Oklahoma*, 433 U.S. 682 (1977), *Illinois v. Vitale*, 447 U.S. 410, 420–21 (1980), and *United States v. Dixon*, 509 U.S. 688, 698, 717 (1993) (and compare *Barrett v. United States*, 607 U.S. ___, slip op. at 9 n.10, 14 (2026))—when a compound statute incorporates another offense as an alternative element (here, inflicting "a sexual offense"), a court applying *Blockburger* does not treat all statutory alternatives as a single indivisible element; rather, the predicate offense (`CSP II`, the only sexual offense submitted to the jury) is a lesser-included species of the "sexual offense" alternative of first-degree kidnapping.
- Because the trial court submitted both the "physical injury" and "sexual offense" statutory alternatives to the jury in a single general verdict without a special verdict form, the New Mexico Supreme Court applied its settled general-verdict rule (*State v. Foster*, 1999-NMSC-007, ¶ 28—paralleling *Stromberg v. California*, 283 U.S. 359 (1931), and *Yates v. United States*, 354 U.S. 298 (1957)) presuming that the jury may have relied on the "sexual offense" alternative (Pet. App. 16a–22a). Thus, the case does not cleanly isolate New Mexico's "modified *Blockburger*" reliance on trial evidence/closing argument from the conventional *Whalen*/*Stromberg* treatment of compound statutes with alternative statutory predicates.
