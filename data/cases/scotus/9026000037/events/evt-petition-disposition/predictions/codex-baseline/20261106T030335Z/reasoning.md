## Anchor and adjustment

The authoritative context freezes sal-v7, band `state`, Term 2026, and cert_anchor.rate = 0.23333333333333334. This is the starting point, not the private-petitioner floor. Pooling every strictly prior Term shown in the committed sal-v7 table, 2017–2025, using the JSON's unrounded reached figures, gives 98 grant-family outcomes / 420 resolved petitions = 23.33%. I exclude the 2026 row. The modern whole-cert figures imply 1,275 grants or GVRs / 45,084 resolved petitions, approximately 2.83%, but that unconditioned population is not the right anchor for a state petitioner. Sovereign status is already in the band and receives no second boost.

The requested response and a demonstrable disagreement about the sources used in the same-offense inquiry justify moving above the frozen anchor. The petition's contrast is not wholly rhetorical: United States v. Nash, 115 F.3d 1431, 1437–38 (9th Cir. 1997), emphasizes statutory elements rather than the indictment; United States v. Gamboa, 439 F.3d 796, 809 (8th Cir. 2006), expressly considers the indictment's specific charges. I checked those passages through CourtListener, not subsequent treatment of this petition. The broader claimed circuit and state-court lineup remains petitioner's characterization, not independently established in full. Moreover, differing statutory alternatives and successive-prosecution versus multiple-punishment settings can explain some apparent disagreement. The petition itself places the Eleventh Circuit on different sides when discussing different decisions, a reason not to price a perfectly clean three-way split.

The important downward adjustment is vehicle quality. The opinion below does more than compare trial evidence: it interprets the kidnapping statute's amendment, structure, and punishment levels, finds no sufficiently clear authorization of cumulative punishment, and invokes lenity (App. 22a–26a, paragraphs 24–28). It also records the State's concession that CSP II would be subsumed if the jury relied on the sexual-offense kidnapping theory (App. 18a–20a, paragraph 21). That makes the abstract question less cleanly separable from local statutory meaning, alternative instructions, and the State's litigation choices.

The petition's Michigan v. Long argument is substantial: App. 40a expressly intertwines the federal and state constitutional guarantees rather than clearly announcing a broader independent state constitutional rule. I therefore do not treat federal jurisdiction as certainly absent. But Missouri v. Hunter, 459 U.S. 359, 368–69 (1983), distinguishes reviewable federal double-jeopardy conclusions from binding state statutory construction; I independently checked that distinction through CourtListener. A federal methodological disagreement may not change the result if New Mexico's statutory-intent conclusion survives. These obstacles offset much of the split and response-request uplift. The net increase from 23.33% to 28% is a judgmental calibration, not a fitted numerical model.

## Procedural claims and stakes

The paid-segment terminal cuts in metrics/statpack.md are cross-checks, not forward hazards. Grant-family rates are about 1.8% at zero relists (n=10,204 resolved), 13.5% at one (n=2,532), 41.2% at two (n=495), and 37.1% at three-plus (n=482). A petition awaiting a requested response can still move between those buckets. The no-CVSG rate is 6.4% (n=13,549), versus 34.8% with CVSG (n=164); neither is a probability of receiving a CVSG from here. The zero-amicus bucket's grant-family rate is 3.8% (n=11,109), but that terminal, all-caption-class marginal rate should not replace this state-petitioner anchor or double-count the same quiet-docket signal.

I set the distribution increment at 98% because the requested opposition ordinarily entails returning the petition to conference after its original distribution. The most likely increment is one redistribution, not multiple substantive relists. CVSG is only 2.5%: this is a criminal constitutional question, not a dispute whose resolution plainly requires the federal government's specialized regulatory views. Conditional on a grant, I put 15% on a cert-order disposition and 85% on plenary review. There is no identified intervening decision supporting a routine GVR; a summary correction is conceivable, but the competing doctrinal approaches and state-law complications favor briefing and argument if review is taken.

Conditional on denial, a dissent or statement has 10% probability. The order-writer entries are unconditional per-Justice writing probabilities, not votes and not conditional denial probabilities. Their combined small mass permits the 7.2% joint probability of denial with a writing, plus occasional grant-order writings and multiple authors. Thomas's participation in Dixon's competing elements analysis, as described in the petition, supports a modestly higher writing probability; the remaining individual differences are low-confidence judgment rather than a measured authorship model. No writing is the modal prediction for every Justice.

The stakes score, 0.45, concerns significance if decided, not the 28% grant probability. A nationwide rule about cumulative criminal punishment could matter broadly, but this is a technical methodology case with pronounced local statutory complications, not a demonstrated broad public-policy confrontation.
