## In brief

The Court denied the Trump administration's request to pause a court order blocking a federal prison policy limiting transgender inmates' access to hormone treatment and related accommodations.

## What happened

Federal inmates diagnosed with gender dysphoria, led by Alishea Sophia Kingdom, sued over a 2026 Bureau of Prisons policy ending hormone treatment, social accommodations, and surgeries previously available for gender dysphoria, arguing the policy was adopted without adequate justification. A federal district court agreed that the inmates were likely to succeed on their claim that the policy was arbitrary and capricious, and blocked the government from enforcing it. The United States Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit declined to pause that order while the government's appeal continued. The government then asked the Supreme Court to step in.

## What the Court is being asked

The government asked the Court to pause the lower court's order while the case continues, arguing it is likely to win on the merits because the prison agency reasonably explained its policy change and the lower court exceeded limits on court orders affecting prison conditions. The inmates countered that the agency ignored its own decades of experience providing this care and that its stated reasons do not hold up.

## Where it stands

The Chief Justice requested a response, and after receiving briefing from both sides along with amicus filings, the application was referred to and denied by the full Court. Justices Thomas and Alito noted they would have granted it.

## What each outcome would mean

The government, represented by the Solicitor General on behalf of President Trump and other officials, asked for the pause. Because the request was denied, the lower court's order blocking the 2026 policy remains in effect, meaning the Bureau of Prisons must continue providing the prior treatment approach while the underlying lawsuit and appeal proceed. Had the Court granted the request, the agency could have enforced its new policy immediately while litigation continued. The denial does not decide the underlying legal dispute, which remains before the lower courts.
