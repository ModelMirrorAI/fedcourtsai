The committed `metrics/statpack.md` interim table supplies **31 grants / 301 resolved substantive applications = 10.299%** for the eligible 2016–2025 window: 17/226 in 2025 plus 14/75 in 2024; the other eligible Terms have zero parsed substantive resolutions. Coverage matters: 2024 has 937 unparsed applications out of 1,297, while 2025 has zero unparsed out of 1,467. Earlier eligible Terms contribute no parsed substantive observations. Those gaps do not establish a historical change in Court behavior. Escalation columns include pending applications and describe last-poll states, not prediction-time conditional rates. Selection into this cell favors precisely the attention signals absent from most of the pooled denominator. I therefore use 10.299% as the required unconditioned starting point, not an estimate for a federal-government application with a requested response and amici.

These are committed-pack figures, not a claim about today's live corpus. The pack's last modifying commit is `3f3ca14f4`, dated October 8, 2026 at 21:56:24 UTC; that is a publication vintage, not the blob's pull vintage. `fedcourts corpus-info` could not report corpus freshness because this cell uses the service backend without a client-side connection. No live corpus totals or case-specific `last_pulled` were obtained; the case information set is the dated provisioned snapshot.

I move substantially above the pooled anchor for three case-specific reasons. First, this is a Solicitor General application concerning a systemwide federal policy, with affirmative judicial attention and two amicus submissions, rather than an undifferentiated emergency request. Second, the government's application offers distinct routes to intervention: APA review of agency policymaking and PLRA constraints on prospective relief (application pp. 15–33). Third, its objection that the order reaches severable surgical provisions beyond the asserted preliminary injury provides a concrete remedial issue, not just disagreement with factual findings (pp. 30–32). The asserted preservation of that objection in opposing the renewed August order makes the preservation dispute genuinely contestable (p. 32). These are qualitative adjustments, not an estimated government-applicant conditional base rate.

There are important reasons to stop at **0.61**. Respondents argue that BOP ignored its own treatment experience, that the injunction preserves individualized clinical decisions rather than prescribing treatment to everyone, and that the PLRA objections were forfeited (opposition pp. 1–5, 31–35). They also contest urgency and identify concrete consequences of withdrawing ongoing care (pp. 36–39). Those arguments make status-quo preservation a serious alternative. I have not independently reviewed the administrative record, medical declarations or operative order, so I do not accept either side's medical characterizations as established fact. A prison-policy deference argument does not automatically decide a record-based APA challenge.

The government's narrower remedial objections also create a specific calibration risk: a partial stay can vindicate an objection without producing an unqualified grant. My working allocation is 0.61 full grant, 0.18 mixed partial relief, 0.19 outright denial and 0.02 withdrawal/dismissal. The latter three sum to the scored ungranted probability of 0.39. This allocation is judgmental, not a fitted empirical model. An inference that some intervention is likely must not be mistaken for a higher full-grant probability.
