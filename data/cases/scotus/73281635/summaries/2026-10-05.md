## In brief

Chicago police officers and the city asked the Court to review whether prejudgment interest can ever be added to noneconomic damages; the Court denied review.

## What happened

Eddie Bolden spent 22 years in prison before his conviction was vacated and charges dropped. He sued the City of Chicago and several police officers, and a jury awarded him $25 million in compensatory damages for pain, suffering, and loss of a normal life. A district court also awarded him about $7.6 million in prejudgment interest, covering the years between his release and the judgment. The officers and the city appealed, and the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals upheld the idea that such interest can apply to noneconomic damages, though it sent the case back to recalculate the interest based on how much of the jury's award covered past versus future harm.

## What the Court is being asked

The petitioners asked the Court to decide whether courts are flatly forbidden from adding extra compensation, in the form of prejudgment interest, onto damages awarded for pain, suffering, and similar non-monetary harms, regardless of the type of legal claim involved.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, the Court requested a response from Bolden, and after further briefing the petition was considered again at a later conference. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The city and officers, as petitioners, asked the Court to take up the case and adopt a categorical rule against this kind of interest. By denying review, the Court leaves the Seventh Circuit's decision in place: Bolden's noneconomic damages can carry prejudgment interest, subject to the lower court recalculating the amount to separate past from future harm. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Seventh Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling standing and ends this case's path through the Supreme Court.
