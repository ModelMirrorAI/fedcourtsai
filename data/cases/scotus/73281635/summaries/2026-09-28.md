## In brief

Chicago and several police officers ask the Court to decide whether interest on a wrongful-conviction damages award may cover harms like pain and suffering, not just financial losses.

## What happened

Eddie Bolden spent 22 years in prison before his conviction was vacated; he later sued the City of Chicago and several officers under federal civil rights law. A jury awarded him $25 million in compensatory damages for pain, suffering, and loss of a normal life. The district court also awarded him about $7.6 million in prejudgment interest, covering the years between his release and the judgment. The Seventh Circuit largely upheld that interest award, though it sent the case back to sort out how much of the jury's award covered past versus future harm. The city and officers then asked the Supreme Court to review the interest question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether there is a fixed legal rule against adding interest, for time spent waiting for judgment, to damages that compensate for pain and suffering rather than lost money or other financial harm. The petitioners argue such interest makes no sense for harms that money cannot truly reverse. Bolden answers that no court has adopted such a categorical bar and that the lower court's discretion to award it was proper.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for the September 28, 2026 conference, after full briefing including a response the Court had requested and a reply. The docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Pesavento and the other petitioners are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the interest question itself, without yet indicating who wins. If it denies review, the Seventh Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place its ruling that courts may, at their discretion, award prejudgment interest on noneconomic damages like Bolden's, subject to the ordered recalculation of past versus future damages. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with that ruling, only that it lets it stand.
