I reduce the reached-band anchor substantially for three case-specific reasons:

- **The petition does not demonstrate a square conflict on its federal question.** Its argument relies heavily on extending criminal-appeal decisions to civil tort appeals (pp. 16–18). Its long survey of waiver and settlement decisions does not establish opposing holdings about the same constitutional notice duty; the petition itself says those cases did not raise its questions (pp. 19–22). I treat the case descriptions as advocacy, not verified holdings.
- **The proposed federal rule is entangled with a particular state's finality rules and this record.** The discussion of whether an order dismissing parties with prejudice was final, the subsequent refusal to issue proposed orders, and the delayed appeal make the vehicle less clean than an appellate ruling squarely deciding a general constitutional duty (pp. 11–15, 22–24). Possible preservation and state-procedural obstacles remain uncertainties, not established jurisdictional bars.
- **The observed docket supplies no affirmative escalation signal.** There is one distribution, a response waiver, and no recorded requested response, CVSG, or amicus filing. Mere first distribution is the cell's selection moment, not independent evidence of unusual interest. Silence about the latter features means absence from this snapshot, not independently verified absence from the current public docket.

The probability remains above zero because the notice/fairness question could have broader consequences, especially if the underlying orders affirmatively misled the litigant. The petition's Hunter analogy is a limited upward consideration, not a known companion-case hold: the question quoted there concerns a criminal plea-agreement waiver, unlike a deadline and finality dispute in civil litigation (pp. 16–17). I did not retrieve Hunter's current status or subsequent history and do not presume its eventual holding.
