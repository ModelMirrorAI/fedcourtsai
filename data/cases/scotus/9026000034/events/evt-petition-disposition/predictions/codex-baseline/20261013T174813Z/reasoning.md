## Case-specific adjustments

**Reasons to raise the odds:** Missouri seeks review of a federal appellate decision invalidating a state-law requirement; sixteen other States support it; and the petition has returned for another conference. The petition's single question is intelligible and the contested scrutiny standard was decided below. Missouri argues that the required no-candy notice merely communicates compliance with unchallenged Halloween conduct restrictions, comparing it to incidental speech in FAIR and factual disclosures upheld elsewhere. The reply directly addresses the vehicle objections rather than abandoning the dispute. These are real reasons to retain a substantial grant possibility. Sources: petition pp. 10–17; reply pp. 2–8; snapshot's amicus and distribution entries.

**Reasons not to move far above the anchor:** The asserted split is not square. The petition contrasts the Fifth Circuit's approval of SORNA registration in United States v. Arnold, 740 F.3d 1032 (5th Cir. 2014), with the Eighth and Eleventh Circuits' treatment of notices posted at homes. The opposition persuasively distinguishes reporting information to government from being required to host a prescribed message for the public. The closest Halloween-sign precedent, McClendon v. Long, 22 F.4th 1330 (11th Cir. 2022), is on the same side as the decision below, not opposite it. The reply stresses the comparative burdens and factual nature of the disclosures but does not identify an appellate decision upholding an equivalent home-sign mandate. Sources: petition pp. 10–12; opposition pp. 6–10; reply pp. 2–6. A CourtListener citation search independently verified McClendon's identity and January 19, 2022 date; I did not obtain its full opinion through that search.

The lower court did not simply overlook incidental-speech doctrine. Its appended opinion addresses FAIR and distinguishes a requirement prescribing verbatim speech from the statute's other conduct rules. It then ties narrow tailoring to trial evidence, including the law's lack of size or placement requirements and the sufficiency of the remaining restrictions. That makes this less obviously a summary-correction case. Sources: petition appendix pp. 6a–11a; opposition pp. 10–14.

The district court additionally concluded that the provision failed a less demanding First Amendment standard (appendix p. 36a n.9). Missouri fairly answers that the court of appeals relied on strict scrutiny and could apply a corrected standard on remand (reply pp. 7–8). I therefore treat this as friction, not an insuperable jurisdictional obstacle. The opposition also reports that the post-remand injunction was narrowed to Sanderson alone, reducing the immediate remedial stakes without eliminating the appellate precedent (opposition p. 6). No basis appears for forecasting a CASA-related GVR merely because the lower proceedings discussed injunction scope.

## Final probabilities and stakes

I settle at **24% for any grant**: the state-petitioner anchor and relist prevent a generic low-single-digit forecast, but the weak same-question conflict and record-specific tailoring findings substantially offset the affirmative signals. Denial is the modal disposition. Further relisting (40%), a new CVSG (3%), a summary route conditional on grant (12%), and a separate writing conditional on denial (18%) are judgmental forecasts, not empirically estimated transition rates. A further conference could serve either grant consideration or preparation of a writing; I do not equate delay with a grant.

The **0.50 stakes score** reflects the public salience of the Halloween restriction and a potentially consequential boundary between compelled speech and factual notices. It is limited by the particular provision at issue, the narrow remedial posture reported in the opposition, and the plausible distinction from ordinary registration laws. It is not a second grant-likelihood estimate.

Web searches and an attempted opening of the historical McClendon opinion returned no usable content.
