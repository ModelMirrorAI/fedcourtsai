## In brief

Missouri asked the Court to revive a Halloween sign-posting rule for sex offenders that lower courts struck down as compelled speech; the Court declined to hear the case.

## What happened

Missouri law bars registered sex offenders from participating in Halloween, including a requirement to post a sign reading "No candy or treats at this residence." Thomas Sanderson, convicted of a sex offense years earlier, had been told by police the law did not apply to him and kept up his annual Halloween display until he was prosecuted. He challenged only the sign requirement as unconstitutional compelled speech. After a trial, a federal district court and then the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals agreed, finding Missouri had not shown the sign added any real benefit beyond the law's other restrictions. Missouri and a Hazelwood police official then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether a state can require people convicted of sex offenses to post a specific sign at their homes on Halloween, given trial evidence that the sign did not meaningfully help protect children or help police enforce the law's other restrictions, or whether that requirement forces people to express a government message in violation of free speech protections.

## Where it stands

The Court considered the petition at conferences on October 9 and October 16, 2026, and on October 19, 2026, it denied the petition. Justice Alito noted that he would have granted it.

## What each outcome would mean

Missouri and the Hazelwood police chief, as petitioners, asked the Court to reverse the Eighth Circuit's decision favoring Sanderson. With the petition denied, the Eighth Circuit's ruling stands: Missouri cannot enforce the sign-posting requirement, though its other Halloween restrictions on sex offenders remain in place. A grant would have brought the case before the Court for full briefing and argument, with no outcome yet decided; that did not happen here.
