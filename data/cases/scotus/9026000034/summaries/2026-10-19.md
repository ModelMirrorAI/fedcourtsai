## In brief

The Court declined to hear Missouri's bid to revive a law requiring sex offenders to post "no candy or treats" Halloween signs, struck down as compelled speech.

## What happened

Missouri law bars registered sex offenders from participating in Halloween and requires them to post a sign reading "No candy or treats at this residence." Thomas Sanderson, convicted of a sex offense years earlier, had been told by police the law didn't apply to him and continued hosting Halloween displays until he was prosecuted. He sued, challenging only the sign requirement as compelled speech. After a trial, a federal district court found Missouri had a legitimate interest in protecting children but had not shown the sign added any real protection, and struck it down. The Eighth Circuit agreed, joining a similar ruling from the Eleventh Circuit.

## What the Court is being asked

Missouri asked the Court to decide whether states may require convicted sex offenders to post a sign disclosing their non-participation in Halloween, or whether such a mandate counts as unconstitutional compelled speech. Missouri argued the requirement merely discloses a fact tied to lawful conduct restrictions, while Sanderson argued it forces him to broadcast an unwanted message without evidence it protects children.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for two conferences, and on October 19, 2026, the Court denied it. Justice Alito noted he would have granted the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Missouri, as petitioner, asked the Court to take up the case and reverse the lower courts. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Eighth Circuit's ruling in place, meaning Missouri cannot enforce the sign-posting requirement against Sanderson or other registered sex offenders in the state. The denial does not mean the Court agreed the sign mandate is unconstitutional; it simply leaves that question undecided at the Supreme Court level, with the lower court's decision standing.
