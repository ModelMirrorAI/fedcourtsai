## In brief

The Court declined to hear a Pennsylvania man's challenge to decades-old DNA evidence and a warrantless trash search used to convict him of a 1995 rape.

## What happened

In 1995, T.L. was assaulted in State College, Pennsylvania, and DNA was recovered from her sexual assault exam. In 2000, police filed a "John Doe" warrant describing the suspect only by generic genetic markers, without attaching an actual DNA profile. Decades later, after Scott Williams's mother submitted her DNA to a genealogy company, investigators identified Williams as a possible match. Without a warrant, police collected DNA from items used by Williams's minor son and from Williams's own household trash, then used it to link him to the 1995 crime. Williams was convicted of rape and aggravated assault. He argued the original warrant was unconstitutionally vague and that the warrantless DNA collection violated his rights, but the Pennsylvania Superior Court rejected both arguments, and the Pennsylvania Supreme Court declined further review.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions: whether a warrant naming an unknown suspect only by genetic markers shared by all humans, without an actual DNA profile, is specific enough to satisfy the rule against vague warrants; and whether police need a warrant before extracting and analyzing DNA collected from someone's discarded trash or from items used by a family member, rather than taken directly from the person.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, and the Court denied it on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Williams, who lost in the Pennsylvania courts, asked the Court to take up his case. Had the Court granted review, it would have heard full arguments and decided whether the warrant and DNA collection methods violated constitutional search protections, with no result presumed in advance. By denying review, the Court leaves the Pennsylvania Superior Court's decision in place, meaning Williams's conviction stands and the evidence obtained through the DNA warrant and trash search remains valid in his case. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning, only that it leaves that ruling undisturbed.
