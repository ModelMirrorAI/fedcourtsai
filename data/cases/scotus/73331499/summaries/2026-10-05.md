## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to review a Pennsylvania man's challenge to his rape conviction, which rested on DNA evidence obtained from an old warrant and his discarded trash.

## What happened

In 1995, police collected DNA evidence from a sexual assault but could not identify a suspect. In 2000, to preserve the case before a deadline expired, they filed a "John Doe" warrant describing the unknown attacker only by generic genetic markers shared by all humans, without attaching an actual DNA profile. Decades later, using a genealogy database and DNA from Scott Williams's mother, police identified Williams as a suspect. Without a warrant, they collected DNA from his son's discarded utensils at a school event and from chew-spit bottles in Williams's trash, then built a DNA profile matching the 1995 evidence. Williams was convicted of rape and aggravated assault. Pennsylvania's courts upheld the conviction, ruling the old warrant was specific enough and that no warrant was needed to test the discarded DNA.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions about police use of DNA. First, whether a warrant naming an unknown suspect only by genetic markers common to everyone, without including his actual DNA profile, is specific enough to satisfy the Constitution's warrant requirements. Second, whether police need a warrant before extracting and analyzing DNA from items a person has thrown away or left behind, including items used by a family member.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference, and on October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Williams asked the Court to take up his case and overturn the Pennsylvania courts' rulings. The Court's denial means it will not hear the case, leaving the Pennsylvania Superior Court's decision in place: the warrant was valid, no warrant was needed to test the discarded DNA, and Williams's conviction stands. The denial does not mean the Court agreed that the lower court was right, only that it chose not to review the case.
