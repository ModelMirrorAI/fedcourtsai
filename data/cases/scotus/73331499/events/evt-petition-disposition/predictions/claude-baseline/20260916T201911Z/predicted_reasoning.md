# Forecast: Williams v. Pennsylvania, No. 25-1277 (evt-petition-disposition)

**Disposition.** The Court will deny the petition. The petition was distributed once, on June 24, 2026, for the Conference of September 28, 2026 (the long conference), and no brief in opposition or waiver appears on the docket. The most likely path is a denial on the first order list of October Term 2026 (Monday, October 5, 2026).

**Relists.** The docket shows one distribution. I expect no further distribution in about four of five cases. The main route to a second distribution is not a true relist but a call for a response: the Commonwealth has not opposed, and the Court does not grant an unopposed petition without first requesting a response, which pulls the petition off the September 28 conference and redistributes it after the response arrives (roughly 20% for any additional distribution, most of that mass being a response request). A relist after the response, if one is requested, would be the signal to watch; absent a response request, a relist off the long conference for this petition is unlikely.

**CVSG.** No call for the views of the Solicitor General. This is a state prosecution with no federal party, and the Court almost never invites the SG's views on a state criminal Fourth Amendment petition. The FBI's role in the CODIS profiling is a factual detail, not a federal interest the Court would need briefed.

**Which question, if taken.** If the Court took anything it would be a reformulated version of QP 2, the warrantless extraction and profiling of shed DNA (the "abandonment" question), which is the question of broad and recurring importance now that genetic genealogy drives cold-case identification. QP 1, the particularity of a John Doe DNA complaint listing only RFLP loci, is bound up with a 2000 charging document under a superseded testing method and Pennsylvania's tolling rules, and the Court would leave it behind. As written, QP 2 is grammatically truncated ("from."), so the Court would have to rewrite it.

**Summary disposition.** Not the likelier route. No intervening decision of this Court bears on either question, so a GVR has nothing to ride on, and a per curiam reversal of a published state appellate decision on an open Fourth Amendment question is implausible. Conditional on a grant, plenary review is the overwhelmingly likely form (roughly 92%).

**Dissent from denial.** Most likely none. The shed-DNA and genetic-genealogy questions are the kind on which Justice Sotomayor or Justice Gorsuch has occasionally written separately to flag an open Fourth Amendment issue, so a short statement respecting denial is possible but not expected (about 6%).

**If granted (conditional only).** The Court would most likely hold that extracting and profiling DNA from lawfully seized abandoned items is a search requiring a warrant absent an exception, extending Carpenter's reasoning about the revealing nature of the data rather than applying California v. Greenwood's abandonment rule to the genetic content of the item. It would reach that question only on the trash-bottle DNA; the son's DNA raises a standing problem the Court would avoid.
