## In brief

A lawyer suspended by a federal appeals court for filing briefs with fake case citations asks the Supreme Court to overturn the suspension for lack of fair notice.

## What happened

Mike Singh Sethi, an immigration lawyer, filed appellate briefs containing fabricated case citations and misquoted opinions. The Ninth Circuit ordered him and a colleague to explain why they should not be disciplined. After they responded, blaming the errors on staff and denying knowing use of artificial intelligence, the court found their explanations themselves were not truthful, and issued a published order suspending Sethi from practice before it for six months, along with a fine and other requirements. Sethi's petition says the court's final order relied on conduct and findings never spelled out in the original notice.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it violates fair notice and due process rules for a court to rely on an attorney's response to disciplinary charges, or on his later conduct, as the basis for additional misconduct findings and a harsher penalty, without first charging him with that new conduct and giving him a chance to defend against it. The Court is also asked whether a court of appeals can punish a lawyer for not following a specific disclosure requirement that the court announced only in its final decision, rather than beforehand in a rule or order.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and the federal respondents waived their right to respond. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on November 6, 2026, and the docket shows no action since.

## What each outcome would mean

Sethi, as petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide whether the suspension violated fair-notice requirements, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's order suspending Sethi and the other sanctions it imposed would stand. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the suspension was proper, only that the lower court's decision remains in place.
