## In brief

A lawyer suspended by a federal appeals court for filing briefs with fabricated case citations asks the Supreme Court to overturn that suspension for lack of fair notice.

## What happened

Mike Singh Sethi, an immigration lawyer, filed appellate briefs containing fake case citations and misquoted opinions, generated by an unlicensed brief writer at his firm who may have misused artificial intelligence. The Ninth Circuit granted his clients' immigration case on the merits but separately ordered Sethi and a colleague to explain why they should not be disciplined. After reviewing their responses and oral argument testimony, the court suspended Sethi from practice for six months, fined him, and imposed other sanctions, citing violations of professional conduct rules including a failure of candor about the errors' cause.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a court of appeals can impose or increase professional discipline on a lawyer based on conduct — including his own written defense or later corrective filings — that was never spelled out as a charge against him, denying him a fair chance to respond. It is also asked whether a court can punish a lawyer for not following a specific disclosure rule that was first announced in the very decision punishing him, rather than in any law, rule, or earlier notice.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed September 1, 2026. The federal government, representing the court below, waived its right to respond. The case was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference, where the justices will decide whether to take it.

## What each outcome would mean

Sethi, the lawyer disciplined below, is asking the Court to take up his case and overturn the suspension. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide whether the Ninth Circuit's procedure violated fair-notice requirements; granting review alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's suspension and sanctions against Sethi remain in effect, and the case would not receive further Supreme Court consideration.
