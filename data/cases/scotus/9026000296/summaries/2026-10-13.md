## In brief

An attorney suspended by a federal appeals court for filing briefs with fabricated case citations asks the Supreme Court to overturn that suspension.

## What happened

Mike Singh Sethi, an immigration attorney, filed briefs in the Ninth Circuit that contained fabricated case citations and misquoted opinions. After the court questioned his colleague at oral argument, it ordered Sethi and the colleague to explain why they should not be disciplined. Sethi responded that he believed the errors were typographical, then later acknowledged AI use by a firm employee was likely responsible. The Ninth Circuit's final order suspended Sethi from practice for six months, fined him, and imposed other requirements, finding that his explanations and later filings in other cases involved additional dishonesty toward the court. Sethi now challenges that discipline.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal appeals court violates due process when it disciplines a lawyer more harshly based on conduct, such as the lawyer's own defense statements or later filings, that was never separately identified as a charge before the lawyer had to respond. The Court is also asked whether the appeals court could punish the lawyer for not meeting a specific disclosure requirement, about naming AI as the source of errors, that was not written into any law, rule, or prior order before the lawyer acted.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed and the federal respondents, represented by the Solicitor General, waived their right to respond. The docket shows no further action since that waiver.

## What each outcome would mean

Sethi, the attorney, asked the Court to hear the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide whether the suspension violated due process or exceeded the appeals court's rulemaking limits, without yet deciding who is right. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's order suspending Sethi and imposing other sanctions stays in effect, though denial does not mean the Court agrees the suspension was proper.
