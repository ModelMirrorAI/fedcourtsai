## In brief

Alabama asks the Court to reverse a court order blocking its 2023 congressional map, which a lower court found violates the Voting Rights Act and the Constitution.

## What happened

Alabama's congressional map has long been challenged by Black voters and organizations who say it dilutes their voting strength by creating only one district, out of seven, where they can elect their preferred candidate. Federal courts blocked an earlier map in 2022, and the Supreme Court agreed in 2023 that it likely violated the Voting Rights Act. Alabama then passed a replacement, the 2023 Plan, which again included only one such district. After a trial, the district court found this plan also violated the law and was enacted with intentional racial discrimination, and ordered Alabama to use a court-drawn map instead. Alabama appealed, and after the Supreme Court decided a related case called Callais, the dispute returned to the lower court, which reached the same conclusion. Alabama now asks the Supreme Court to take up the case before a federal appeals court rules.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the lower court was wrong to find that Alabama's map likely dilutes Black voters' voting strength, including whether it properly required challengers to show their proposed alternative maps met the state's stated priorities, properly separated race from politics in analyzing voting patterns, and properly weighed historical and present-day evidence of discrimination. The Court is also asked whether Alabama violated the Constitution by declining to draw a map with a second district where Black voters could elect their preferred candidate.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for three conferences, most recently November 13, 2026, and the docket shows no action on it since.

## What each outcome would mean

Alabama, the Secretary of State and other officials, asked the Court to take the case and reverse. The voters and organizations who sued, including Marcus Caster and others, argue the petition should be denied, in part because they say the dispute is moot since the Supreme Court already allowed the 2023 Plan to be used in the 2026 election. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments on whether the map is lawful, deciding the question itself rather than any particular outcome. If it denies the petition, the lower court's ruling against Alabama's map stands, at least for now, though the underlying case continues in the lower courts.
