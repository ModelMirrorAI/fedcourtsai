## In brief

Accused September 11 plotters ask the Supreme Court to review a ruling that let the Defense Secretary withdraw from plea agreements sparing them the death penalty.

## What happened

Khalid Shaikh Mohammad and a co-defendant, charged before a military commission with orchestrating the September 11 attacks, signed plea agreements promising guilty pleas in exchange for the government not seeking death sentences. Two days later the Secretary of Defense withdrew from those agreements. A military judge and the Court of Military Commission Review both ruled the withdrawal came too late because the men had already begun performing their side of the deal. The government asked the D.C. Circuit for writs of mandamus and prohibition, extraordinary orders used to correct a lower court seen as clearly wrong. The D.C. Circuit granted them, over one judge's dissent, and later denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The petitioners ask the Court to decide two things: whether the appeals court had authority to issue this kind of extraordinary order at all, given that Congress did not give the government a right to appeal at this stage of a military commission case; and, if it did, whether the government's right to the order was so clear and obvious that issuing it was proper, given that the military judge had followed an existing military appeals court precedent.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply, and was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference; the docket shows it was then rescheduled, with no record yet of the Court granting or denying review.

## What each outcome would mean

Mohammad and his co-defendant petitioned after losing in the D.C. Circuit, which had ruled for the United States. If the Court grants review, it will decide the case on the merits after full briefing and argument, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the D.C. Circuit's decision stands, meaning the Secretary's withdrawal from the plea agreements takes effect and the military judge cannot proceed on the agreements' terms. A grant does not indicate which side would ultimately prevail.
