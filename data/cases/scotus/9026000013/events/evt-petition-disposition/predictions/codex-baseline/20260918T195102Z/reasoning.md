## Why 16%, rather than the anchor or a likely grant

Factors raising the probability above 5.02%:

- **Exceptional consequences.** The pre-decision filings describe life-and-death plea terms in the September 11 prosecutions, prolonged proceedings, and the Secretary's reversal of an authorized bargain. These stakes justify unusually serious consideration, independently of celebrity or media coverage. Petition pp. 1–4, 39–40; BIO pp. 18–19.
- **A substantial supervisory question.** There is a real distinction between an appellate court possessing prospective jurisdiction and its using mandamus as ordinary interlocutory error correction. The military judge, unanimous military appellate panel, and D.C. Circuit dissenter took a different view of performance from the panel majority. That disagreement makes the clear-and-indisputable question credible, even though disagreement alone does not legally preclude mandamus. Petition pp. 12–15, 30–38; BIO pp. 5–8, 15–18.
- **A concrete precedent tension.** The available panel opinion expressly declines to follow aspects of United States v. Dean, while also distinguishing its facts and treating CAAF constructions as nonbinding in military commissions. This is more substantive than merely counting different adjectives used by circuits. It nevertheless falls short of a clean identical-statute circuit split. Petition appendix pp. 40a–41a n.10.
- **Attention beyond the parties.** The snapshot records three amicus filings, including victim-family and academic submissions. I use their presence, not imagined contents, as modest evidence of attention.

Factors keeping denial clearly favored:

- **The alleged jurisdictional split is contested and imperfect.** The petition itself acknowledges that Kane recognized jurisdiction and that the Second Circuit decisions assumed jurisdiction. The BIO identifies government mandamus grants in the supposedly contrary circuits and distinguishes an extraordinary remedy's availability from its appropriateness in a particular case. I have not independently verified every circuit decision and do not treat either side's characterization as conclusive. Petition pp. 18–23; BIO pp. 12–15.
- **Will does not decide the categorical proposition as broadly as the petition suggests.** I checked Will v. United States, 389 U.S. 90, 95 n.4, 97–98 (1967), through CourtListener. Its reserved question about nondismissal procedural orders supports the government's response to a categorical prohibition. Its warning against using mandamus to evade restricted criminal appeals simultaneously supports the petitioners' narrower supervisory concern. Thus the precedent gives a genuine issue, not an obvious entitlement to reversal.
- **Vehicle complications.** The BIO says the jurisdictional theory was not meaningfully developed below and notes that the panel did not decide whether the government could appeal a final commission judgment. I treat these as obstacles to a clean grant, not forfeiture of subject-matter jurisdiction. The distinctive military-commission statutes also complicate using this case to settle the civilian-circuit dispute. BIO pp. 10–11, 15.
- **Question two can look like case-specific error correction.** The government's account rests on the agreements' text and the absence of post-execution performance, not a declared reduction of the mandamus standard. The panel's Dean discussion includes an alternative factual distinction. A dispute over applying a shared demanding standard is less compelling than a demonstrably incompatible rule. BIO pp. 15–18; petition appendix pp. 39a–42a.
- **Opposition and no observed escalation.** The government seeks denial; there is no confession of error or CVSG recommendation favoring review. Only the first petition conference is pending. The record supplies no later relist signal, and the BIO reports only the panel dissenter noted support for rehearing en banc.

My 16% is a judgmental upward adjustment, not a fitted estimate or a product of the descriptive cuts. The biggest uncertainty is how much supervisory concern the Court attaches to the exercise of mandamus despite the disputed split and unusual statutory setting. National significance alone does not make four votes for certiorari likely.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The **40% additional-distribution** forecast exceeds an ordinary first-conference expectation because of the stakes, substantial briefing, and plausible separate writing, while retaining no further distribution as the modal path. **0.5% CVSG** reflects the fact that the Solicitor General is already appearing for the respondent. **12% summary route conditional on grant** allows a supervisory correction but discounts it because there is no identified intervening authority and the disagreement is not straightforward. **30% separate writing conditional on denial** reflects the capital consequences and extraordinary-writ issue without presuming any Justice's position. These increments and conditional forecasts are judgmental; the pack supplies no directly applicable forward-hazard estimate.

The **0.97 big-case score** measures consequences if decided: accountability for the September 11 attacks, capital exposure, plea enforcement, and executive control of military commissions. It is deliberately distinct from the 0.16 grant probability.
