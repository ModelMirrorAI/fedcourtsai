# Forecast — Khalid Shaikh Mohammad, et al. v. United States, No. 26-13 (evt-petition-disposition)

**Bottom line: the Court denies certiorari, most likely at or shortly after the
October 2026 long conference, with a realistic chance of a noted dissent or a
statement respecting denial.**

## Procedural path from here

- **Distribution and relists.** The docket shows exactly one distribution so
  far, and it was the sealing motion (25M91), distributed June 10, 2026 for the
  June 25 conference and granted June 29. The petition itself has never been
  conferenced: the brief in opposition is due August 26, 2026 (extended once, at
  the government's request). I forecast the petition is distributed for the
  long conference (late September 2026) once the BIO and any reply are in, and
  — given the case's profile, three cert-stage amicus briefs, and the
  likelihood a Justice writes separately on denial — that it is then relisted
  one to two further times before disposition. So from the frozen count of one
  distribution, at least one further distribution is close to certain
  (~0.97), and two or more further distributions are more likely than not.
- **No CVSG.** The United States is the respondent and the Solicitor General
  is already counsel of record; a call for the views of the Solicitor General
  is structurally impossible here. ~0.01, reflecting only clerical residue.
- **Disposition and timing.** Deny, most likely on an order list in October or
  November 2026. Aggregate probability of any grant (plenary, in part, GVR, or
  summary reversal): 0.10.
- **Summary route, conditional on grant.** If the Court takes it, it takes it
  for plenary review — there is no intervening decision to GVR in light of,
  and summarily reversing a published, divided D.C. Circuit mandamus grant in
  the 9/11 case is not a realistic route. P(summary disposition | grant) ≈
  0.03.
- **If granted, which question.** QP1 — whether a court of appeals has All
  Writs Act jurisdiction to give the government mandamus relief from an
  interlocutory procedural order in a criminal case where Congress provided no
  appeal — is the more certworthy question and the one the Court would take,
  likely as written. QP2 (what "clear and indisputable" requires) reads as
  error-correction dressed as a split and would either be left behind or
  folded into QP1. (Registered context-only; no scorer resolves this.)
- **Separate writings on denial.** A dissent from denial or a statement
  respecting denial is a live possibility (~0.15 conditional on denial). The
  government's use of mandamus to undo a trial-court ruling that followed
  existing precedent — in a capital case, with Will v. United States squarely
  in tension — is the kind of institutional question that has drawn statements
  from Justices Sotomayor and Jackson; Justice Gorsuch's criminal-procedure
  formalism makes him a secondary candidate. (The "from whom" half is
  context-only and unscored.)
- **A wrinkle worth recording:** Justice Kavanaugh took no part in the June 29
  sealing-motion order. If that recusal carries to the petition, an
  eight-Justice bench modestly lowers the odds of four grant votes.

## Why deny

The Court has refused every invitation to supervise the Guantanamo military
commissions since Boumediene (2008) — it denied certiorari on the al-Nashiri
and Baluchi mandamus litigation from the same D.C. Circuit — and here the
Solicitor General, who won below, will oppose. The posture is interlocutory:
commission proceedings continue, and the Court can let the plea-agreement
question return, if ever, on a fuller record. The asserted splits, while
real enough on paper, are entangled with the commissions' sui generis
appellate structure (10 U.S.C. § 950d), which gives the Court a
vehicle-quality reason to stay out. Against that: the QPs are well-built, the
panel divided sharply (Judge Wilkins called the majority "stunning"), and the
case's salience is maximal — which is why the denial probability is not
higher than 0.90.
