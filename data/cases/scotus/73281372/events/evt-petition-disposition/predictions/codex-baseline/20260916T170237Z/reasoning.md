## Why 16%, rather than 5% or 50%

The petition has a stronger claim to review than an undifferentiated private petition. Its question is a substantial interaction between Chapter 15, the securities safe harbor, foreign-law recovery powers, and the presumption against extraterritoriality. Petition pages 17–30 argue that Section 561(d) cannot enlarge the domestic safe harbor into a direct defense against foreign statutory and common-law claims. The distinction between a direct statutory bar and implied preemption is potentially consequential where the underlying law is foreign rather than state law. Pages 30–35 describe the wider cross-border effects and approximately $6 billion in asserted claims. Those are petitioners' stakes assertions, not an independently audited recovery estimate. Experienced Supreme Court counsel and the two filed amicus briefs are modest additional attention signals; neither establishes that four Justices want review.

The opposition materially weakens the asserted conflict. On its account, Condor addressed the availability of foreign avoidance powers under Section 1521(a)(7), not the operation of Sections 546(e) and 561(d). A holding that a foreign-law power exists need not decide whether a separate safe harbor limits it. On the common-law issue, petition pages 27–30 emphasize different legal mechanisms in Frost and Grede; opposition pages 13–16 emphasize that those domestic cases ultimately barred the challenged claims and did not adjudicate the Chapter 15 incorporation question. I regard this as a serious disagreement about reasoning and possible downstream consequences, but not a verified square conflict producing opposite judgments on the same statutory issue. These descriptions rest on the competing provisioned briefs, not independent reads of those precedents.

Vehicle objections also matter. Opposition pages 25–26 describe roughly 300 adversary proceedings, more than 400 parties, extensive foreign litigation, and multiple alternative dismissal grounds. Petition pages 34–35 answer that the questions were fully litigated and cleanly presented. Complexity alone does not defeat review, and I have not established that every alternative ground independently defeats all possible relief. Nevertheless, the opposition identifies genuine reasons to prefer further development or a cleaner case. I therefore raise the baseline materially to **16%**, but keep denial decisively more likely than review. That adjustment is judgment, not a fitted model or a claim that the split has been established.

## Other probabilities and stakes

The **35%** additional-distribution probability reflects the nonfrivolous statutory controversy and the possibility of closer scrutiny, while leaving no additional distribution as the mode. The **8%** CVSG probability is above routine private-litigation expectations because the petition frames international cooperation concerns, but there is no existing invitation, government participation, or provisioned foreign-sovereign brief on which to anchor a high consultation probability. No published transition baseline was used for either increment.

The **6% conditional summary-route probability** reflects the absence of an identified intervening authority and the complexity of the competing interpretations. It is conditional on any grant, not an unconditional six-percentage-point allocation of outcomes. The **4% conditional separate-writing probability** reflects my expectation of a routine denial even though the legal question is important. No per-Justice cert vote is inferred from docket silence.

The **0.65 big-case score** measures stakes, not review odds: the asserted financial exposure, foreign investor recoveries, and potentially generalizable cross-border bankruptcy rules make this more consequential than a typical commercial dispute, but its direct reach remains a specialized area rather than a broad constitutional controversy.

## Limitations and retrieval

`documents.json` marks the 345-page petition source as truncated, although the supplied text includes its question presented and complete main argument through the conclusion. I read those portions, not an assuredly complete appendix. The BIO is listed as 31 pages, nonempty and untruncated; its extracted text has some dropped words. I used its readable arguments and did not fill those gaps with invented quotations. The reply and amicus contents remain unavailable in the provisioned inputs.

A CourtListener citation lookup for Condor was throttled with HTTP 429. General web searches for Section 561(d) and the Court's certiorari criteria, and a statutory-page open, returned no usable content. I therefore proceeded on the provisioned advocacy and committed base rates, with no direct REST fallback and no independently verified precedent text. This reduces confidence in distinguishing the claimed conflicts. I did not retrieve this petition's disposition or subsequent history and have no known outcome for it. The BIO's reference to a 2015 denial concerns a different, parallel domestic proceeding, not the event being forecast; it received little predictive weight.
