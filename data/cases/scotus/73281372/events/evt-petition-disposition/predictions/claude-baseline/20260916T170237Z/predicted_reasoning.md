# Forecast: Fairfield Sentry Ltd. v. Citibank NA London, No. 25-1089

**Bottom line: the Court denies certiorari, most likely after the petition
is carried past the September 28, 2026 long conference at least once.**

## Relists

The docket shows **one distribution** (June 17, 2026, for the Conference of
September 28, 2026 — the long conference). No relist has occurred yet.

I expect the petition to be distributed **at least once more** with roughly
40% probability. The paths to a further distribution are (a) a call for the
views of the Solicitor General, which takes the petition off the calendar
until the SG files and then re-distributes it, and (b) an ordinary relist
while a Justice considers writing or the Court weighs a grant. If the
petition is relisted without a CVSG, I expect one or two further relists,
not a long string; if a CVSG issues, the redistribution would follow the
SG's brief, most likely in the spring of 2027.

## Call for the views of the Solicitor General

No CVSG is on the docket. I put the chance of one at about **22%** — well
above the docket-wide rate, because the petition (i) presents an
extraterritoriality question about a statute that implements the UNCITRAL
Model Law on cross-border insolvency, which the United States negotiated and
in which the Executive has a standing institutional interest, and (ii) asks
about the §546(e) safe harbor, on which the Court invited the SG's views in
the Tribune litigation. Against a CVSG: no federal party or program is
directly implicated, the SG's Madoff-related views have historically favored
the safe harbor, and the Court denied every prior Madoff safe-harbor petition
without an invitation.

## Which question the Court would take, if it takes one

Petitioners frame a single question — whether §546(e) applies
extraterritorially through §561(d) to bar foreign statutory and common-law
claims. If the Court grants, I expect it to **reformulate**, most plausibly
into the two questions the Brief in Opposition separates: (1) whether
applying §561(d) in a Chapter 15 case is an extraterritorial application of
the Code at all, and (2) whether the safe harbor, applied through §561(d),
reaches common-law claims that seek to undo covered transfers. The second
question — the Second Circuit's holding that §546(e) directly bars
common-law claims without engaging *Merit Management* — is the one with the
clearest tension with other circuits' reasoning and the likelier hook for a
grant. A grant limited to the extraterritoriality question alone is less
likely, because the district court's alternative "domestic application"
ground (step two of the *Abitron* framework) gives the Court a reason to
think the question may not be outcome-determinative.

## Summary disposition

Unlikely. There is no intervening decision of this Court bearing on §561(d)
or on the common-law-claims question, so a GVR has nothing to rest on, and
the case is far too procedurally tangled for a per curiam reversal.
Conditional on a grant, I put the summary route at about 5%; the ordinary
grant would be plenary, set for argument in the spring of 2027 or the
following Term.

## Dissent from denial

If the Court denies, I expect **no separate writing** (about 5% chance of a
dissent or statement respecting denial). Business-bankruptcy petitions of
this kind rarely draw a noted dissent, and no Justice has staked out a
public position on the §546(e) safe harbor's reach beyond the unanimous
*Merit Management* opinion. The most plausible author of a statement, were
one to appear, would be Justice Sotomayor (the Second Circuit's Circuit
Justice, who has written separately on bankruptcy questions) or Justice
Gorsuch on the extraterritoriality canon, but I do not predict one.

## If granted

The likely ground for reversal would be the Second Circuit's treatment of
"avoidance powers" in §546(e)/§561(d) as reaching common-law claims, which
sits uneasily with *Merit Management*'s description of the safe harbor as an
exception to the enumerated Chapter 5 avoiding powers. The
extraterritoriality holding would be the harder ground for petitioners,
because respondents have a serious step-two argument that applying §561(d)
inside an American Chapter 15 proceeding is a domestic application.
