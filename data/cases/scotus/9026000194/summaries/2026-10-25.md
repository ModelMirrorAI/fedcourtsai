## In brief

Mei Guo and her company ask the Court to decide whether a bankruptcy trustee could use a federal statute to claim their yacht as Kwok's alter ego.

## What happened

Ho Wan Kwok filed for bankruptcy after a New York court found he controlled a yacht, the Lady May, nominally owned by HK USA, a company owned by his daughter Mei Guo. The bankruptcy trustee, Luc Despins, argued HK USA was Kwok's alter ego and that its assets, including the yacht and $37 million in escrow, belonged to Kwok's estate. The bankruptcy court and district court agreed, and the Second Circuit affirmed, holding that a federal bankruptcy statute let the trustee pursue this claim on behalf of creditors generally.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the law giving a bankruptcy trustee the same powers as a hypothetical creditor with a court-ordered claim on the debtor's property lets the trustee bring a claim—available under state law to such a creditor—arguing that a company is really just the debtor in disguise, so the company's assets belong to the estate for all creditors, rather than leaving the claim to be pursued, if at all, by creditors individually.

## Where it stands

The trustee opposed the petition, and after an extension, filed a brief in opposition on October 21, 2026. The petition remains pending; the docket does not show that the Court has acted on it since that filing.

## What each outcome would mean

Mei Guo and HK USA are asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Second Circuit's decision, which favored the trustee. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide whether the statute allows this kind of claim; a grant alone would not decide who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving the trustee's claim to the yacht and other assets intact. The trustee's brief argues that even a ruling for Guo and HK USA would not change the outcome, since other legal grounds could support the same result.
