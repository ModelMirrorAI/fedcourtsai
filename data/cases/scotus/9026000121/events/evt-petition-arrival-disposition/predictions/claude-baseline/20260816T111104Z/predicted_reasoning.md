# Forecast: Basso v. Rodriguez, No. 26-121 (petition disposition, forecast at arrival)

The docket shows zero distributions — this is the arrival moment, before any
conference signal exists. The petition was docketed July 27, 2026, with the
brief in opposition due August 26, 2026.

**Disposition: denied.** The Court will deny this petition without comment. I
expect the respondents to waive a response (a private-party respondent in a
low-stakes civil case usually does), the petition to be distributed once — for
the long conference at the end of September 2026 or an early October 2026
conference — and a denial to issue on the order list that follows, most likely
in early-to-mid October 2026.

**Relists: one distribution, no relist.** The petition will be distributed at
least once (nearly every paid petition that is not withdrawn or dismissed
reaches a conference), but I expect exactly one distribution and no relist.
Nothing here has the profile that draws a second look: no circuit or state
split is alleged — the petition expressly frames QP 1 as a question of first
impression and concedes "there is no controlling Supreme Court precedent
directly on point" — and the claim is fact-bound.

**CVSG: none.** No federal party, no federal statute, no federal-government
interest of any kind — a private consumer-fraud and attorney-fee dispute under
Maryland law. The Court will not call for the Solicitor General's views.

**Which QP, if taken.** If the Court acted at all, it would act only on QP 1
(the Caperton/Williams due-process claim about a judgment entered by a retired
judge allegedly ineligible for recall while practicing law), and it would
reformulate it — the QP as drafted is a multi-clause run-on the Court would
not carry into a grant. QP 2 (whether summary reversal should issue because
the Maryland Supreme Court's later Sugarloaf decision requires more specific
attorney-fee memoranda) is a pure question of state law the Court has no
jurisdiction to correct; it will not be taken in any form.

**Summary route, conditional on a grant.** If the Court did act favorably —
already unlikely — the route would more likely be summary than plenary: the
extreme-facts judicial-bias cases the Court has acted on (e.g., Rippo v.
Baker's per curiam vacatur) went by summary disposition, and this record is
too idiosyncratic to be a plenary vehicle. Hence my conditional
summary-disposition probability sits above the population's cert-order share
of grants. A GVR specifically is implausible: the only intervening decision
the petition points to is a state-court one, which is not a basis on which
the Court GVRs.

**Dissent from denial: none expected.** No Justice has a stake in this
fact-bound, state-law-entangled recusal claim; denial will be silent.
