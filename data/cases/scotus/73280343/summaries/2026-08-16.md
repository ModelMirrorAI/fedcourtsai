## In brief

Two immigrants whose asylum status was later terminated ask the Court to decide whether they can still become lawful permanent residents.

## What happened

Tamer Wassily and Byron Velasquez Arreaga were each granted asylum, then had that status terminated after criminal convictions. Both later sought to become lawful permanent residents. In Wassily's case, an immigration judge found him eligible and granted the request, but the Board of Immigration Appeals reversed, holding that only people who currently hold asylum status can adjust. In Velasquez Arreaga's case, the immigration judge and Board reached the same conclusion. The Second Circuit denied both men's appeals, agreeing that terminated asylum status forecloses eligibility, and later denied rehearing. The men then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether a law letting immigrants who were "granted asylum" become lawful permanent residents still covers people whose asylum was later taken away, or whether only people who currently hold asylum status can use that path. The petitioners say the wording covers anyone ever granted asylum; the government and the lower court say it requires current asylum status.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition on June 29, 2026. The case is now in the merits stage: briefing deadlines have been extended, with petitioners' brief due August 24, 2026 and the government's due October 2, 2026. The case has not yet been argued.

## What each outcome would mean

Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga, who lost in the lower court, asked the Court to take up the case. If the Court ultimately rules for them, people whose asylum was terminated could still qualify to become lawful permanent residents, and their cases would likely return to the immigration courts for further proceedings. If the Court rules for the government, the Second Circuit's decision would stand, and people in this situation would remain ineligible for that path to permanent residence. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the question either way.
