## In brief

Two men whose asylum status was terminated after criminal convictions ask the Court to decide whether they can still seek green cards under a federal immigration statute.

## What happened

Tamer Wassily and Byron Velasquez Arreaga were each granted asylum years ago, then had that status terminated after criminal convictions. Both later sought to adjust to lawful permanent resident status under a federal law allowing adjustment for "any alien granted asylum." Immigration judges reached different results in their cases, and the Board of Immigration Appeals ultimately ruled that both men were ineligible because their asylum status was no longer in effect when they applied. The Second Circuit denied their petitions for review, holding that only noncitizens with current asylum status qualify, over a dissent. The Second Circuit's decision aligned with the Fourth Circuit but conflicted with the Fifth Circuit, which had read the statute to cover anyone ever granted asylum.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a noncitizen who was once granted asylum, but whose asylum was later taken away, can still qualify for a green card under the provision allowing adjustment of status for "any alien granted asylum," or whether that benefit is available only to people who currently hold asylum status.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed and argued; it was set for argument on November 30, 2026, and now awaits a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga, who petitioned the Court after losing below, are seeking a ruling that terminated asylum does not bar adjustment to permanent residence. If the Court rules for them, it would mean the government may still have discretion to grant green cards to noncitizens whose asylum was terminated, and their cases would likely return to the lower courts for further proceedings on that basis. If the Court rules for the government, it would uphold the Second Circuit's decision that termination makes a person categorically ineligible, leaving Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga without that path to permanent residence.
