## In brief

Two immigrants whose asylum status was terminated after criminal convictions ask the Court to decide whether they can still seek lawful permanent residence under a federal adjustment-of-status law.

## What happened

Tamer Wassily and Byron Velasquez Arreaga were each granted asylum years ago, then had that status terminated after criminal convictions. Both sought to adjust to lawful permanent resident status under a federal law allowing the government to adjust "the status of any alien granted asylum." Immigration judges and the Board of Immigration Appeals disagreed about whether termination of asylum status forecloses this path, ultimately ruling against both men. The Second Circuit consolidated their cases and held, over a dissent, that only noncitizens with current asylum status may adjust to permanent residence. The court denied their petitions for review, and the full Second Circuit declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a noncitizen who was once granted asylum, but whose asylum was later terminated, can still apply for lawful permanent residence under the relevant statute, or whether termination permanently forecloses that path. The Solicitor General's office, representing the government, has told the Court it agrees with petitioners that the courts of appeals are split on this question and that review is warranted, while still defending the Second Circuit's interpretation as correct on the merits.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, the case was briefed and argued, and it was fully briefed through a reply brief filed in early November 2026. The docket does not yet show a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga, who petitioned the Court after losing below, are asking the Court to reverse the Second Circuit's ruling that barred them from seeking permanent residence. If the Court affirms, the Second Circuit's rule stands: noncitizens whose asylum has been terminated remain categorically ineligible to adjust to permanent resident status, and Wassily and Velasquez Arreaga's removal proceedings would continue on that basis. If the Court reverses, former asylees like petitioners could still apply for permanent residence despite termination, with eligibility and final approval remaining subject to immigration officials' discretion. A ruling to vacate would send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
