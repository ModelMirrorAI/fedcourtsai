## Why below the anchor, but still on the reversal side

The strongest petitioner argument is structural, not the proposition that every past participle necessarily denotes a past event. Section 1159 expressly requires continuing refugee qualification, admissibility at adjustment, and, in the neighboring refugee provision, nontermination. Omitting comparable continuing-asylum language supports treating a prior asylum grant as the gateway, subject to the enumerated conditions. Petitioners develop this contrast at pp. 20–24, following their explanation that even someone without lawful asylum status retains a legal status capable of adjustment at pp. 19–20. The question presented and the briefs distinguish being a refugee from retaining an asylum grant. Termination therefore need not defeat the separately specified refugee requirement.

The petitioners' practical argument also has some weight: adjustment and termination can concern overlapping criminal conduct, yet the statute expressly preserves certain humanitarian waiver authority. The Wassily account in petitioners' pp. 6–8 describes an immigration judge finding continuing refugee qualification and granting a waiver and adjustment, followed by reversal on categorical eligibility grounds. I use that account as advocacy describing the record, not as my own finding that discretionary relief is warranted. Eligibility for consideration, not automatic permanent residence or restoration of asylum, is the attractive narrower holding.

The respondent's best answer is substantial. At pp. 12–19 it reads the entire status-to-status formulation together, connects it to the benefits of an alien granted asylum under Section 1158(c), and invokes the 1990 provision expressly addressing certain former asylees. Petitioners answer the 1990 point at pp. 33–35 as a targeted exception to continuing-refugee requirements, with an explanatory termination parenthetical. Neither account makes the other frivolous. The respondent also explains at pp. 24–25 why waiver authority retains work under its reading: the government can decline termination before considering adjustment. Thus the policy consequence does not itself establish statutory contradiction.

I checked the general precedent **Henson v. Santander Consumer USA Inc., 582 U.S. 79, 84–85 (2017)** through CourtListener, including its discussion of past participles. That opinion defeats a grammar-only argument for petitioners: contextual usage can describe a present condition. It does not decide this provision, whose express neighboring continuity requirements give petitioners a distinct argument. The government's contextual case and the uncertainty about persuading a fifth Justice move me appreciably below the approximately 0.698 pooled anchor, to 0.57. I expect ordinary text and structure, not immigration lenity, to carry a petitioner victory.
