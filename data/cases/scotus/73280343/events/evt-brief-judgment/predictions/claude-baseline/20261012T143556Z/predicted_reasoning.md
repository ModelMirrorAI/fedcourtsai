# Predicted outcome — Wassily v. Blanche, No. 25-842 (merits, briefed moment)

**Judgment: affirmed** (P(disturbed) = 0.42). The Court holds that 8 U.S.C. 1159(b)
lets the Secretary or Attorney General adjust only an alien who **currently holds**
asylum status, which is the Second and Fourth Circuits' rule. That abrogates the Fifth
Circuit's *Siwe v. Holder*.

**Vote: 5–4.** Majority: Roberts, Thomas, Alito, Kavanaugh, Barrett. Dissent: Sotomayor,
Kagan, Jackson, and Gorsuch. Gorsuch has repeatedly joined noncitizens on textualist
grounds and has written on immigration lenity. Barrett is the other plausible defector
to a past-participle reading. If she defects too, the judgment flips to a 5–4 reversal.
That is the main way this forecast goes wrong. A 6–3 affirmance (Gorsuch with the
majority) is roughly as likely as the 5–4 lineup named here.

**Ground.** The majority reads "the status of any alien granted asylum" as naming the
status being adjusted. Adjustment converts one current status into another, so the
alien must hold asylee status to have it adjusted. Two points carry the opinion:

- **Parallel usage.** 1158(c)(1) uses the same phrase, "alien granted asylum," for
  benefits that undisputedly end when asylum is terminated: protection from removal
  and work authorization.
- **The 1990 carve-out.** Congress let "certain former asylees" adjust "regardless of
  whether or not such asylum has been terminated" (8 U.S.C. 1159 note). That
  provision would be pointless if 1159(b) already covered former asylees.

The majority treats petitioners' structural argument as unpersuasive: Congress wrote
express continuing conditions in 1159(a) and in 1159(b)(3) and (b)(5). It answers the
1159(c) waiver argument by saying the Attorney General keeps that discretion by
choosing not to terminate asylum, or by deciding adjustment first. It rejects lenity
because 1159(b) is not a removal statute and is not grievously ambiguous.

**Breadth.** The rule is categorical. It reaches every terminated asylee, including
derivatives, and is not limited to terminations for particularly serious crimes.

**Dissent.** The dissent (likely Gorsuch or Jackson) would rely on "any" plus the past
participle "granted," on Congress's express continuing-status language elsewhere in
1159, on the waiver provision being hollowed out, and on lenity.

**Procedural exits.** A DIG is unlikely. The SG agreed the case is a suitable vehicle,
the question was the sole basis for the decision below, and Wassily's IJ had granted
adjustment on the merits. An equally divided Court is very unlikely, since no recusal
is apparent.

**Timing.** Argument is set for November 30, 2026, and the decision is expected between
March and June 2027.
