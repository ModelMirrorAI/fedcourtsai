# Predicted reasoning — Wassily v. Blanche, No. 25-842 (merits, grant moment)

## Judgment class

The Court will **reverse** the Second Circuit's judgment and hold that
noncitizens who were "granted asylum," but whose asylum was later terminated,
remain statutorily eligible for adjustment to LPR status under 8 U.S.C.
§ 1159(b). Reversal outright (with remand for the agency to exercise its
restored discretion) is more likely than vacatur: the question is a pure,
outcome-dispositive issue of statutory interpretation, and in Wassily's case
the IJ already found every other eligibility element satisfied and granted a
§ 1159(c) waiver, so nothing turns on unresolved subsidiary questions. A DIG
is very unlikely — the petition is a clean vehicle, jointly presenting two
cases decided in one opinion below, and the BIA denied Wassily's adjustment
*solely* on the categorical-ineligibility ground. No recusal is apparent, so
an equally divided affirmance is remote.

## Vote lineup

A **7–2 reversal**: Roberts, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett,
and Jackson in the majority; Thomas and Alito dissenting. The petitioners'
argument is the kind of intra-section structural textualism (three express
continuing-status requirements elsewhere in § 1159, none in (b)'s prefatory
clause) that has repeatedly assembled cross-ideological majorities in
immigration statutory cases (Niz-Chavez, Esquivel-Quintana), and it has
already persuaded five appellate judges (the unanimous Siwe panel, Judge
Harris in Cela, Judge Robinson below). Thomas and Alito are the most likely
to credit the government's "status signals a present condition" reading and
its reliance on the Immigration Act of 1990's statutory note. A 6–3 with
Roberts or Kavanaugh joining the dissent is the main alternative lineup; a
9–0 for petitioners is also live if the government's textual case collapses
at argument.

## Ground and breadth (the two semantic claims, in prose)

The majority's ground will be textual and structural, siding with the Fifth
Circuit's *Siwe* reading: "any alien granted asylum" is backward-looking —
"granted" describes a completed event, "any" is expansive — and Congress's
choice to write continuing-status requirements expressly three times within
§ 1159 (§ 1159(a)(1)(A) "has not been terminated"; (b)(3) "continues to be a
refugee"; (b)(5) admissible "at the time of examination for adjustment")
makes the absence of a continuing-asylum requirement in (b)'s prefatory
clause a purposeful omission. § 1159(b)(2)'s "after being granted asylum" —
indisputably a past event — supplies the identical-words presumption. I
expect the Court not to rest on the immigration rule of lenity (it will call
the text clear, post-Loper Bright) and not to defer to the DOJ regulation,
though both may appear as confirmation. The holding will be categorical:
termination, whatever its ground, does not strip § 1159(b) eligibility — the
Court will point to § 1159(b)(3)'s continues-to-be-a-refugee requirement and
the Attorney General's ultimate discretion ("may adjust") as the safeguards
Congress actually enacted. A concurrence (plausibly Gorsuch or Jackson) may
write separately on lenity or on the 1990 statutory note; a Thomas or Alito
dissent will press the "status"/"adjust" present-condition reading and the
§ 1158(c) neighboring-usage argument that carried Cela and the decision
below.

## Timing (unscored, but it disciplines the rest)

Merits briefing was extended on July 20, 2026: petitioners' brief due

against a statutory case of moderate difficulty, most likely in the

## What would move this forecast

The government's merits brief could narrow the fight (e.g., concede
eligibility but contest the (b)(3) refugee requirement's application), which
would raise the vacatur share relative to outright reversal. A change in the
Court's composition before argument would change the lineup arithmetic but
not the direction.
