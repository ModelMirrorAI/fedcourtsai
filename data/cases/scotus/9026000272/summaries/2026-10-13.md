The federal government asks the Court to review a ruling that blocked part of a military policy disqualifying people with gender dysphoria from service.

## What happened

Several transgender individuals, including current servicemembers and people seeking to join the military, sued over a Department of War policy that generally disqualifies people with gender dysphoria, a history of the condition, or related medical treatment from military service. A district court blocked the policy nationwide. A divided panel of the D.C. Circuit narrowed that order: it kept the block in place for people already serving but let the policy take effect for applicants seeking to join. The majority concluded the challengers were likely to win their claim that the policy violates constitutional equal-protection guarantees; a dissenting judge argued the policy deserved deference as a military judgment. The federal government now asks the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether this military policy, which screens out people with gender dysphoria or a history of related treatment, violates constitutional guarantees that the government treat people equally under the law.

## Where it stands

The federal government filed its petition in August 2026. The respondents' deadline to file a response was extended to November 2, 2026, and amicus briefs supporting the government's petition have been filed. The Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government is asking the Court to hear the case and reverse the part of the lower court ruling that went against it. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the policy is constitutional, without presuming either side correct beforehand. If the Court denies the petition, the D.C. Circuit's decision stays in place: the policy would remain blocked for servicemembers currently in the military while applying to new applicants, and the underlying lawsuit would continue in the lower courts without the Supreme Court having resolved the constitutional question.
