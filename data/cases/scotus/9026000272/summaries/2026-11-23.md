## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to overturn a lower-court order that blocked it from discharging 28 transgender servicemembers while their legal challenge to a military service policy proceeds.

## What happened

In 2025 the Department of War adopted a policy, following an executive order, that generally disqualifies from military service anyone with gender dysphoria, a history of it, or related medical treatment. Nicolas Talbott and other transgender servicemembers and applicants sued, arguing the policy violates constitutional equal-protection guarantees. A district court blocked the policy nationwide. A divided appeals panel narrowed that order, letting the policy apply to new applicants but keeping it blocked for 28 named servicemembers already serving, finding the policy likely reflected bias against transgender people rather than a legitimate military reason. The government appealed that partial ruling to the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the military's policy disqualifying people with gender dysphoria from service violates the Constitution's equal-protection guarantee, or whether courts must defer to the military's judgment that the policy serves legitimate readiness, cohesion, and cost concerns rather than reflecting bias against transgender people.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply from the government, and was distributed for the Court's conference on December 4, 2026. No action on the petition has been recorded since.

## What each outcome would mean

The government is asking the Court to reverse the appeals court's decision, which favored Talbott and the other servicemembers. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the policy is constitutional, deciding the issue for neither side in advance. If it denies the petition, the appeals court's order stays in place, meaning the 28 named servicemembers could not be discharged while their case continues in the lower courts, though the policy could still apply to new applicants. Either way, the underlying lawsuit, including a scheduled trial, would continue unless the Court's action changes that.
