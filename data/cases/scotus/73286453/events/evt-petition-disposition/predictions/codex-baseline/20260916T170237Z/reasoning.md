## Case-specific adjustment

The strongest reasons to increase the baseline are the unusually serious alleged conduct, a divided published appellate decision, and substantial tension with Browder and the Fourth Circuit decision identified in the QP. The complaint alleges that an intoxicated deputy drove home at about 70 mph in a 25-mph zone after dark without lights, while not responding to an emergency, causing a fatal collision. These are allegations accepted at this procedural stage, not trial findings. The petition's arguments appear at pp. 9–25; Judge Jordan's dissent begins at petition appendix 17a.

Browder is a meaningful comparator rather than just a favorable citation. Its opinion discusses reckless indifference, time for deliberation, and the reduced specificity of precedent required for obviously egregious misconduct. I verified those points in CourtListener's copy of Browder, 787 F.3d 1076 (10th Cir. 2015), opinion 2804970, in the passages surrounding the clearly-established-law discussion. I do not adopt the opposition's suggestion that Browder necessarily required an intent to cause the particular collision. Lewis, 523 U.S. 833, 854 n.13 (1998), does contain the vehicle-misuse distinction invoked by petitioner; I verified that footnote through opinion 118214. Its applicability to these particular allegations and to clearly established law remains contested.

Three features substantially offset that upward adjustment. First, the panel assumed a constitutional violation rather than deciding that this driving was constitutionally permissible; it resolved the distinct notice inquiry. Its discussion expressly recognizes Browder but distinguishes the Tenth Circuit's earlier precedent (petition appendix 9a–12a). That is not a clean holding that reckless non-emergency driving can never violate due process.

Second, preservation is a concrete obstacle, not merely the respondent's assertion. The majority states that Hughes proceeded under obvious clarity rather than the materially-similar-precedent or broader-clearly-established-principle routes (appendix 10a). The opposition's pp. 3–11 and reproduced appellate-brief passages argue that the petition repackages Lewis footnote 13 into a theory not presented below. The appellate brief did invoke Lewis and intentional misuse, so I do **not** treat forfeiture as conclusively established. But the dispute itself lowers vehicle quality, especially without the reply text.

Third, the panel did not resolve whether the deputy acted under color of state law, and Chief Judge Pryor's concurrence specifically doubts that element (appendix 15a–16a). Even a victory on qualified immunity would leave that distinct question. The seriousness of the alleged wrong therefore does not by itself establish an attractive Supreme Court vehicle. These considerations leave a modest net increase from 5.12% to 7%, not a large split-driven adjustment.
