## In brief

Gun owners and gun-rights groups ask the Court to overturn a ruling that upheld Connecticut's ban on certain semiautomatic rifles and related firearms.

## What happened

Eddie Grant, Jr. and other individuals and gun-rights organizations sued Connecticut officials, arguing that the state's restrictions on so-called assault weapons, including AR-15 style rifles, violate the Second Amendment. A federal district court denied their request for a preliminary injunction blocking the law, finding they were unlikely to succeed on the merits. The Second Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that the restrictions fit within a historical tradition of regulating unusually dangerous weapons, and that the challengers also failed to show the other factors needed for a preliminary injunction. The gun owners then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution protects the right to possess semiautomatic rifles that are widely owned for lawful purposes, including the AR-15, against a state ban. The case turns on how courts should decide whether a firearm can be banned as unusually dangerous versus protected because it is commonly owned.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief opposing review and a reply, and the Court has consolidated this case with a related petition, granted review, and scheduled oral argument; amicus briefs have been filed and the record has been received from the lower courts.

## What each outcome would mean

Grant and the other challengers are asking the Court to reverse the Second Circuit's decision upholding Connecticut's law. If the Court rules for Grant, it would find the restrictions unconstitutional, a result favoring broader gun rights and likely affecting similar laws in other states. If the Court rules for the Connecticut officials, Connecticut's ban on these firearms would stand as constitutional. If the Court vacates the decision, it would send the case back to the lower courts without resolving who is right.
