## In brief

The Court agreed to hear a challenge to Connecticut's ban on AR-15-style rifles and similar firearms, consolidated with a related case.

## What happened

Gun owners and gun-rights groups sued Connecticut officials, arguing the state's ban on so-called assault weapons violates the right to keep and bear arms. A federal district court refused to block the law while the case proceeded, and the Second Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding the challengers had not shown they would likely win and that the law fits within a historical tradition of restricting unusually dangerous weapons. The challengers then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling, while Connecticut officials urged the Court to leave it in place.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether the right to keep and bear arms protects semiautomatic rifles, including the AR-15, that are widely owned for lawful purposes, or whether states may ban them as unusually dangerous weapons despite their popularity.

## Where it stands

After many conferences, the Court granted the petition, consolidating this case with a related one for one hour of argument; argument was later set and held, and the case now awaits a decision following briefing, amicus filings, and receipt of the lower court record.

## What each outcome would mean

Eddie Grant and the other gun owners and groups asked the Court to take up the case after losing below. If the Court ultimately rules for Grant, Connecticut's restrictions on these firearms could be struck down or narrowed. If it rules for the Connecticut officials, the law would stand as the lower courts upheld it. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the constitutional question itself, leaving the issue for further proceedings.
