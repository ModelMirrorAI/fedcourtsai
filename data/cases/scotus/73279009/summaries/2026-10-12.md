## In brief

The Court granted review in a related case to decide whether states may ban semiautomatic rifles like the AR-15, consolidating this case with it for argument.

## What happened

Connecticut restricts possession of certain semiautomatic firearms it calls "assault weapons," including AR-15 style rifles, a law strengthened after the Sandy Hook school shooting. Eddie Grant, Jr. and other individuals and gun-rights groups sued, seeking a preliminary court order blocking enforcement while their challenge proceeds, arguing the ban violates their right to keep and bear arms. A federal district court refused to block the law, and the Second Circuit Court of Appeals agreed, finding the restrictions consistent with a historical tradition of regulating unusually dangerous weapons. Grant and the others then asked the Supreme Court to take up the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution protects a right to possess semiautomatic rifles that are commonly owned for lawful purposes, including the AR-15, meaning whether a state can ban such widely owned firearms consistent with the right to keep and bear arms.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition, consolidated this case with a related one for one hour of argument, and set argument for December 2, 2026. Briefing and other filings are continuing, and the case awaits argument and a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Grant and the other challengers asked the Court to take up the case after losing below, where Connecticut's law was left in place. Because the Court granted review, the case will now be argued and decided on the merits. If the Court affirms, Connecticut's restrictions would stand and the challengers would not get the order blocking enforcement that they sought. If the Court reverses, it would side with the challengers, calling the ban's constitutionality into question. If the Court vacates, it would send the case back to the lower courts without deciding who is right.
