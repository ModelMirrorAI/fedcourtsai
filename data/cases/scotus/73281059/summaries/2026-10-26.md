## In brief

Arizona legislative leaders ask the Court to overturn a ruling that struck down state voter-registration and mail-voting restrictions and revived a discrimination claim against a voter-roll law.

## What happened

After the 2020 election, Arizona passed laws requiring documentary proof of citizenship and residence on its state voter-registration form, barring mail voting for registrants lacking such proof, and directing officials to check databases and cancel registrations of suspected noncitizens. Civil rights groups, the Democratic National Committee, and the United States sued, arguing the laws violated federal voter-registration law, a prior consent decree, and civil rights law. A federal district court struck down several provisions after a trial and rejected a claim that one law was enacted with discriminatory intent. The Ninth Circuit largely affirmed, but also revived the discriminatory-intent claim and sent it back for further review. Arizona Senate President Petersen and House Speaker Montenegro, who intervened to defend the laws, now ask the Supreme Court to reverse.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether federal voter-registration law, or an older consent decree, stops Arizona from requiring documentary proof of citizenship and residence on its state registration form; whether that same federal law stops Arizona from barring mail voting by registrants who have not supplied such proof; and whether the lower court wrongly disturbed the trial court's finding that a related voter-roll law was not enacted with discriminatory intent.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, after full briefing including an opposition from the United States and other respondents and a reply from the petitioners; the docket shows no action recorded since the second conference. Related petitions raising overlapping questions are pending at the same time.

## What each outcome would mean

Petersen and Montenegro, who lost below, are asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide the questions itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands: the voter-registration and mail-voting restrictions remain blocked, and the discrimination claim returns to the district court for further proceedings, a result favoring the groups and officials who challenged the laws. The United States has urged the Court to deny this petition while supporting review of a related one.
