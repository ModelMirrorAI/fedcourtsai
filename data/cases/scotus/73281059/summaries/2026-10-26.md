## In brief

Arizona legislative leaders ask the Court to overturn a ruling striking down state voter-registration requirements and faulting a voter-roll law as potentially discriminatory.

## What happened

Arizona passed laws requiring voters to show documentary proof of citizenship and residency on the state registration form, barring mail voting for registrants who lack that proof, and directing county officials to check databases and cancel registrations of suspected noncitizens. Civil rights groups, the Democratic National Committee, and the United States sued, arguing the laws conflict with federal voter-registration law and an existing court settlement. A federal district court struck down several provisions after trial; the Ninth Circuit mostly affirmed, and also sent back for further review a finding that one law was not enacted with discriminatory intent. Arizona's Senate President and House Speaker, who had intervened to defend the laws, now ask the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether federal voter-registration law or an existing court settlement stops Arizona from requiring documentary proof of citizenship and residency on its state voter-registration form, whether that same federal law stops Arizona from denying mail voting to registrants who have not provided such proof, and whether the lower court wrongly second-guessed the trial court's finding that a related voter-roll law was not motivated by discriminatory intent.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including responses from the federal government and other parties and a reply from the petitioners, and was distributed for the Court's conferences in June 2026; merits briefing has since begun, with petitioners filing their opening brief in August 2026. The docket does not yet show whether the Court has granted or denied review.

## What each outcome would mean

Petersen and Montenegro, the Arizona legislative leaders, are asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit and uphold the challenged laws. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions; a decision to reverse would let Arizona enforce its documentary proof-of-citizenship and residency requirements and its mail-voting restriction, while a decision to affirm would leave those provisions blocked and send the discriminatory-intent question back for further proceedings. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, keeping the challenged provisions enjoined and requiring the lower court to reexamine whether the voter-roll law was discriminatory.
