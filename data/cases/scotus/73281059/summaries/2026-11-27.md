## In brief

Arizona legislative leaders ask the Court to review a ruling that struck down state voter-registration and mail-voting requirements and revived a discrimination claim against another voting law.

## What happened

After the 2020 election, Arizona passed laws requiring documentary proof of citizenship and residency on its state voter-registration form, rejecting forms lacking such proof, barring mail voting by people who registered without proof of citizenship, and requiring officials to check databases and cancel registrations of suspected noncitizens. Several groups, the Democratic National Committee, Arizona's Democratic Party, and the United States sued, arguing the laws violated federal voting law and the Constitution. A federal district court struck down several provisions after a trial. The Ninth Circuit largely affirmed, and also revived a claim that one law was enacted with discriminatory intent, over dissent from several judges. The Arizona Senate President and House Speaker, who had intervened to defend the laws, now ask the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal voter-registration law, or an earlier court-approved settlement, bars Arizona from requiring documentary proof of citizenship and residency on its own state registration form for people registering for federal elections. It is also asked whether that federal law stops Arizona from barring mail voting by people who registered without providing such proof, and whether the appeals court wrongly second-guessed the trial court's finding that a related voter-list law was not passed with discriminatory intent.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including an amicus brief and responses from the federal government and other respondents opposing review, and the petitioners have filed a reply. The case was distributed for the Court's conference on June 18, 2026, and again for the conference of June 25, 2026; the docket does not yet show an outcome from either conference.

## What each outcome would mean

The Arizona legislative leaders are asking the Court to overturn the Ninth Circuit's rulings against the state's voting laws. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the legal questions; a grant alone would not determine who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, leaving in place the rulings against the proof-of-citizenship, proof-of-residency, and mail-voting restrictions, and allowing the discriminatory-intent claim to proceed in the lower court. A denial would not mean the Supreme Court agrees with the Ninth Circuit, only that the decision remains undisturbed.
