# Forecast of the Court's action

The Court will most likely deny the requested stay, leaving the limited preliminary injunction against the Secretary's April Directive in place while the First Circuit considers the appeal. The denial will probably be a short order rather than a substantive opinion. This is a close forecast: the probability of an unqualified grant is 0.41, not a claim that emergency relief is implausible. A mixed grant-and-denial order belongs on the ungranted side of this event's prescribed resolution rule.

The application will probably be referred by Justice Jackson to the full Court (0.85). The frozen record shows submission to her and her request for a response, but no referral yet. That requested response has already been filed. There will be no new initial response request; the response-requested-increment number is 0.0 and that claim is already vacuous from this vantage. The record counts one amicus submission, by the Republican National Committee. No additional amicus entry is the most likely trajectory; the probability that the count rises above one, counting submissions as well as Latin-form entries without double counting their lifecycle, is 0.24.

Expect disposition within several days of the October 19 response, most likely during October 21–26, 2026, and before the November 3 election. An administrative stay is possible but is not the substantive relief this forecast prices. The modal path is referral followed by denial without an intervening administrative stay.

The reason for leaving the injunction undisturbed will be the unusually weak showing for emergency intervention in this particular record: HB 323 itself remains operative; the injunction concerns only the categorical exclusion of student IDs from existing discretionary identity-verification safety valves; and revised guidance has already been disseminated. The opposition identifies federal constitutional grounds and findings about implementation that the application does not directly answer. On the modal path, these features prevent the State's election-proximity argument from establishing entitlement to a stay. An unexplained order would not itself confirm this predicted legal rationale.

If the Court instead grants relief, the likely ground is that the October injunction altered an established, months-old election-administration practice too near the general election, despite leaving the statute formally intact. That would be a provisional election-stability ruling, not a final holding that the April Directive complies with the First, Fourteenth, or Twenty-Sixth Amendments. The application will not produce a plenary merits judgment on those constitutional questions at this stage.
