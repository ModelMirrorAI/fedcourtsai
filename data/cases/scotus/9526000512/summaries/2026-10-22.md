## In brief

New Hampshire election officials ask the Supreme Court to pause a lower court order requiring revised guidance on student ID use before the November election.

## What happened

New Hampshire's HB 323 removed student IDs from the list of identification a ballot clerk must automatically accept. The Secretary of State then issued guidance telling election officials that student IDs could not be used for any purpose, including under separate "safety valve" provisions letting officials consider other evidence of identity. The Coalition for Open Democracy sued, arguing this guidance went beyond the statute and violated constitutional voting rights. A federal district court denied relief before the primary but, on October 2, partially granted an injunction against the guidance for the general election, ordering revised guidance. The First Circuit twice declined to stay that injunction, including a 2-1 vote on October 14, with one judge dissenting.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to pause the district court's order while the case continues, on the grounds that courts should generally avoid changing election rules close to an election, and that the lower court's reasoning about what state law requires was mistaken.

## Where it stands

The application for a stay was submitted October 15 to Justice Jackson, who requested a response. Both sides have now filed briefs, including a response in opposition and a reply, and the application remains pending.

## What each outcome would mean

New Hampshire's Secretary of State and Attorney General, as applicants, are asking the Court to pause the injunction. Granting the stay would let the Secretary's original guidance excluding student IDs remain in effect for the general election while the appeal continues. Denying it would leave in place the district court's order requiring the revised guidance, which the Coalition says already matches what the Secretary has been telling voters since October 2. Either way, the underlying lawsuit continues in the lower courts, and the Court is not deciding who is ultimately right.
