## In brief

The Court declined to hear a prisoner's challenge to a federal law capping attorney's fees in his successful civil rights lawsuit against prison guards.

## What happened

Michael Webb, an incarcerated man, sued Great Meadow Correctional Facility officers for beating him during an intake interview. A jury awarded him $15,000. His appointed lawyers then sought $206,977.50 in fees under a federal civil rights fee statute. The district court, citing a federal law limiting fees in prisoner lawsuits, capped the award at $22,500 — 150% of the judgment — following binding Second Circuit precedent. The Second Circuit affirmed, and the full court denied rehearing. Webb asked the Supreme Court to take up the fee-cap question.

## What the Court is being asked

The case turns on how to read a law limiting attorney's fees for prisoners who win civil rights cases. Webb's petition argues that the statute, properly read, only guarantees payment for fees up to 150% of the judgment and says nothing about fees above that amount, while multiple appeals courts have read it as an outright ceiling barring any fee award beyond 150% of the judgment. The Court is asked whether that ceiling reading is correct.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, after the respondents waived their right to respond. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Webb, the prisoner, asked the Court to overturn the Second Circuit's ruling that capped his attorney's fees. The denial leaves that ruling in place: Webb's lawyers remain limited to $22,500 in fees rather than the larger amount they sought, and the Second Circuit's interpretation of the fee-cap law continues to apply in that circuit. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed the lower court was correct; it simply leaves that decision standing without the Court deciding the question itself.
