I reduce the approximately 5.12% anchor to **0.5% for any grant**, including GVR and summary reversal, for these case-specific reasons:

1. **No demonstrated split.** The petition expressly acknowledges that its claimed inconsistency is not directly conflicting circuit holdings on identical facts (petition pp. 14–15). It instead seeks development of a new framework reconciling recusal and immunity. That can present an important issue, but it is weaker cert evidence than a square, developed conflict.
2. **The requested rule is not already established by the authorities invoked.** The petition relies on Williams and Caperton for judicial disqualification and asks the Court to carry those principles into immunity from a separate civil action (questions presented; petition pp. 9–15, 22–24). I checked the general immunity framework in the primary opinion in Mireles v. Waco, 502 U.S. 9, 11–13 (1991), through CourtListener. It distinguishes nonjudicial conduct and conduct in the absence of all jurisdiction from judicial acts alleged to be malicious or erroneous. My inference is that the challenged dispositive ruling is facially adjudicative; allegations about motive and the timing of service do not themselves establish the existing exceptions. I do not decide whether the alleged bias actually occurred.
3. **Preservation is a serious vehicle obstacle.** The opposition states that the October 28, 2024 complaint preceded the January 2025 service and subsequent ruling, that no amendment added the post-filing conduct, and that the post-service conflict theory was never presented below (BIO pp. 7–10). The petition's own chronology confirms the sequence, though not the opposition's complete preservation account (petition pp. 5–8). I treat the claimed nonpreservation as a specific and plausible adversarial objection, not a finding independently verified against the complaint or appellate record.
4. **Other grounds obstruct the requested relief.** The petition says the lower courts relied solely on judicial immunity (petition pp. 7–8). The BIO instead describes official-capacity sovereign immunity, limits on prospective relief and section 1983 injunctions, and futility of individual-capacity amendment (BIO pp. 5–7). Its pinpoint references to the decisions make these substantial vehicle concerns, but without the opinions I cannot independently settle the parties' conflicting descriptions. A new damages-immunity exception would not necessarily cure the other grounds.
5. **The factual presentation is diffuse.** Besides its post-service theory, the petition advances administrative-function, financial-conflict, and disciplinary-remedy arguments (petition pp. 16–28). These increase the potential stakes but do not create a clean preserved holding on the central question. The opposition describes both lower-court decisions as unpublished (BIO p. 1). Nothing in the supplied procedural trajectory independently offsets these weaknesses.

The strongest argument for a higher number is that judicial accountability and an impartial tribunal are consequential federal concerns, and the alleged three-day interval after service gives the petition a concrete factual focus. The functional argument also prevents treating every alleged act as immune simply because a judge performed it. I retain a small nonzero grant probability for unexpected interest in those questions or a narrower issue, while placing most weight on the preservation and remedy obstacles. The 0.5% is a judgment, not a fitted estimate for an observed judicial-immunity cohort.
