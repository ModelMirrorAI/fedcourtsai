## In brief

Arizona asks the Court to review a ruling that let an advocacy group sue over a voter-roll law without identifying any member it actually harmed.

## What happened

Arizona passed laws in 2022 to check voter rolls for noncitizens and to require proof of citizenship on its state registration form. Promise Arizona and another group sued, claiming the voter-roll law was enacted with discriminatory intent. After a trial, a federal district court rejected that claim. The Ninth Circuit vacated that finding and sent it back for further review, ruling first that Promise Arizona could sue on behalf of its members even though it had not identified any member actually harmed by the law, and second that the district court had not properly weighed the evidence of discriminatory intent. Judge Bumatay dissented, and eleven judges dissented from rehearing the case en banc.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether an organization can sue in federal court over a law by claiming only that some unidentified members might be affected, without naming anyone actually harmed, and whether the appeals court overstepped its role by reweighing the trial court's factual findings instead of checking them only for clear mistakes.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, after the United States, as respondent, filed a brief opposing it while supporting a related petition. Arizona later filed a supplemental brief citing a new Ninth Circuit ruling it says undercuts the panel's standing decision below, and the Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Arizona is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse the Ninth Circuit's rulings on standing and on how the evidence was reviewed. If the Court denies the petition, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands, and the case returns to the district court for further proceedings on whether the voter-roll law was discriminatory. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide these questions itself; a grant does not decide who is right. Arizona has also asked the Court, as an alternative, to grant the petition, vacate the Ninth Circuit's decision, and send the case back for reconsideration in light of a newer Ninth Circuit ruling, without the Court itself deciding the questions.
