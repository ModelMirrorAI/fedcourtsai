## In brief

Arizona asks the Court to review a Ninth Circuit ruling that revived a claim that an Arizona voter-list law was enacted with discriminatory intent.

## What happened

Arizona passed two 2022 laws aimed at confirming voters' citizenship, including one directing officials to check voter rolls against databases and cancel registrations of suspected noncitizens who cannot show proof of citizenship. Two nonprofit organizations sued, claiming that law was enacted with discriminatory purpose. After a trial, a federal district court found no discriminatory purpose. A Ninth Circuit panel vacated that finding, holding one organization had standing to sue even though it could not identify any specific member harmed, and ruling the lower court had not properly weighed the evidence as a whole. Judge Bumatay dissented, and eleven judges dissented from rehearing the case en banc. Arizona and its attorney general then petitioned the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

Arizona raises two questions. First, whether an organization may sue over a law by claiming some unidentified members might someday be harmed by it, without identifying any member actually affected. Second, whether the appeals court overstepped its role by reassessing the evidence itself, rather than simply checking whether the trial court's finding of no discriminatory intent was clearly wrong.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, after full briefing including the federal government's brief recommending the Court deny this petition while granting a related one, and the petitioners' reply. Arizona later submitted a supplemental brief. The docket does not show that the Court has acted on the petition since the most recent conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Arizona and its attorney general, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case fully and decide the standing and evidence-review questions itself, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's decision reviving the discrimination claim stands, and the case would continue in the lower courts, with the district court reconsidering the evidence on remand.
