# Rationale

I assign a 0.82 probability that the judgment below is disturbed and predict a reversal. The committed merits baseline for an OT2025 grant pools the ten strictly prior grant Terms. The available OT2017–OT2024 rows contain 359 disturbed judgments among 515 parsed judgments, or 69.7%; OT2015 and OT2016 have no parsed rows. Coverage is 515 of 557 granted cases in those available rows (92.5%), with numeric exclusion counts, so the pool clears the 30-case floor and is quotable. I adjusted upward by about twelve percentage points from that baseline.

The principal upward adjustment is case selection and fit between the granted question and the record. The Court granted a single question asking whether established First Amendment chilling-effect doctrine is displaced by land-use finality. The record supplies unusually concrete facts for that question: the law director sent a cease-and-desist letter warning of citations; Grand cancelled the next prayer gathering; the mayor later reiterated that a house of worship could not operate without a permit; police were directed to watch for violations; and a prosecutor and inspector followed up. Those facts allow a narrow ruling under ordinary pre-enforcement principles without deciding whether the zoning ordinance is valid. The grant after repeated conference distributions and the current Court's strong protection of religious exercise reinforce the disturbance forecast, although cert-stage distribution history is not itself a merits baseline.

The downward adjustment reflects serious respondent arguments. Grand withdrew the special-use application before any responsible zoning body acted, stated that he did not intend to operate a "house of worship" as the ordinance defines it, apparently continued to convene at least some prayer, received no citation, and could still seek a Board of Zoning Appeals interpretation. The Sixth Circuit also relied independently on lack of hardship. The opposition identifies forfeiture problems with the categorical RLUIPA and facial theories, and the alleged circuit-conflict cases involved more targeted ordinances. These points create meaningful affirmance, narrow-vacatur, and DIG risk.

I chose `reversed` rather than `vacated` because the forecasted holding rejects the legal rule used to affirm dismissal and resembles a conventional reversal followed by remand for claim-specific proceedings. Vacatur is the principal exact-judgment alternative if the Court instead directs the Sixth Circuit to apply a clarified standard without itself finding the chilling injury sufficient.

The lineup is less certain than the binary result, but the granted distinction is not inherently ideological. *Steffel* and *Susan B. Anthony List* supply conventional pre-enforcement principles, while a narrow opinion can preserve land-use finality whenever the asserted injury depends on a definitive property-use decision. That route can attract all nine Justices without agreement on the ordinance's validity or on broad RLUIPA doctrine. A separate vote is most likely to concern whether these particular government acts created an objectively credible threat. I therefore give the overall forecast 0.67 confidence despite the 0.82 disturbance probability.

This is a forward-mode forecast from the grant moment. The snapshot postdates the grant but precedes merits briefing: petitioner's merits brief was due August 31, 2026 and respondents' brief October 16, 2026. I therefore did not have merits briefs or oral argument. The provisioned petition was a 44-page scan with `empty_text: true`; I used the extractable brief in opposition, the snapshot, the official Question Presented sheet, and the pre-grant Sixth Circuit opinion. The opposition is respondent-framed, so the forecast may understate petitioner arguments that will be developed at merits.
