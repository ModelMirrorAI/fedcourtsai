## What moves the forecast

Petitioner's merits brief, printed pp. 19–24 and the opening of its argument, describes claims for damages arising from completed intimidation, compelled permitting expenses, and alleged discriminatory treatment. It argues that no later permit could undo those injuries. That gives the Court a reason to reject a rule treating all land-use claims as premature until a zoning board issues a final decision. These are petitioner's allegations and characterizations, not findings of liability.

The United States expressly supports petitioner. Its merits brief, printed pp. 10–13, 18–25, argues for ordinary Article III analysis: finality matters when the injury depends on the eventual land-use decision, not when a challenged government act has already injured the plaintiff independently of that decision. It treats retrospective claims as ripe and allows unresolved prospective-relief questions to be remanded. I follow that narrower position more closely than petitioner's categorical proposal that finality belongs only to takings. It supplies a route to broad agreement without deciding substantive constitutional liability.

Respondents' merits brief, printed pp. 25–33 and 34–43, is the main reason not to forecast a near-certain reversal. Grand sold the property and moved away. Respondents also invoke their stipulation permitting small gatherings, a change in mayor, and failure to advance damages on appeal. Crucially, respondents acknowledge that the complaint requested compensatory damages but contest whether the damages theory remained part of the appellate case. A pleaded demand is evidence against complete mootness, not proof that preservation is settled. Their further contention that the alleged threat concerned a larger house-of-assembly use, not small prayer groups, also makes objective injury and claim characterization contested. I regard a surviving retrospective claim as more likely than complete mootness, but not assured. The unresolved replies could change that assessment.

Vacatur rather than outright reversal is my modal label because a narrower opinion can reject an overly categorical rule while leaving claim preservation, the consequences of the move, and individualized injury analysis for remand. I do not predict an award of damages or a finding of religious discrimination.
