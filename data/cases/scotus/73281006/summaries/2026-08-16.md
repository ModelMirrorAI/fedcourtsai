## In brief

Daniel Grand asked the Court to revive his lawsuit over a city's response to his home prayer gatherings; the Court granted review of his case.

## What happened

Daniel Grand, an Orthodox Jew in University Heights, Ohio, proposed hosting small prayer gatherings at his home. After a neighbor complained, city officials sent Grand a cease-and-desist letter and later he applied for, then withdrew, a special use permit before the city's planning commission finished considering it. Grand sued the city and several officials, raising First Amendment, RLUIPA, and other claims. The district court ruled most of Grand's claims were not yet ready for court because there was no final decision on his permit application, and it rejected his remaining claims on the merits. The Sixth Circuit unanimously affirmed. Grand, representing himself, then asked the Supreme Court to review the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a rule requiring a final, completed local decision before a land-use dispute can go to court also applies when someone claims that government threats and warnings, made before any final decision, already deterred them from exercising their religious rights. Grand argues that this timing requirement should not block claims based on such chilling threats; the city argues the claims were correctly found premature and that no real conflict among appeals courts exists.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted the petition on June 30, 2026. The case is now being briefed on the merits, with deadlines extended for both sides' briefs, and awaits full briefing, argument, and decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Grand, the petitioner, asked the Court to take up the case after losing in the lower courts. If the Court eventually rules in Grand's favor, it would revive his claims and send the case back for further proceedings on the merits. If it rules for the city, the lower courts' decision rejecting his claims as premature or otherwise unsuccessful would stand. The Court could also vacate the lower ruling and send the case back without deciding who is right.
