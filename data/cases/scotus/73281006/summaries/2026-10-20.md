## In brief

Daniel Grand asked the Supreme Court to decide whether a land-use ripeness rule can block constitutional and religious-liberty claims over a city's threats against home prayer gatherings.

## What happened

Daniel Grand, an Orthodox Jew in University Heights, Ohio, invited neighbors to pray with him at his home. After a neighbor complained, the city sent a cease-and-desist letter and told Grand he needed a special-use permit for a "house of worship." Grand applied for the permit, faced a contentious public hearing, and then withdrew his application, saying the ordinance did not apply to his small gathering. He sued the city and several officials, raising First Amendment, equal protection, due process, and RLUIPA (a federal law protecting religious land use) claims, along with other claims. The district court dismissed most of these claims as not ripe for review because no local body had issued a final decision, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a rule developed for property-takings cases—requiring a final decision from local authorities before a claim can go forward—should also apply to claims that government threats chilled a person's religious exercise, or whether such claims become ripe as soon as the government's threats cause a concrete injury, without waiting for a final zoning ruling.

## Where it stands

The briefing schedule was later amended to allow additional filings, including a respondents' sur-reply addressing Grand's move out of University Heights, due by December 2, 2026. ## What each outcome would mean

Grand, who brought the lawsuit and is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling, asks the Court to hold that his claims were ripe without a final zoning decision. If the Court agrees with Grand, his claims would go back to the lower courts to be decided on their merits rather than dismissed as premature. If the Court instead sides with the city and upholds the lower court's approach, Grand's claims would remain dismissed under the finality requirement, though without prejudice, meaning he could potentially refile if the city later took further action against him. The Court could also vacate the lower court's decision and send the case back without resolving the question for either side.
