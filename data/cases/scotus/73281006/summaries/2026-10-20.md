## In brief

The Court will decide whether a land-use ripeness rule that normally applies to property-takings claims also blocks free-exercise and religious-land-use lawsuits before a final zoning decision.

## What happened

Daniel Grand, an Orthodox Jew living in University Heights, Ohio, invited friends to pray at his home. The City sent a cease-and-desist letter saying he needed a special-use permit for a "place of religious assembly," and the Mayor warned him not to host any religious gathering without one. Grand applied for the permit, faced a contentious hearing, and then withdrew his application, saying the ordinance did not apply to his small gatherings. He sued the City and several officials, raising First Amendment, equal protection, due process, and federal religious-land-use claims, along with other claims. The district court dismissed most of these claims as not yet ripe for review and ruled against Grand on the rest; the Sixth Circuit affirmed, holding Grand needed a final decision from the City's zoning board before suing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a rule developed for property-takings cases — requiring a final decision from local zoning authorities before a claim can go to court — should also apply to claims that government threats chilled someone's religious exercise, worship, and assembly, or whether those claims can proceed once a credible government threat has deterred protected activity, without waiting for a final zoning ruling.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review on June 30, 2026. The case has been fully briefed on the merits and was set for oral argument on December 9, 2026; the briefing schedule has since been amended to let the parties and the City address the effect of Grand having moved out of University Heights while the case was pending.

## What each outcome would mean

Grand, as petitioner, asked the Court to reverse the Sixth Circuit's ruling that his claims were not ripe. If the Court affirms, the Sixth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Grand's claims remain dismissed unless he obtains a final ruling from the City's zoning board or otherwise satisfies the finality requirement. If the Court reverses, it would hold that Grand's claims were ripe for review without a final zoning decision, and the case would return to the lower courts to proceed on the merits. If the Court vacates, it would set aside the Sixth Circuit's ruling and send the case back for further proceedings without deciding the ripeness question for either side.
