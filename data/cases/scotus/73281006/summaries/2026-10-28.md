## In brief

Daniel Grand asks the Court to revive his lawsuit claiming University Heights, Ohio, unlawfully chilled his religious exercise by threatening him over a home prayer group.

## What happened

Grand, an Orthodox Jew, emailed neighbors inviting them to pray at his University Heights home. The city sent a cease-and-desist letter telling him he needed a special-use permit to host a "place of religious assembly," and the mayor told him by phone that he could not gather for prayer without one. Grand applied for the permit, faced a contentious public hearing, and then withdrew his application, saying he did not intend to operate a "house of worship" as the zoning ordinance defined it. The city's mayor said afterward that the cease-and-desist order remained in effect. Grand sued the city and officials, raising First Amendment, equal protection, and federal land-use statute (RLUIPA) claims, among others. The district court and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that most of these claims were not ripe because Grand had withdrawn his permit application before any city body issued a final decision on whether the ordinance applied to his gatherings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a rule requiring a final decision from local zoning authorities before a property-related claim can go to court—a rule this Court developed for cases where property owners claim the government has taken their property without paying for it—also applies to block claims that government threats chilled someone's religious exercise, even though in other contexts a credible threat alone is enough to let a person sue right away.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on December 9, 2026. The briefing included a United States amicus brief supporting Grand, and the case now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Grand, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to reverse the Sixth Circuit's decision. If the Court rules for Grand, his claims would be sent back for consideration on their merits without his having to secure a final city decision first. If the Court rules for the city, the Sixth Circuit's dismissal of Grand's claims as premature would stand, though that dismissal was without prejudice, meaning he could potentially sue again if the city later enforced the ordinance against him. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the question either way.
