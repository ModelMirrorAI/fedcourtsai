## In brief

The Court heard a homeowner's case over city orders that stopped him from hosting prayer gatherings at home, and awaits a decision.

## What happened

Daniel Grand, an Orthodox Jew living in University Heights, Ohio, invited neighbors to pray with him at his home. The city issued a cease-and-desist letter telling him he needed a special-use permit to use his home as a "place of religious assembly," and the mayor reinforced that message by phone. Grand applied for the permit, faced a contentious public hearing, and then withdrew his application, saying he did not intend to operate a "house of worship" as the zoning law defined it. He sued the city and officials, raising First Amendment, equal protection, and federal land-use law claims. The district court and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals ruled that most of his claims were not ripe for review because no local body had ever issued a final decision applying the zoning ordinance to his proposed gatherings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a legal rule that normally requires property owners to get a final decision from local zoning authorities before suing over land-use disputes should also apply to claims that government action chilled someone's religious exercise, worship, or assembly. Grand argues that a credible government threat discouraging protected religious activity is itself a complete injury, regardless of whether a zoning process ever reaches a final decision. The city argues that without a final decision on whether the permit was actually required, a court cannot yet know whether the law caused him any legally recognized harm.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was fully briefed and argued on December 9, 2026. It now awaits a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Grand, the homeowner, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling that his claims were premature. If the Court rules for Grand, his claims could go forward in the lower courts, potentially allowing him to pursue damages and other relief for the city's actions without first obtaining a final permitting decision. If the Court rules for the city, the lower court's dismissal of Grand's claims as unripe would stand, meaning he could not pursue them unless he first obtained a final decision from local zoning authorities or showed further proceedings would be futile. The Court could also send the case back to the lower courts without deciding the dispute itself, leaving the ripeness question for further consideration there.
