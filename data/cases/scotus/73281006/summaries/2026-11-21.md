## In brief

A homeowner asked the Supreme Court to revive his lawsuit claiming a city's cease-and-desist order chilled his religious prayer gatherings; the Court heard the case.

## What happened

Daniel Grand, an Orthodox Jew living in University Heights, Ohio, invited friends to pray at his home. After a neighbor complained, the city issued a cease-and-desist letter and told Grand he needed a special-use permit for a "place of religious assembly." Grand applied, faced a contentious public hearing, and then withdrew his application, saying he did not intend to operate a house of worship. He sued the city and officials, raising First Amendment, equal-protection, and religious-land-use claims. The district court and the Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals dismissed most of these claims as not ripe, reasoning that Grand never obtained a final decision from the zoning board on whether the ordinance applied to him.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a doctrine normally used to decide when property-related compensation claims become ripe for court review should also apply to claims that government threats chilled someone's religious exercise. Grand argues that a credible government threat alone creates an immediate, separate legal injury, regardless of whether a zoning process concludes. The city responds that without a final decision on how the ordinance applies, there is no concrete dispute yet for a court to resolve.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted review, the case was briefed and argued, and it was set for oral argument on December 9, 2026. The docket shows the case fully briefed, including an amicus brief from the United States supporting Grand, with no decision yet recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

Grand, the homeowner, is asking the Court to reverse the lower court's decision, which had sided with the city of University Heights and its officials. If the Court affirms, the lower court's ruling stands, and Grand's claims remain dismissed as filed too early, though he could potentially pursue new claims if the city later enforces the ordinance against him. If the Court reverses, Grand's claims would be revived, allowing him to pursue his lawsuit over the alleged chilling of his religious practice without needing a final zoning ruling first. The Court could also vacate and send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
