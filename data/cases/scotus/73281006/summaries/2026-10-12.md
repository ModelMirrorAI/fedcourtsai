## In brief

The Court will decide whether a city must reach a final zoning decision before a homeowner can sue over religious-liberty burdens from a cease-and-desist order and related conduct.

## What happened

Daniel Grand, an Orthodox Jew living in University Heights, Ohio, invited neighbors to join him in prayer at his home. The City sent him a cease-and-desist letter saying he needed a special-use permit for a "place of religious assembly," and Grand applied for one. After a contentious public hearing, he withdrew his application, saying the ordinance did not apply to his small prayer gatherings. He then sued the City and officials, alleging constitutional and statutory violations connected to the cease-and-desist order, the permit process, and alleged surveillance and harassment. The district court dismissed most claims as unripe because no local body had issued a final decision, and the Sixth Circuit affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether a rule requiring property owners to obtain a final decision from local zoning authorities before suing over land-use disputes also applies to claims that a government's actions burdened religious exercise, free speech, and assembly, or whether those claims can proceed once a credible government threat has already chilled protected activity, without waiting for a final administrative ruling.

## Where it stands

The petition was granted on June 30, 2026. The case has been fully briefed on the merits, with oral argument scheduled for December 9, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Grand, who petitioned the Court, asks it to allow his claims to proceed without requiring a final zoning decision. If the Court rules in his favor, his case would return to the lower courts, where he could pursue his claims on the merits. If it rules for the City, the dismissal of his claims as unripe would stand, meaning he would need to obtain a final decision from local zoning authorities before suing, though he could refile if the City later enforces the ordinance against him. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
