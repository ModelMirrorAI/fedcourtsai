## In brief

A Navy officer convicted by court-martial of murdering his wife asks the Court to review whether excluding her statements about her mental health violated his right to present a defense.

## What happened

Craig Becker, a Navy lieutenant, was convicted by general court-martial of premeditated murder, battery, and conduct unbecoming an officer, in connection with his wife's death from a fall from their apartment window in Belgium. His defense at trial was that she died by suicide. The military judge excluded certain statements she had reportedly made about being "terrified" of losing her mental faculties. The Navy-Marine Corps Court of Criminal Appeals found that ruling, and a related evidentiary ruling, erroneous but affirmed the conviction anyway, concluding the excluded evidence lacked relevance or probative value. The Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces declined to review the case further.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether keeping out the wife's statements about her mental state, which Becker says supported his suicide defense, violated his constitutional right to present a complete defense at trial, even though the appeals court agreed the evidence had been wrongly excluded.

## Where it stands

Becker filed his petition for certiorari on October 16, 2026. The government's response is due November 20, 2026. The Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Becker, the petitioner, is asking the Court to take up his case and overturn the military appeals court's decision affirming his conviction. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether excluding the evidence violated his right to present a defense, deciding nothing yet about who prevails. If it denies the petition, the lower military courts' decisions stand, leaving Becker's conviction in place.
