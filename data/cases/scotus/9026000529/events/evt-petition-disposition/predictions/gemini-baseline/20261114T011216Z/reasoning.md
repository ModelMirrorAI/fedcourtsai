Three strong negative factors push `P(grant)` well below the `5.17%` `baseline` risk-set anchor:

1. **Solicitor General Waiver of Response and Zero Amicus Support (`5.17%` -> ~`1.8%`)**:
   The `5.17%` `baseline` risk-set figure pools all private-petitioner paid filings at first distribution, including cases where the respondent filed a brief in opposition on the first round, cases attracting cert-stage amici, and petitions held for pending merits cases. Here, Solicitor General D. John Sauer waived the United States' right to respond on November 5, 2026 — fifteen days ahead of the November 20 due date — and no amicus curiae brief has been filed. Under settled Court practice, the Court will not grant plenary review or GVR over a government waiver without first calling for a response (unless holding for a pending merits case, of which there are none here, confirmed both by `same-question.json` and by corpus queries of OT2025–OT2026 grants). Moreover, OSG does not waive early when it spots a plausible *Brady*/instructional/jurisdictional confession-of-error candidate or a pending merits overlap.

2. **Summary CAAF Denial of Review Over an Unpublished Intermediate Opinion (~`1.8%` -> ~`1.0%`)**:
   CAAF denied LT Becker's petition for grant of review in a one-sentence order on June 22, 2026 (No. 26-0131/NA, App. 1) and denied reconsideration on July 27, 2026 (App. 2), leaving only an unpublished, non-precedential opinion of the Navy-Marine Corps Court of Criminal Appeals (*United States v. Becker*, No. 201900342, App. 3–112). Even setting aside the petition cover's minor styling defect (captioning the writ to NMCCA while properly invoking 28 U.S.C. § 1259(3) over CAAF's denial in the jurisdictional statement), the absence of any published opinion from either military appellate court makes this a poor doctrinal vehicle.

3. **Purely Factbound M.R.E. 403 Ruling Foreclosed by *Holmes* and *Nevada v. Jackson* (~`1.0%` -> `0.8%`)**:
   - **No split alleged**: `petition.txt` (at 7–15) alleges no conflict among federal circuits, state courts of last resort, or CAAF. The four circuit decisions cited (*Begay*, *Turning Bear*, *Lankford*, and *Hurn*) apply the same general Sixth Amendment framework that CAAF itself applies (e.g., *United States v. Maebane*, 86 M.J. 173 (C.A.A.F. 2025)).
   - **Discretionary contextual balancing, not an arbitrary exclusionary rule**: At LT Becker's retrial for the premeditated murder of his wife in Mons, Belgium (drugging her with clonazepam and pushing her from a seventh-floor window), the defense sought to admit two October 3, 2015 text lines in Defense Exhibit H (*"I'm afraid that something could happen again. I'm just terrified"* and *"I think I'm going crazy and I get scared"*) to support a suicide theory. Crucially, the military judge *agreed* that the statements satisfied the state-of-mind hearsay exception under Mil. R. Evid. 803(3), but excluded them under Mil. R. Evid. 401/403 after reviewing the immediately preceding September 2015 messages (Appellate Exhibit 412), which showed Mrs. Bravo was terrified of *Petitioner's* violent anger over her extramarital affair rather than expressing suicidal ideation (App. 97–106). Taking those lines out of context would either mislead the court-martial panel or require admitting the surrounding domestic-abuse context.
   - **Squarely governed by Supreme Court precedent**: *Holmes v. South Carolina*, 547 U.S. 319, 326–27 (2006), expressly reaffirmed that ordinary Rule 403 exclusions based on unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury are constitutional unless arbitrary or disproportionate. And *Nevada v. Jackson*, 569 U.S. 505, 509 (2013) (per curiam), summarily reversed a circuit court for reading *Chambers* and *Holmes* to constitutionalize case-specific evidentiary exclusions. Petitioner also presented his non-homicide defense extensively through toxicology, pathology, and cross-examination, creating formidable harmless-error headwinds even if review were granted.

I retain a `0.008` (`0.8%`) tail probability — rather than pushing closer to zero — because (a) Petitioner is represented by experienced civilian military-justice appellate counsel alongside Navy JAG counsel in a life-sentence premeditated murder case, (b) expanded certiorari jurisdiction over unreviewed CAAF denials under 28 U.S.C. § 1259(3) is still relatively new, and (c) there is a small (~2.5–3%) chance a Justice calls for a response out of caution on a life-sentence suicide-defense record, after which OSG record review occasionally surfaces an independent ground for remand or merits interest.
