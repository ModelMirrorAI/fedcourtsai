## Question and vehicle

The petition asks whether excluding the decedent's statements about her mental health violated Becker's constitutional right to present a complete defense. Its strongest point is the asymmetry it alleges: the prosecution presented substantial testimony that the decedent was not suicidal, while the defense could not introduce her own statements expressing fear of losing her faculties. The trial judge confused hearsay exceptions and described prejudice to the trial process, language that warrants scrutiny. These are reasons to take the constitutional contention seriously, not findings that the petition's account is undisputed.

The appendix supplies an important qualification to the petition's framing. At App. 98-104, NMCCA recognizes the hearsay error but expressly conducts its own Rule 403 review, grounding exclusion in temporal remoteness and potential confusion rather than adopting the trial judge's rationale. App. 201-202 also records that the defense expert could discuss the decedent's mental-health history and had access to the messages. Thus this was not a categorical prohibition on presenting suicide as the defense. The petition seeks a different assessment of how important particular messages were relative to evidence already admitted.

Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 324-327, 330-331 (2006), verified through CourtListener opinion 145659, protects against arbitrary or disproportionate exclusion but also recognizes legitimate evidentiary balancing. Its invalid rule used the perceived strength of the prosecution's evidence to bar third-party-guilt evidence. The distinction between that categorical rule and NMCCA's individualized remoteness/confusion analysis weakens the claimed direct conflict. The petition's cited favorable circuit applications do not establish that another court would adopt an incompatible legal rule on this record. The existing suicide expert testimony also complicates any assertion that the entire defense was foreclosed. These observations address certworthiness; I am not forecasting approval of every ruling below.

No provisioned companion or identified intervening Supreme Court decision supplies a concrete hold/GVR rationale. CAAF's refusal to review is not itself treated as a jurisdictional bar, and I do not base this estimate on the pre-amendment limits on military certiorari review.
