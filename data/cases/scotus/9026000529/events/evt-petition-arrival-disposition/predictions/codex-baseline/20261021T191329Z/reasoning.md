## Anchor and adjustment

The petitioner is a private individual, not a federal petitioner merely because he is a Navy officer or the United States is respondent. The correct arrival anchor is therefore the sal-v4 `baseline` band's bracketed `reached` rate. I pooled every strictly prior Term displayed in the committed statpack, 2017–2025, excluding 2026. Using the exact `segments` fields in `metrics/statpack.json`, the executed calculation yields 644 weighted grant-family outcomes over 12,883 weighted resolved petitions, or 0.0499884 (approximately 5.0%). This is a denial-reweighted, class-specific arrival risk set, not a relist-zero terminal rate. The salience versions match.

These figures describe the committed statpack supplied in this checkout, not a fresh live-corpus query. Its artifact supplies no corpus-wide pull/snapshot vintage that I can quote here; I made no claim that its counts are current as of this run. The narrower CAAF-origin and terminal relist/CVSG cuts are descriptive cross-checks, not replacements for the prior-Term arrival anchor. The paid-segment relist table shows substantially different terminal grant-family shares across distribution counts, and the CVSG bucket is much more selected than the no-CVSG bucket; neither directly estimates the probability that this arrival will acquire its next signal.

I reduce the anchor to **P(any grant) = 0.02**. The petition raises a serious constitutional complaint, but it principally asks the Court to correct the application of established evidentiary principles to one record. The service court's opinion is expressly nonbinding precedent, and CAAF declined review without supplying a competing constitutional rule. The petition invokes several circuits' favorable applications of the complete-defense right, but does not establish a clean conflict over a generally applicable legal test. The difference between a rule conflict and disagreement over probative value is the main downward adjustment.

The strongest upward consideration is the alleged asymmetry: the prosecution could introduce extensive evidence against suicide, while the defense lost the decedent's own mental-health messages. The appendix independently confirms that the military judge confused two hearsay exceptions and that the appellate court recognized the mistake. That makes the constitutional concern substantial rather than merely a disputed hearsay ruling. Nevertheless, the appellate analysis retained a separate Rule 403 basis, discussing temporal remoteness, confusion, and the expert's already-admitted testimony. The record also shows eleven of thirteen offered message pages were admitted; this was not a categorical prohibition on presenting the suicide defense.

I checked the general precedent, Holmes v. South Carolina, 547 U.S. 319, 326–31 (2006), through CourtListener, not through a search for this case. Holmes rejects exclusion keyed to the prosecution's supposedly strong case but preserves legitimate rules addressing marginal relevance, confusion, and remoteness. Whether this exclusion crossed that constitutional boundary is arguable; it does not transparently follow from the fact that the original hearsay rationale was mistaken. This distinction supports a low cert probability without deciding that the trial was fair or that the conviction was correct.

## Uncertainty and secondary claims

No brief in opposition is provisioned; none should be inferred from the scheduled response deadline. The appendix is explicitly truncated inside the central Rule 403 discussion at App. 104, omitting the remainder of that appellate section and substantial later material. Thus the petition's characterization of App. 104–106 is advocacy I cannot fully check against the supplied opinion. The retained opening analysis and trial-ruling excerpt provide some independent balance, but not complete vehicle or harmless-error analysis.

The petition describes life without parole, while the lower-court opinion's sentencing statement at App. 6 says life with the possibility of parole. I use the court's statement and do not resolve the inconsistency by conjecture. Both the discrepancy and the missing central appendix discussion are flagged.

The 0.95 distribution increment is high because an arrival normally still needs its first conference; it is consistent with a low probability of an actual later relist. CVSG is especially unlikely with the United States already represented. Conditional on the rare grant, I allow a meaningful but minority summary-reversal route (0.30); no supplied intervening authority makes GVR the default. The dissent/statement probability is conditional on denial, not an unconditional writing probability. These secondary numbers are judgmental estimates, not published conditional base rates. I omit cert votes because no observable per-Justice lineup is supported.

The stakes score of 0.32 reflects the severe individual liberty interest and the general complete-defense principle, tempered by the narrow factual vehicle and absence of an established nationwide rule conflict. It is separate from grant likelihood.
