## Question, conflict, and vehicle

The provisioned `questions-presented.txt` asks whether Airline Deregulation Act preemption reaches passengers' tort and product-liability claims against an independent software vendor that neither transports nor serves passengers and has no commercial relationship with them. I read the petition's statement, reasons for review, and bound appendix, especially petition pp. 7–29 and App. 1a–17a. No brief in opposition or cert reply is listed in `documents.json`; the response deadline is still in the future. Their absence is not an extraction failure or a concession by the respondent.

The petition makes a substantial case for a longstanding disagreement over the meaning of airline “service”: the Fifth Circuit's broader approach in *Hodges v. Delta Airlines*, 44 F.3d 334 (5th Cir. 1995) (en banc), versus the narrower approaches in *Charas v. TWA*, 160 F.3d 1259 (9th Cir. 1998) (en banc), and *Taj Mahal Travel v. Delta Airlines*, 164 F.3d 186 (3d Cir. 1998). Its discussion of *National Federation of the Blind v. United Airlines*, 813 F.3d 718 (9th Cir. 2016), provides a relatively concrete technology-related comparison. The independent-vendor setting and widespread outage make the statutory boundary important beyond one passenger's claim. Those are positive cert considerations, and a final dismissal on the pleadings avoids an interlocutory posture.

But the claimed general split is not yet a demonstrated square conflict about this vendor and these losses. Even the narrow definition includes flight scheduling and transportation; the district court emphasized cancelled flights, refunds, rebooking, and related expenses, not merely optional in-flight amenities. More importantly, the petition often argues that CrowdStrike's own conduct is not an airline service, whereas the statutory inquiry also asks whether the passengers' claims **relate to** an airline's service. A defendant need not itself provide the protected service for that distinction to matter. The district court relied on both the claims' connection to airline services and their asserted significant regulatory effects (App. 7a–15a). It did not simply label all software development an airline service. Whether its economic-effects reasoning stretches preemption too far is a serious merits question, but possible error alone does not establish a cert-worthy conflict.

The provisioned `decision-below.txt` matches the judgment identified by the petition: Fifth Circuit No. 25-50518, May 20, 2026, panel Richman, Duncan, and Oldham. It is an unpublished, one-line affirmance under Rule 47.6 with no developed appellate rationale. That makes this a less attractive vehicle for resolving a broad circuit disagreement. `lower-court-docket.txt` reports that en banc entries were unavailable to the provisioning reader; I do not mistake that for no rehearing request. The petition's actual rehearing order, App. 16a–17a, confirms denial and states that no panel member or active judge requested an en banc poll. There is no noted lower-court dissent in the material read.

The petition's assertions that preservation and jurisdiction are clean are helpful but remain one-sided. The missing opposition is my main substantive uncertainty: it could identify closer vendor decisions, other threshold defects, or an intervening development. The supplied same-question list is empty, so it supplies no companion or hold theory. Its cutoff metadata is one day later than the snapshot/current date; I flag that discrepancy and draw no timing or exhaustive-search conclusion from the list.

## Other probabilities and significance

The **98% redistribution increment** is high because a response was requested after the initial distribution and is still outstanding; redistribution after response/reply processing is the ordinary anticipated next step. This number is not a 98% forecast of an evaluative relist after a fully briefed conference. I expect one additional distribution most often, with additional true relists less likely.

The **4% new-CVSG probability** recognizes federal transportation interests but discounts the lack of a federal party, an already identified government position, or a demonstrated need for agency expertise in the current record. The **12% summary-route probability is conditional on a grant**. No intervening lead ruling is identified in the inputs, and the disputed statutory boundary is more suited to plenary treatment than obvious-error correction. This low conditional route estimate deliberately differs from pooled grant-family route frequencies.

The **7% probability of a dissent or statement is conditional on denial**. The petition notes that Thomas joined the historical *Duncan* denial dissent concerning the service-definition issue (petition p. 21 and n.1); that supports a small, case-specific Thomas writing probability, not an assumption of his present vote. The unconditional order-writer marginals are small and also allow writings on a grant order. Thomas is the leading individual possibility; Gorsuch is a more speculative second. Most likely nobody writes. No merits vote lineup is forecast.

The **0.62 significance score** concerns the stakes if decided: the reach of statutory preemption into non-airline technology vendors and passengers' remedies after large disruptions. It is not the 12% review probability and does not imply that this petition resolves every claim arising from the outage.
