## Why move above the anchor, but remain well below a likely grant?

**Reasons to raise the estimate:** The call for a response after waiver is an affirmative attention signal absent from the band anchor's conditioning. The QP is a concrete limit on federal preemption, with potential application beyond one outage. The petition describes an acknowledged, entrenched disagreement between broad and narrow definitions of airline service. Final judgment on the pleadings and a dispositive federal question reduce some common vehicle concerns. Those considerations justify roughly doubling the anchor rather than treating the petition as an ordinary unremarked filing. The prompt and statpack supply no measured response-requested uplift; this is a judgment, not an estimated conditional base rate.

**Reasons to stop at 11%:** The appeal produced only an unpublished, unexplained affirmance, not a reasoned precedential extension of circuit law. More importantly, the broad definition-of-service split is not necessarily outcome-determinative for claims seeking compensation for canceled or delayed flights: scheduling and transportation fall near the core even of the narrower definition. The new defendant-side question—liability of a general-purpose software vendor—is related to that split but is not shown by this record to have generated conflicting appellate decisions on materially comparable facts.

The appendix exposes another weakness in the petition's presentation. The district court did not simply declare that software development itself is an airline service. It found that these particular claims **relate to** airline services and would significantly affect those services, relying on the complaint's operational allegations and the flight-disruption damages sought. See appendix 7a–12a and 14a–15a. That difference leaves a substantial respondent argument that the petition attacks a broader categorical rule than the judgment actually requires. The appendix also documents a rehearing denial with no requested en banc poll, rather than a recorded judicial protest urging Supreme Court intervention.

General precedent supports caution about converting defendant status into an automatic escape from preemption. *Northwest, Inc. v. Ginsberg*, 572 U.S. 273 (2014), holds that state common-law obligations can fall within ADA preemption. Its holding does not decide this vendor question. Conversely, *Dan's City Used Cars, Inc. v. Pelkey*, 569 U.S. 251 (2013), rejects boundless preemption under the related motor-carrier provision, whose additional transportation-of-property limitation matters. Neither case supplies a mechanically controlling outcome for this petition. They make a plenary statutory-boundary dispute more plausible than an obvious summary correction.

The historical denial in *Northwest Airlines, Inc. v. Duncan*, 531 U.S. 1058 (2000), is a closer procedural caution: O'Connor, joined by Rehnquist and Thomas, urged review of the service-definition split, but the Court denied review. That supports both the reality of the longstanding issue and the distinction between an acknowledged split and four votes to hear this particular vehicle. It is one selected precedent, not a grant-rate sample.
