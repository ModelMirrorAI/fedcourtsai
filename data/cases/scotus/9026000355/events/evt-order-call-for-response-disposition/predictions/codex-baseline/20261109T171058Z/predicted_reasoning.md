# Forecast of the Court's disposition

The Court will deny the petition after receiving or awaiting the requested response. The denial will be unexplained and will not endorse the lower courts' reasoning. The chance of any grant, including a summary disposition, is 11%.

The snapshot shows one distribution, for the November 6, 2026 conference, and no CVSG. The requested response is due November 18. I expect one additional distribution after response briefing, then denial, most likely in December 2026 or early 2027. The probability of at least one new distribution is 98%; that administrative redistribution is not a forecast that the Court will repeatedly hold the petition for substantive discussion. Multiple further distributions are not the modal path.

The Court will not call for the Solicitor General's views. I assign an 8% probability of a new invitation; if one occurs, it will most likely follow response briefing in early 2027 and postpone disposition substantially.

If the Court grants plenary review, it will take the petition's single vendor-preemption question, focused on whether passengers' state tort claims against an independent cybersecurity supplier are sufficiently related to airline services under 49 U.S.C. § 41713(b)(1). It is less likely to undertake a free-standing resolution of every disagreement about onboard amenities. The conditional merits question will be the connection between the particular claims and airline transportation, not whether CrowdStrike becomes an air carrier merely by selling software to airlines.

Conditional on a grant, plenary briefing and argument are more likely than disposition in the cert order: 82% versus 18%. There is no identified intervening decision around which to organize a routine GVR. A summary reversal remains possible but would require treating the preemption extension as sufficiently clear error despite the competing statutory arguments.

Conditional on denial, the chance of a noted dissent or statement is 7.5%. I expect none. If a Justice writes to urge resolution of the longstanding service-definition issue, Thomas is the most plausible individual author, although his participation in the historical Duncan dissent does not make a new writing likely.

The denial path leaves the unpublished judgment in place without resolving the supplier-liability boundary nationwide. If review instead occurs, the likely controversy will concern the limits of the statute's broad relational language and the adequacy of the connection between general-purpose software negligence and airline services; this is conditional merits content, not a prediction that a majority opinion accompanies denial.
