## In brief

Horseracing regulators ask the Court to overturn a ruling that a federal horseracing law's enforcement powers are unconstitutional.

## What happened

Congress created the Horseracing Integrity and Safety Authority, a private nonprofit, to write and enforce national safety and anti-doping rules for thoroughbred racing, under Federal Trade Commission oversight. Horsemen's associations, racetrack groups, and Texas sued, arguing the arrangement unlawfully hands government power to a private entity. After Congress amended the law to expand FTC oversight, the Fifth Circuit upheld the rulemaking structure but ruled the law's enforcement provisions—letting the Authority investigate, subpoena, search, fine, and sue without FTC approval—unconstitutional. The Sixth Circuit, reviewing a similar challenge, upheld HISA's enforcement provisions, creating a split between the circuits.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law can let a private organization investigate, subpoena, search, fine, and sue people on its own, without needing a federal agency's approval first, without that violating the constitutional rule against handing government power to private parties unsupervised.

## Where it stands

The petition and a companion petition from the federal government have been fully briefed, including responses from multiple respondent groups and a reply from the petitioners. The case was distributed for the Court's December 4, 2026 conference, and no decision on whether to grant review has yet been recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The Horseracing Integrity and Safety Authority and related petitioners are asking the Court to reverse the Fifth Circuit's ruling against them. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether HISA's enforcement provisions are constitutional, with no outcome assured for either side. If the Court denies review, the Fifth Circuit's ruling striking down those enforcement provisions as unconstitutional stands, leaving in place a split with the Sixth Circuit, which upheld similar provisions in a separate case.
