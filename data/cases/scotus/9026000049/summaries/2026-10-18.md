## In brief

Iowa's attorney general asks the Court to revive the state's law criminalizing immigrants' reentry after the Eighth Circuit blocked it as conflicting with federal immigration law.

## What happened

Iowa passed a law making it a state crime for a noncitizen to enter or be found in Iowa after being denied admission, excluded, deported, or removed, and requiring judges to order such people to leave the country. Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and two individual immigrants sued, arguing federal law preempts the statute. A district court blocked the law, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed, finding it likely conflicts with and intrudes on an area controlled by federal immigration law. Iowa's attorney general, the only defendant who appealed, asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two things: first, whether the people challenging the law had the legal right to sue before it was enforced against them, given that Iowa's attorney general says she won't apply the law to people with legal immigration status; and second, whether a state law that largely mirrors federal immigration crimes is invalid because it might sometimes interfere with federal officials' discretion not to prosecute someone for illegal reentry.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference multiple times, most recently for the October 16, 2026 conference, after earlier conferences in September and October 2026 were rescheduled. The docket does not show a decision on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Iowa's attorney general is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the law is preempted and whether the plaintiffs had standing to sue, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Eighth Circuit's decision blocking Iowa from enforcing the law stands, leaving the injunction in place while the case continues below. A denial would not mean the Supreme Court agrees the law is unconstitutional, only that the lower court's ruling remains in effect for now.
