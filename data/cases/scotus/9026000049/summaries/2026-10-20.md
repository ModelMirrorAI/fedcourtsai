## In brief

Iowa's attorney general asks the Court to revive the state's law criminalizing immigrants' reentry after a federal appeals court blocked it as conflicting with federal immigration law.

## What happened

Iowa passed a law making it a state crime for a noncitizen to reenter or be found in Iowa after being denied admission, excluded, deported, or removed, and requiring judges to order such people to leave the country. Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and two individuals sued, arguing the law conflicts with federal immigration law. A federal district court blocked the law, and the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, finding the challengers had standing and that the law likely conflicts with federal law. The Eighth Circuit denied rehearing, with two judges dissenting. Iowa's attorney general then asked the Supreme Court to review both the standing and merits rulings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions. First, whether people suing before a law is enforced against them have standing when the state's own attorney general says the law does not apply to them as she reads it. Second, whether a state law that overlaps with federal immigration crimes can be blocked entirely because it might sometimes interfere with federal officials' discretion not to prosecute, even though the state law otherwise mirrors federal law.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once without the Court granting or denying it. Most recently, on October 19, 2026, the Court invited the Solicitor General to file a brief giving the federal government's views on whether the case should be heard.

## What each outcome would mean

Iowa's attorney general, Brenna Bird, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review and later rules for Bird, the lower court's block on the law could be lifted, letting Iowa enforce it, or the case could be sent back for further proceedings. If the Court rules for the challengers, Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and the individual plaintiffs, the block on enforcement would stand. If the Court denies review, the Eighth Circuit's decision blocking the law remains in effect, without the Supreme Court ruling on who is right.
