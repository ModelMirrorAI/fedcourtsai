## In brief

Iowa's attorney general asks the Court to revive the state's law criminalizing immigrants' reentry after removal, which lower courts blocked as unconstitutional.

## What happened

Iowa enacted a law making it a state crime for a noncitizen to enter or be found in Iowa after being denied admission, excluded, deported, or removed, and requiring judges to order such people to leave the country. Iowa Migrant Movement for Justice and two individual immigrants sued, arguing the law is preempted by federal immigration law. A federal district court blocked the law, and the Eighth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, finding the plaintiffs had legal standing to sue and that the law conflicts with the federal government's exclusive authority over immigration enforcement. The full Eighth Circuit declined to rehear the case, over two judges' dissent.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two questions. First, whether people suing before a law is enforced against them can show they are harmed enough to sue when the state's attorney general says she will not enforce the law against them. Second, whether a state law that largely duplicates a federal immigration crime is invalid everywhere it might apply just because enforcing it could sometimes interfere with federal officials' discretion not to prosecute.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including responses from amici, and was distributed for conferences in late September and early October 2026. Both conferences were followed by a "rescheduled" notation, and the docket does not yet show whether the Court granted or denied review.

## What each outcome would mean

Iowa's attorney general, Brenna Bird, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide whether the law can be enforced, with no outcome assumed in advance. If the Court denies review, the Eighth Circuit's ruling blocking enforcement of the law stands, leaving Iowa unable to enforce it while the broader lawsuit continues in lower courts. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the law is unconstitutional, only that the lower court's decision remains in place.
