## Why 14%, below the state anchor

**Positive evidence:** The Court affirmatively requested a response after a waiver and initial distribution (snapshot, August 26). That gives this case more attention than an unattended petition. The question also invokes a real legal mechanism for revisiting continuing decrees after changed law. Horne v. Flores, 557 U.S. 433, 447 (2009), confirms that Rule 60(b)(5) can support modification or vacatur when changed circumstances warrant it, with the burden on the movant (CourtListener opinion 145850, majority Part III.A, consulted through MCP). The post-Dobbs setting creates a plausible interest in ensuring that obsolete constitutional premises do not continue controlling officials. These considerations keep the forecast well above the whole-docket rate.

**The most important contrary evidence is inside the petition's own appendix.** The petition repeatedly invokes Judge VanDyke's statement as support for intervention. But Appendix F, pp. 61a and 67a–70a, expressly supports the mootness result: lifting this injunction would not permit enforcement of a statute the Guam Supreme Court has held repealed; a newly enacted abortion restriction would not be restrained by this old injunction. His criticism of Roe's historical consequences is not disagreement with the panel's mootness holding. I therefore do not count the statement as a dissent from the disposition or an intra-circuit conflict supporting review.

Appendix A, pp. 1a–2a, is a brief dismissal tied to the territorial judgment, not a broad holding that Rule 60(b)(5) relief is unavailable after overruled precedent. The petition itself acknowledges the Fifth Circuit's similar McCorvey approach (petition pp. 16–17), and Appendix F explains the analogy. The record does not demonstrate a developed circuit split. My inference is that the Court can regard this case as an application of mootness to a repealed law rather than a vehicle for resolving a recurring disagreement over prospective relief.

The assertion that no federal court addressed the Rule 60 request also requires qualification. Appendix B, pp. 6a–8a, denies relief because the attorney general did not answer plaintiffs' Organic Act/void-ab-initio argument and did not establish entitlement to vacate the injunction in its entirety. Appendix B, p. 5a, also records separate First Amendment objections to portions of the statute. Those are actual alternative arguments and preservation complications, not findings that every such objection is correct. They nevertheless weaken the petition's presentation of an injunction with one obsolete premise and no other issues.

Starting at 23.63%, I make a modest upward qualitative adjustment for the response request and potentially important decree issue, but a larger downward adjustment for the authoritative local-law barrier, absence of a shown split, the rehearing statement's express agreement with mootness, and the remaining preservation/scope complications. **14%** is a judgmental synthesis, not the output of a fitted response-request model. The unavailable BIO is the main remaining evidentiary uncertainty. The petition's Munsingwear argument (pp. 14–16) preserves a plausible narrow grant-side path, but its application to a decades-old final injunction on an appeal from a later Rule 60 denial is not self-evident.
