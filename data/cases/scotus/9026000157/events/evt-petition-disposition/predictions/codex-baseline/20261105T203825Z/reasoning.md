## Reasons to adjust upward

The question concerns the boundary between religious institutional autonomy and civil adjudication under neutral law, not just who breached a local bylaw. The Nevada court expressly adopted an extension of neutral principles beyond property disputes and applied it to internal governance (App. 10a–15a). That creates a reviewable legal rule in a published state-high-court decision.

The claimed division has genuine substance, even though not every cited case is equivalent. The petition contrasts Puri v. Khalsa, 844 F.3d 1152, 1165–68 (9th Cir. 2017), involving governance of Sikh-affiliated entities, with Crowder v. Southern Baptist Convention, 828 F.2d 718, 724–26 (11th Cir. 1987), rejecting civil intervention in internal electoral governance. CourtListener opinion 4113720 independently confirms Puri's express willingness to use secular rules outside property cases, subject to its doctrinal and ministerial limits. CourtListener's Crowder record and opinion excerpts provide the competing church-governance context. The petition also presents a recent Fifth Circuit decision as sharpening the conflict (Pet. 13–15), although I did not independently retrieve that decision. The requested response after waiver is affirmative attention to this petition, not merely an automatic filing opportunity.

## Reasons denial remains more likely

The best argument against review comes from the actual opinion rather than the petition's characterization. Nevada said religious eligibility for membership and management positions was **not contested**, and the pleaded claims could be resolved through secular requirements concerning corporate authority, records, and procedures (App. 14a–15a). It repeatedly qualified its assessment by the litigation's early stage. The Court could view the dispute as application of established nonentanglement rules to a particular complaint, rather than a clean categorical choice about all religious governance.

The petition's wider split includes defamation, employment, donations, and hierarchical-church disputes; different outcomes can partly reflect those differences. Nor is review guaranteed merely because an issue concerns religious liberty. The petition itself acknowledges prior denials in Belya and O'Connell, and distinguishes the latter's appellate-jurisdiction problems (Pet. 10–11, 29–30). Those are prior cases identified in the supplied filing, not this petition's outcome.

Finality remains a vehicle uncertainty: the Nevada court denied extraordinary relief from an order allowing the civil action to proceed. Petitioners make serious finality arguments based on the original-writ proceeding, Atlantic Richfield, and Cox Broadcasting (Pet. 28–29), so I do not assume a jurisdictional bar; neither do I treat their assurance as established. The missing, not-yet-due opposition limits assessment of that issue and of whether the alleged split survives respondents' factual distinctions. I therefore move well above the anchor, to 26%, but not near even odds.
