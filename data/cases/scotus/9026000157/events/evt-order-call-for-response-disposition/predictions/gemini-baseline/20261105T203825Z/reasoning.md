# Reasoning: `scotus/9026000157` (`evt-order-call-for-response-disposition`)

## 1. Case Overview and Procedural Posture

In *Jasvinder Singh, et al. v. Second Judicial District Court of Nevada, Washoe County, et al.* (No. 26-157), Petitioners—members of the management committee and subcommittees of the Northern Nevada Sikh Society (NNSS), a Nevada religious nonprofit that owns and operates the Sikh Temple (Gurdwara) of Reno—seek review of a published April 2, 2026 decision of the Supreme Court of Nevada (*Singh v. Second Jud. Dist. Ct.*, 586 P.3d 858 (Nev. 2026)).

Respondents (Real Parties in Interest below) are general members and former management-committee members of NNSS who sued Petitioners in Nevada state district court under NRS 82.216(1) for breach of NNSS's bylaws and declaratory relief. Respondents alleged that Petitioners:
1. Created an Interim Committee and a subsequent Subcommittee without the approval of NNSS's general members in order to transfer the Temple into a trust, contrary to Article One of the bylaws (which states that NNSS's assets are vested in the general body, the Sikh Sangat as a whole, forever) and Article Seven (which requires majority general-member approval for committees);
2. Failed to issue numbered membership cards/certificates or maintain a membership roster as required by Article Three, preventing NNSS from ensuring that valid General Members receive notice and vote at elections;
3. Refused to allow members to inspect corporate books and records under Article Nine; and
4. Were not properly elected to the Management Committee by valid General Members at an election meeting pursuant to the bylaws, rendering their official actions void.

Petitioners moved to dismiss the complaint for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction under the First Amendment's church-autonomy (ecclesiastical abstention) doctrine. After the state trial court denied the motion to dismiss, the parties stipulated to stay trial-court proceedings, and Petitioners petitioned the Nevada Supreme Court for an original writ of prohibition or mandamus.

The Nevada Supreme Court entertained the writ petition on the merits and denied relief in a published opinion. Acknowledging that courts are split on whether the "neutral principles of law" approach from *Presbyterian Church in the United States v. Mary Elizabeth Blue Hull Memorial Presbyterian Church*, 393 U.S. 440 (1969), and *Jones v. Wolf*, 443 U.S. 595 (1979), applies outside real-property ownership disputes, the Nevada Supreme Court aligned itself with the Ninth Circuit's decision in *Puri v. Khalsa*, 844 F.3d 1152 (9th Cir. 2017). It held that the neutral-principles exception applies beyond real-property disputes so long as the underlying controversy can be resolved by applying secular legal principles without inquiry into religious doctrine, practice, or texts. Applying that rule at the motion-to-dismiss stage, the court concluded that Respondents' claims turn on whether Petitioners complied with corporate procedures in NNSS's bylaws and NRS 82.216(1) rather than any dispute over Sikh religious doctrine or religious eligibility requirements.

## 3. Legal Merits, Circuit Split, and Vehicle Analysis

### A. Strength of the Question Presented and Split
The petition raises a recurring and doctrinal question of strong interest to several Justices on the current Court: whether the "neutral principles of law" framework of *Jones v. Wolf* is a narrow carve-out restricted to disputes over title/ownership of church property, or a general rule permitting civil courts to adjudicate internal governance, bylaw, and election disputes within religious organizations whenever the court thinks it can apply facially secular corporate rules without resolving theological questions.

- **Lower-Court Division:** The Nevada Supreme Court explicitly acknowledged the split in authority (App. 10a). On one side, the Fifth Circuit (*Lutheran Church—Missouri Synod v. Christian*, 178 F.4th 193 (5th Cir. 2026); *McRaney v. North American Mission Board*, 157 F.4th 627 (5th Cir. 2025)), Sixth Circuit (*Hutchison v. Thomas*, 789 F.2d 392 (6th Cir. 1986)), Eleventh Circuit (*Crowder v. Southern Baptist Convention*, 828 F.2d 718 (11th Cir. 1987)), and state high courts in Oklahoma (*Oklahoma Annual Conference of the United Methodist Church v. Timmons*, 2023 OK 101), Georgia, and Arkansas strictly cabin neutral-principles review to property-ownership disputes and refuse to adjudicate internal church governance or interpret church bylaws even when framed in secular procedural terms. On the other side, the Ninth Circuit (*Puri v. Khalsa*, 844 F.3d 1152 (9th Cir. 2017)), Second Circuit (*Belya v. Kapral*, 45 F.4th 621 (2d Cir. 2022)), D.C. Circuit (*O'Connell v. U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops*, 134 F.4th 1243 (D.C. Cir. 2025)), Texas Supreme Court (*Southern Methodist University v. South Central Jurisdictional Conference*, 716 S.W.3d 475 (Tex. 2025)), Kentucky Supreme Court, and now the Nevada Supreme Court permit civil courts to adjudicate non-property corporate governance, contract, or tort claims involving religious bodies if resolvable through secular legal principles.
- **Recent SCOTUS Interest and Denials:** The scope of church autonomy and the neutral-principles doctrine has generated multiple recent dissents from denial of rehearing en banc (e.g., by Judges Cabranes and Park in *Belya*, Judges Bress and Bumatay in *Huntsman*, and Judge Rao in *O'Connell*). However, the Supreme Court denied certiorari in *Belya* (2023) and recently denied certiorari after four distributions in *O'Connell* (No. 25-849, denied May 26, 2026), which featured 15 amicus briefs. While *O'Connell* suffered from a threshold federal collateral-order jurisdiction problem that is absent here (because *Singh* arises from a state supreme court's final denial of an original writ of prohibition, akin to *Atlantic Richfield Co. v. Christian*, 590 U.S. 1 (2020), and satisfies *Cox Broadcasting Corp. v. Cohn*, 420 U.S. 469 (1975)), the Court's repeated reluctance to grant interlocutory church-autonomy petitions counsels caution.

### B. Vehicle Vulnerabilities (What the Brief in Opposition Will Emphasize)
Although Petitioners frame this case as a pure governance dispute outside the property context, Respondents (now represented by experienced Supreme Court counsel Paul Hughes) will have strong vehicle and merits arguments in their forthcoming Brief in Opposition:
1. **The Dispute Is Substantively Tied to Control of Church Real Property:** The core catalyst of the lawsuit (First Claim for Relief) is Petitioners' alleged attempt to transfer the Sikh Temple (real property at 1380 Selmi Drive, Reno) out of NNSS and into an undisclosed trust, despite Article One of the bylaws vesting NNSS's assets in the congregation forever. Even courts on the "property-only" side of the split routinely apply neutral principles of state nonprofit and trust law when a faction attempts an ultra vires transfer of the congregation's house of worship. Thus, the distinction between a "property ownership dispute" and a "corporate governance dispute over authority to transfer church property into a trust" is blurred on these facts.
2. **Interlocutory / Motion-to-Dismiss Posture:** The Nevada Supreme Court repeatedly emphasized the preliminary posture of the case: *"At this juncture, the claims presented in RPIs' underlying complaint appear to be secular in nature"* and *"nothing in the relevant sections of NNSS's bylaws or the claims alleged would—as it appears at this point in the proceedings—require the district court to evaluate Sikh doctrine, practices, or texts"* (App. 15a). The court also noted that *"the parties ... do not contest whether the Management Defendants meet the religious eligibility requirements"* (App. 14a). Respondents will argue that if factual development on remand actually implicates religious qualifications for membership under Article Three or officer status under Article Six, the Nevada courts have not foreclosed applying ecclesiastical abstention to those specific issues.
3. **Finality Dispute Under 28 U.S.C. § 1257(a):** Even though the denial of an independent writ of prohibition in Nevada is formally a separate proceeding and Petitioners have a plausible argument under *Atlantic Richfield* and *Cox Broadcasting*, Respondents will contest § 1257 finality because trial-court proceedings remain at the threshold pleading stage and the Nevada Supreme Court left open whether ecclesiastical issues might arise later in the litigation.
