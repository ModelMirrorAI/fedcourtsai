## Anchor and adjustments

The frozen `sal-v7` baseline-band anchor is **0.051512996760685784**, pooled from prior Terms under the placement-time statpack. I use that reached-band rate, not the much lower terminal-baseline figure. The private petitioners' caption controls the class; naming the Nevada district court as respondent does not make this a state-government petition. The current statpack uses the same salience version and its prior-Term reached-baseline figures broadly corroborate the frozen anchor. I exclude this petition's own 2026 Term from anchoring.

The modern discretionary-cert table implies an approximately 2.8% whole-docket grant-family rate from its displayed counts, but that broader population is not the appropriate conditional anchor. The paid-segment signal cuts provide context: terminal relist buckets 0, 1, 2, and 3+ have grant-family rates of approximately 1.8%, 13.5%, 41.2%, and 37.1%. The CVSG cut is approximately 6.4% without a CVSG and 34.8% with one. The 2+ amicus bucket is approximately **22.7%**, versus 3.8% for zero briefs. These terminal, marginal cuts cannot be multiplied together or treated as forward transition probabilities. The small Nevada-origin bucket is not a reliable case-specific estimate.

I move materially above 5.15% because the Court affirmatively requested a response, three distinct amicus briefs were already filed, and the petition identifies a consequential disagreement over religious governance. The Nevada opinion itself discusses competing approaches and explicitly adopts the broader neutral-principles approach beyond property-ownership disputes (appendix, pages 10a–13a). This is stronger evidence of a review-worthy question than the petition's assertion of a split alone. The requested relief includes questions about valid membership, elections, committee authority, and the proposed temple trust, creating a potentially concrete setting for the governance/property distinction (petition, pages 4–7 and 24–26; appendix, complaint).

The petition compares the Nevada/Ninth Circuit approach with Fifth, Sixth, and Eleventh Circuit decisions restricting civil adjudication of internal church governance. A CourtListener lookup independently confirmed the relevant passage in **Puri v. Khalsa, 844 F.3d 1152, 1165 (9th Cir. 2017)**: its rule is not categorically confined to property disputes, but requires avoiding underlying doctrinal controversies. This confirms the existence of the broader side of the argument; it does not independently verify every authority or show that all cited cases conflict on identical facts. The recent Fifth Circuit developments described in the petition make the claimed conflict more salient, but I treat their characterization as advocacy rather than a verified census of circuit positions.

Several considerations keep denial more likely. First, a response request indicates interest by at least someone at the Court, not the four votes needed for a grant, and this request preceded the scheduled conference: there has been no true post-conference relist in this record. Second, the lower court framed the case as enforcement of secular corporate limitations, not selection of religious beliefs or resolution of contested theology. The dispute concerns an attempted property transfer as well as governance, which can blur the petition's proposed bright line. The different results in the cited cases may partly reflect different claims and institutional structures.

Third, the underlying case remains pending. Petitioners offer substantial finality arguments based on the separate original-writ proceeding and, alternatively, the Cox Broadcasting exception (petition, pages 28–30). Those arguments mitigate but do not eliminate vehicle concerns; I do not assert a categorical jurisdictional bar. Fourth, the petition itself acknowledges prior denials in **Belya** and **O'Connell**. Those denials establish no merits rule, and petitioners identify distinctions, but they temper the claim that a familiar church-autonomy disagreement alone makes review likely. Without the opposition I cannot assess preservation, party authority, the precise contested membership issues, or the strongest finality objection from both sides.

The resulting 24% reflects roughly sixfold higher grant odds than the frozen anchor, a substantial but deliberately limited adjustment for the combined response request, acknowledged legal disagreement, and cross-denominational support. It is judgmental, not an estimated response-requested cohort frequency: the committed pack publishes no such conditioning cut. I avoid double counting the correlated salience and amicus signals.

## Additional claims and uncertainty

- **Further distribution, 0.99:** redistribution after the response or expiration of the response period is overwhelmingly likely from this one-distribution posture. This is not a 99% forecast of a substantive post-conference relist, and the response-requested cohort masks this claim.
- **New CVSG, 0.025:** the question is constitutional and concerns private religious governance, with no federal party or identified administrative program requiring the Solicitor General's institutional input. Federal interest is possible, so the probability is not zero.
- **Summary route conditional on a grant, 0.12:** a grant would more likely receive briefing and argument because the Court would be defining a disputed doctrinal boundary. No provisioned lead decision supplies an obvious mechanical GVR route. The 12% is conditional, not the approximately 2.9% unconditional probability.
- **Separate writing conditional on denial, 0.18:** the church-autonomy issue provides a plausible occasion for a dissent or statement, but an unexplained denial remains much more likely. The per-Justice order-writing estimates are unconditional chances of authoring, not joining, an order-related writing. Their concentration on Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch is a low-confidence judgment about this issue, not a retrieved assignment or a forecast of cert votes.

The significance score of **0.63** concerns the potential effect on religious institutions' autonomy and civil remedies across faiths, not grant likelihood. It is below the top tier because the immediate dispute is local and a decision could be framed narrowly.
