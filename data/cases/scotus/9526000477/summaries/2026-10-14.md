## In brief

Republican campaign committees asked the Supreme Court to block an order forcing the FCC to rule on political ad rates; the Court denied it as moot.

## What happened

The dispute concerns whether broadcasters must offer discounted "lowest unit charge" advertising rates to political parties and joint fundraising committees, not just individual candidates. Democratic candidates challenged an FCC public notice extending the discount, and the Fourth Circuit set it aside. The Supreme Court stayed that ruling in September, finding the Fourth Circuit likely lacked jurisdiction because the FCC had not yet ruled on the candidates' administrative appeal. The candidates then asked the Fourth Circuit to order the FCC to act, and it granted that request, ordering a decision by October 9. The Republican committees asked the Supreme Court to block that order too.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether it should pause, while appeals continue, a lower court order requiring a federal agency to issue a decision on a pending administrative appeal by a set deadline. The dispute underneath concerns whether the agency's order forcing quick action improperly overrode the Supreme Court's earlier stay and whether the agency had a clear legal duty to decide the matter on that timetable.

## Where it stands

The Chief Justice referred the stay application to the full Court, which denied it as moot in a per curiam opinion. Justices Sotomayor and Jackson stated they would have denied the application (without reaching the mootness question).

## What each outcome would mean

The National Republican Congressional Committee and National Republican Senatorial Committee, as applicants, sought to block the Fourth Circuit's order requiring the FCC to decide the candidates' pending appeal by a set deadline. The Court's denial, issued as moot, means it did not pause that order, but also did not rule on whether the Fourth Circuit was right to issue it. Because the case was declared moot rather than decided on the merits, the underlying dispute over the advertising rate rules was left unresolved by this ruling, and the practical effect for the parties depended on events that made the application moot rather than on who was right.
