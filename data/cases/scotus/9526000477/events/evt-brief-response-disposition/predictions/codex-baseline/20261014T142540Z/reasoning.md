The applicants' strongest argument is equitable rather than their categorical assertion that mandamus cannot aid future jurisdiction. The panel imposed an immediate deadline during a further agency comment process, with an election approaching, after the Court had preserved the existing arrangements. The Court can view the acceleration as insufficiently respectful of that earlier intervention without holding that administrative delay is categorically immune from review. Application pages 14–21 and the appended Wilkinson dissent support that route. I forecast the Court's likely treatment of these arguments, not their inevitable legal correctness.

The opposition meaningfully reduces the forecast from near-certainty. Mandamus here requires a decision, not a particular substantive result; the FCC could deny administrative review and leave the notice effective. Consequently, the earlier finding of harm from actual vacatur does not automatically establish harm from mandamus. The response at pages 14–21 also explains why prospective appellate jurisdiction and unreasonable-delay review are different from premature merits review, and why a mandatory agency duty need not carry a fixed statutory deadline to be enforceable. Its account of delayed comment periods and an already-circulated agency draft supplies a concrete, fact-bound defense of the writ. A court accepting that distinction could deny a stay without repudiating September 4. I therefore reserve substantial probability for denial and a small residual for non-grant procedural outcomes.

For the governing stay framework I checked the historical decision in *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010), through CourtListener. It requires a reasonable prospect of certiorari, a fair prospect of reversal, and likely irreparable injury, with relative harms considered in close cases. The opposition's strongest objections concern the first and third requirements. Generic web attempts returned no usable material; no forecast fact depends on them.
