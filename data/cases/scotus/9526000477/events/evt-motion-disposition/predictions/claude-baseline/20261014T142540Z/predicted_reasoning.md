# Forecast — NRCC v. Brown, No. 26A477 (stay of Fourth Circuit mandamus)

**Disposition: the Court grants the stay** (unqualified grant, P = 0.72). The
application asks the Court to stay a Fourth Circuit writ of mandamus ordering the
FCC to resolve the candidates' application for review by noon, October 9, 2026 —
issued by the same panel, in the same dispute, one month after the Court stayed
that panel's merits judgment in *NRCC v. Brown*, No. 26A274 (Sept. 4, 2026),
holding the Fourth Circuit likely lacked jurisdiction while the application for
review was pending and that the committees showed irreparable harm in the weeks
before the midterms. I expect the Court to treat the mandamus as an end-run
around that order and stay it, likely by a short order over noted dissents
(Justices Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson are the likeliest dissenters, as context).

**Path to disposition.**
- **Administrative stay first.** Given the October 9 noon deadline, the Chief
  Justice very likely entered an administrative stay on or about October 8–9.
  That is not the disposing entry; the final stay order is.
- **Response called for** (P = 0.85). The record I was shown ends at the
  submission entry: no response requested. The Chief routinely calls for a
  response alongside an administrative stay in a contested matter of this kind,
  from both the candidates and the FCC (which itself sought a stay below).
- **Referred to the full Court** (P = 0.80). The first application was decided
  by the Court with a written order; a repeat in the same case is the full
  Court's business, not a Circuit Justice's in-chambers call.
- **Amicus filings** (P = 0.30 of at least one). The record shows zero. Broadcast
  industry groups (e.g., the Television Bureau of Advertising, which has its own
  pending FCC application) or party/campaign groups could file, but a compressed
  response window keeps the count small.
- **Timing.** Disposition within roughly one to three weeks of filing, i.e., by
  late October 2026, before the November election.

**Main ways it is not an unqualified grant.** (1) Mootness: if the FCC resolves
the application for review (e.g., denies it on the merits, leaving the public
notice in place) the application could be dismissed or withdrawn as moot.
(2) A narrower or mixed order (e.g., staying only the deadline), which the
resolver reads as a denial. (3) An outright denial on the theory that an order
merely compelling agency action does not itself disturb the rules — I think this
is the least likely, given the Court's stated concern with keeping matters stable
before the midterms.
