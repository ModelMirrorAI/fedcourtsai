## Why not near certainty

The best contrary argument is in the actual appendix, not merely inferred from applicants' advocacy. The October 7 panel majority, App. 28–34, stresses more than five months of unresolved administrative review, an already-circulated proposed order, and a statutory duty to decide. Judge Wynn's concurrence, App. 35–39, distinguishes ordering the FCC to make a decision from ordering any particular result: mandamus may complete, rather than evade, the prerequisite the Supreme Court identified. That distinction also makes the applicants' claimed injury from the new order less direct than injury from immediate vacatur of the rate guidance.

I therefore do not adopt the applicants' categorical inference that a lack of present merits jurisdiction necessarily defeats mandamus in aid of future jurisdiction. Nor do I treat the earlier stay as deciding the broadcast-rate statute or immunizing indefinite agency delay. The Supreme Court could accept the narrower distinction, deny relief, or permit limited agency action. These possibilities, together with possible procedural developments not in the arrival record, account for the 16% non-grant probability.

The general stay framework is corroborated by *Hollingsworth v. Perry*, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010), retrieved through CourtListener opinion 9413203: prospects of review and reversal, irreparable harm, and balancing in close cases. That authority verifies the framework, not the outcome of this application.
