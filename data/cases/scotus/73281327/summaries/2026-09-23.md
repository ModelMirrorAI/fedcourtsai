## What happened

Nicole Pileggi signed up for a free email newsletter from the Washington Examiner, then separately watched videos on its website. She sued the paper's owner, Washington Newspaper Publishing Company, arguing that a tracking tool on the site improperly told Facebook which videos she watched, violating a federal video-privacy law. A district court dismissed her claim, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed, holding that her newsletter sign-up did not make her a protected "consumer" under the law because it had no connection to the videos she watched on the website. Other appeals courts have split on this same issue, with some ruling for consumers in similar cases and others against them.

## What the Court is being asked

The dispute is over who counts as protected under a law limiting disclosure of video-watching records. The law protects people who buy, rent, or subscribe to something from a company that provides video services. The question is whether that protection covers anyone who buys or subscribes to anything from such a company, or only those who specifically buy or subscribe to video-related goods or services. Pileggi argues the broader reading applies; the newspaper argues, as the lower courts held, that only the narrower reading does.

## Where it stands

The Court has already agreed to decide this same question in another case, Salazar v. Paramount Global, and the newspaper's brief in opposition asks the Court to hold this petition until that case is resolved, then act accordingly. As of the docket record, the case has been distributed for a conference where the Court will consider what to do with it.
