## What happened

Nicole Pileggi sued the Washington Newspaper Publishing Company, which owns the Washington Examiner, claiming it violated a federal video-privacy law by sending Facebook information about videos she watched on its website. Pileggi had signed up for the Examiner's free email newsletter, which included some videos and video links. A tracking tool on the website allegedly reported her video-viewing activity to Facebook without her consent. The district court dismissed her claim, finding she was not a "consumer" protected by the law because her newsletter signup was not connected to the videos she watched on the website. The Court of Appeals for the District of Columbia Circuit affirmed, agreeing that the law only protects people who buy, rent, or subscribe to video-related goods or services, not just any product from a company that also happens to offer videos.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide what the video-privacy law's protections cover when it refers to a "consumer" who buys, rents, or subscribes to goods or services from a company that provides video content. Pileggi argues the law protects anyone who gets any kind of good or service, video-related or not, from such a company. The company responds that the law protects only those who specifically obtain video or similar audio-visual goods or services. The Court has already agreed to decide this same question in a different case involving similar facts.
