## In brief

A bankruptcy trustee asks the Court to decide what standard of fault lets creditors hold her personally liable for how she managed a debtor's estate.

## What happened

Tammy Phillips and her law firm, owed money by debtor Kevan Gilman, sued Chapter 7 trustee Amy Goldman personally, claiming she let two properties in the bankruptcy estate fall into disrepair and failed to collect rent owed to the estate. The bankruptcy court dismissed the suit, finding the claims time-barred and finding Goldman immune because the complaint alleged only ordinary negligence, not gross negligence. The district court reversed on timeliness but agreed Goldman had immunity. The full Ninth Circuit, rehearing the case, ruled that trustees can be personally liable for ordinary negligence and sent the case back.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to settle how much fault a trustee must show before she can be made to personally pay for mistakes in running a bankruptcy estate: whether ordinary carelessness is enough, whether only deliberate wrongdoing counts, or whether something in between, such as gross carelessness, is the right line. Lower courts around the country currently apply three different answers.

## What each outcome would mean

Goldman, who lost before the full Ninth Circuit, is asking the Court to take up and reverse that ruling. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide which fault standard applies nationwide, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling stands, meaning Goldman could be sued personally for ordinary negligence and the case returns to the lower courts under that standard.
