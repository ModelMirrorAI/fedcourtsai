## Why the split matters

The petition's question is whether Chapter 7 trustees may be personally liable for official acts and, if so, under what liability standard. The petition, pp. 9–17, identifies negligence, gross-negligence, and willful-misconduct approaches. More importantly, the appendix itself confirms that the en banc court permits negligence liability absent applicable immunity and deliberately retains that rule (App. 11a–12a, 20a–22a). This is not merely a disagreement about how to label particular alleged conduct.

Historical primary opinions independently substantiate the doctrinal disagreement. *In re Hutchinson*, 5 F.3d 750, 752–53 (4th Cir. 1993), describes competing personal-liability standards, although that appeal itself concerned official-capacity liability. *In re Mailman Steam Carpet Cleaning Corp.*, 196 F.3d 1, 7–8 (1st Cir. 1999), accepts negligence as a basis for personal liability while ultimately protecting the trustee's court-authorized conduct. *In re Schooler*, 725 F.3d 498, 511–13 & n.10 (5th Cir. 2013), retains the Fifth Circuit's gross-negligence standard. I consulted those opinions through CourtListener MCP, not commentary about this petition. Together with the en banc appendix, they make the split materially more persuasive than the petition's assertions alone, without proving that every cited case involved an identical procedural vehicle.

The en banc decision removes the easy possibility of further correction by a Ninth Circuit panel. A uniform rule would affect trustees' administration of estates and exposure of personal assets. Those considerations support a substantial grant chance independent of whether Goldman should ultimately prevail. The petition's separate implied-cause-of-action theory, advanced at p. 19 n.7, receives less weight: the appendix more clearly presents fault and immunity than a preserved, categorical challenge to every personal-capacity action.

## Why denial remains more likely

The Ninth Circuit has not imposed damages or found negligence. It reversed a pleading-stage immunity ruling and remanded for consideration of remaining dismissal arguments; derived immunity can be raised again on a sufficient record (App. 23a–24a). That creates a plausible reason to await a more conclusive vehicle. I do not treat remand as a categorical bar to Supreme Court jurisdiction. The separate appellate-finality issue was expressly addressed: respondents stood on their complaint, which the en banc court held made the district court order effectively final (App. 7a–11a). That explanation reduces, but does not erase, procedural complexity.

The opinion distinguishes judge-like functions, court-authorized acts, business judgments, and ordinary property management (App. 12a–24a). Those protections complicate the petition's suggestion of unrestricted exposure for official acts. The longstanding nature of the split demonstrates entrenchment, but also cautions against assuming that disagreement alone guarantees review. The provisioned en banc opinion contains no separate dissent. The still-unfiled opposition could identify distinctions or preservation issues not answered in the current one-sided briefing.

## Other probabilities and stakes

Another distribution is **98.5% likely**, chiefly because the Court requested a response after the first distribution. This is a forecast of an additional distribution entry; it is not a 98.5% forecast of a substantive post-consideration relist. A future CVSG is **28% likely**. The U.S. Trustee Program has a direct institutional interest, and *Schooler*'s footnote 10 records the government's historical advocacy for a negligence standard. That older position is not a recommendation about this petition, but makes a request for federal views plausible. No CVSG remains the likelier course.

Conditional on a grant, summary disposition is only **3.5% likely**: the supplied materials identify no controlling intervening decision that would resolve this split by GVR, and contested competing standards favor plenary consideration. Conditional on denial, a noted dissent or statement is **4% likely**. Most weight remains on a silent order. The small unconditional writer probabilities put Thomas, Alito, and Gorsuch modestly ahead because of the petition's arguments about personal liability and judge-made causes of action, not because of any recorded vote or known assignment; these individual allocations are weakly informed.

The **0.46 stakes score** reflects a nationally important but specialized bankruptcy issue, separate from grant likelihood. I did not verify or rely on the petition's numerical bankruptcy-volume claims.

## Retrieval limitations

It therefore serves only as procedural context, not quantitative calibration. The committed statpack and frozen anchors supply the base rates. The query's year mask follows docket-Term semantics rather than a strict calendar-day cutoff; this forward cell did not need a replay clock, and the returned grant dates preceded November 16, 2026.

Web search/open attempts returned no usable payload, including an attempt to read the filed amicus brief and the Court's biographies page. I used no facts from those attempts. The first CLI help attempt encountered an unwritable default uv cache; setting `UV_CACHE_DIR=/tmp/uv-cache` and disabling synchronization allowed use of the existing environment without installation. These are tooling limitations, not a missing-record blocker. All external attempts are listed in `retrieval.md`.
