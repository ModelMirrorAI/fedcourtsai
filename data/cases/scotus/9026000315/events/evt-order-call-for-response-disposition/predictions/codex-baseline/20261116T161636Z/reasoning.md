## Why substantially above the anchor

The petition's question is whether Chapter 7 trustees can be personally liable for official acts and, if so, under what standard. Its pp. 9–16 identify a three-way conflict: ordinary negligence in the First, Second, Ninth and Eleventh Circuits; willful-and-deliberate misconduct in the Fourth, Sixth, Seventh and Tenth; gross negligence in the Fifth. This is advocacy, but the key disagreement is independently corroborated. The First Circuit in *In re Mailman Steam Carpet Cleaning Corp.*, 196 F.3d 1, 7–8 (1999), expressly discussed the division over personal liability for negligence (CourtListener opinion 198701, retrieved passage containing “split”). The Fifth Circuit described all three approaches and retained its gross-negligence rule in *In re Schooler*, 725 F.3d 498, 512–13 n.10 (2013) (CourtListener opinion 1036824, retrieved footnote passages). Those are historical precedents, not subsequent developments in this case.

More importantly, the actual decision reproduced in the appendix confirms that this is not merely a petitioner's reframing of an irrelevant issue. Appendix A, pp. 11a and 20a–24a, recognizes personal-capacity negligence liability, rejects a fault-based immunity standard, and applies its rules to Goldman's alleged failures to protect and administer assets. The en banc court explicitly adheres to the Ninth Circuit's established negligence rule. That makes further intra-circuit correction less promising and leaves a national legal standard suitable for Supreme Court resolution. The apparent absence of a separate en banc dissent tempers, but does not erase, this consideration; I found no separate writing in the reproduced decision.

The posture has a particular advantage: respondents elected to stand on their complaint instead of amending to plead gross negligence. The appendix, pp. 10a–11a, explains that election and the court's appellate-jurisdiction ruling. On the petition's presentation, pp. 19–20, the standard therefore changes the result rather than being an abstract disagreement. The request for a response makes outright dismissal of the petition without adversarial consideration less likely, although it does not establish four votes for review.

## Why still below one-half

The conflict is old rather than newly emergent. Its persistence proves entrenchment but does not prove that this Court will prioritize it now. The decision below primarily organizes two immunity doctrines, while the question presented reaches the existence of personal-capacity liability itself. That breadth can complicate a petition whose cleanest issue is the negligence threshold. Appendix A, pp. 12a–24a, distinguishes absolute quasi-judicial immunity for judge-like functions from derived immunity for properly disclosed, court-approved acts. A split about the required degree of fault may not map perfectly onto every immunity or bond-liability case invoked by petitioner. *Schooler*, for example, involved a trustee's surety, a meaningful comparability limit even though its footnote directly discusses personal liability.

The remand also leaves actual negligence, damages, and potentially developed immunity defenses unresolved; the appendix says Goldman lacks derived immunity on the current record, not necessarily every possible later record. The Ninth Circuit explains its finality jurisdiction, so I do not treat it as plainly absent, but that additional procedural discussion is another reason not to call this a risk-free vehicle. Finally, there is no BIO or cert reply among the provisioned filings. A response is not due until December 4 in the snapshot, so its absence is expected, not failed provisioning. I cannot assess unmade preservation, factual or split-distinction objections. These limitations keep a strong cert candidate at 38%, rather than a grant-favored forecast.
