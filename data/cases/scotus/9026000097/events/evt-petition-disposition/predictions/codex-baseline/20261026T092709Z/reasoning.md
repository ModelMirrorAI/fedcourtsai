The petition asks whether unconstitutional-conditions protection applies to the Pierce right to choose private education. Its strongest asserted conflict is Traverse City School District v. Attorney General, 185 N.W.2d 9 (Mich. 1971), involving special-education benefits and an anti-aid provision. The petition also cites four federal circuits' employment-retaliation cases. A targeted CourtListener check of Barrett v. Steubenville City Schools, 388 F.3d 967 (6th Cir. 2004), confirms that its reasoning connects parental educational choice with protection against denial of employment for exercising a constitutional right. That supports a genuine doctrinal tension, rather than merely an unsupported assertion of a split.

Several considerations keep the number well below even odds. Barrett and the other federal employment cases are not the same benefit-administration setting. The Michigan decision is closer, but its age, statutory framework, and constitutional setting leave room to distinguish it. The First Circuit's actual opinion, reproduced at appendix pages 17a–25a, frames this rule as defining where publicly funded services are delivered, not denying all services to private-school children. It also construes state law as not guaranteeing on-site services, a vehicle complication independent of the breadth of the general doctrinal question. Petitioners' alternative claim about education alongside nondisabled peers makes that obstacle less than dispositive, but does not eliminate it.

Crucially, the opinion expressly notes that petitioners did not bring a Free Exercise claim. The location restriction covers secular and religious private schools alike. The Court therefore could not simply apply the religious-status exclusion rule as if that were the pleaded claim; review would require addressing the scope of parental liberty and unconstitutional conditions outside that familiar setting. These limitations reduce the likelihood that four Justices choose this vehicle even if some disagree with the First Circuit's broad language. A request for a response is evidence of interest, not evidence of four votes.

The petition is complete and marked neither empty nor OCR-derived. The separately provisioned appendix is truncated; I rely on its reproduced appellate opinion rather than treating omitted material as absent from the litigation. No brief in opposition is provisioned or shown filed in this snapshot, and the granted extension places its deadline in the future. I therefore have petitioners' advocacy and the lower court's reasoning, not respondents' cert-stage vehicle arguments. That missing adversarial presentation is a major uncertainty.
