## Case-specific adjustment

The petition's sole question is whether unconstitutional-conditions protection applies to the Pierce right to choose private schooling. This is more substantial than a routine allegation that the court below misapplied settled law. The petition, especially its introduction and pages 17–29, identifies concrete contrary authorities: Michigan's Traverse City decision involving special-education services, four circuits' employment-retaliation decisions involving parents' educational choices, and an Alaska school-transportation decision. I treat this as a **claimed conflict**, not a verified five-court split on this exact service-location rule. The Michigan comparison is closer than the employment cases, which involve an affirmative adverse employment action rather than the logistics of supplying a public service.

The staged First Circuit opinion is materially more qualified than the petition's broad characterization. At Appendix A, pages 20a–22a, it recognizes the parental right but characterizes the pleaded burden as logistical rather than an exclusion from private schooling or a deliberately penalizing scheme. At 24a–26a it distinguishes the religious-benefit precedents and emphasizes that no Free Exercise claim was brought. The place rule covers secular as well as religious private schools. State law was construed not to guarantee delivery at the student's enrolled school. These distinctions could let the Court deny without accepting the proposition that parental rights categorically lack protection against unconstitutional conditions.

Against those obstacles, the petition presents a final published appellate judgment, a focused federal question, and an explicit passage declining to extend the indirect-coercion principle to the parental right. The individual statutory benefit and pleaded loss of education alongside nondisabled peers make the challenge more substantial than a demand that the government fund private schooling from scratch. Six supporting briefs and the response request supply case-specific evidence of attention. Together with the issue's reach, those considerations justify moving well above 5.111% and modestly above the 2+ amicus population's descriptive rate. The imperfect conflict, competing non-subsidy framing, and absence of the response keep the final number at **28%, not a likely grant**. That adjustment is judgment, not an estimated response-request coefficient.
