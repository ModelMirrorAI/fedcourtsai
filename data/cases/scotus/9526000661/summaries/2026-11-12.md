## In brief

James Pavatt asked the Court to pause his execution while it considered his death sentence appeal; the Court denied the stay and denied review.

## What happened

James Pavatt was convicted of murder in Oklahoma and sentenced to death in 2003. Decades later, he filed a new post-conviction application in Oklahoma courts arguing that prosecutors had used knowingly false information at his sentencing, which a juror's affidavit said influenced his vote for death. The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals denied this claim as procedurally barred, finding the facts underlying it had been discoverable for over twenty years. With his execution scheduled for November 12, 2026, Pavatt asked the Supreme Court both to hear his case and to stay the execution in the meantime.

## What the Court is being asked

Pavatt's underlying petition asks whether the Constitution requires an exception to a state rule that normally bars using juror statements to challenge a verdict, when new evidence shows prosecutors gave a jury false information that affected at least one juror's vote for death. The stay application separately asks whether Pavatt has shown enough likelihood of success and enough harm from execution to justify pausing it while the Court considers that question.

## Where it stands

Justice Gorsuch referred the stay application to the full Court, which denied the stay of execution and denied the petition for certiorari on November 12, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Pavatt, the applicant, sought both a stay and Supreme Court review; Oklahoma opposed both. The Court's denial of the stay means no pause was granted, and its denial of certiorari leaves the Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals' ruling against Pavatt in place, without the Supreme Court deciding the underlying constitutional questions. The denial of certiorari is not a ruling that Oklahoma's procedural bar or Pavatt's claims were correctly decided; it only leaves the lower court's decision standing.
