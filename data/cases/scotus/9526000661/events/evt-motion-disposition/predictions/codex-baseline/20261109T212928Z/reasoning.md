## Case-specific adjustment

The application, pp. 1–7, asks the Court to extend the constitutional exception to rules barring post-verdict juror testimony. It alleges that prosecutors knowingly misrepresented who fired the fatal shots, and that the foreman's later account establishes the representation's decisive effect on his own vote and his influence over other jurors. These are the applicant's allegations, not factual findings I have verified. The application, pp. 8–9, connects them to due process and the prohibition on knowingly false evidence or argument. An imminent execution makes the irreparable injury exceptionally strong, and the alleged misconduct, if established, is serious enough to justify moving above the matching private-capital rate.

The main difficulty is the legal bridge, not irreparability. **Peña-Rodriguez v. Colorado, 580 U.S. 206, 221–25 (2017)**, distinguishes racial bias through its particular historical, constitutional, and institutional features; CourtListener's retrieved majority text confirms that rationale. My inference is that extending its juror-testimony exception to prosecutorial deception about the shooter's identity asks for a material doctrinal step, rather than straightforward application of the existing exception. The foreman's description concerns internal sentencing deliberations. The filing identifies no developed split over this precise proposed exception. It also does not independently establish why the asserted misconduct cannot be assessed through nonjuror evidence and an objective prejudice analysis. Those are obstacles to obtaining the necessary support for review and a stay, not findings that the underlying allegations are untrue.

The application, pp. 6–7, invokes **Storey v. Lumpkin, 142 S. Ct. 2576 (2022)** and Justice Sotomayor's statement respecting denial. Its account supports the possibility of individual concern, while illustrating that concern over prosecutorial conduct need not produce review. Applicant argues that Storey's federal habeas vehicle problems are absent here because this case arrives directly from a state court. I credit that distinction modestly, but do not infer that all possible procedural barriers have disappeared without the state decision or petition. The legal stay standard quoted in the application from **Barefoot v. Estelle, 463 U.S. 880, 895 (1983)** requires more than inevitable irreparable harm: a sufficient prospect of review and reversal remains the limiting factor. Taken together, these considerations support 6%, not a near-zero figure, but do not overcome the denial-heavy matching population.
