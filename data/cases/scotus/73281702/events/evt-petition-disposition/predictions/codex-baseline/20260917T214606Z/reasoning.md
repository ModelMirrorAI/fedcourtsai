## Petition, opposition, and reply

The petition's strongest argument is that incorporation after Ramos removes one rationale Richardson used to distinguish state continuous-abuse cases. It also argues that combining independently criminal acts into a series cannot evade constitutionally required agreement about what a defendant actually did. See petition pp. 5–13; September 15 reply pp. 1–8. The reply sharpens the distinction between alternative methods of one crime and disagreement about separate crimes, and disputes Texas's claim that only due process, rather than the incorporated jury right, is implicated. It does not identify a contrary holding creating a developed interjurisdictional split.

Texas offers a substantial competing reading: the jury must agree on the continuous pattern, while individual acts supply alternative means; the statutory definition is explicit; and the setting involves special proof difficulties. Its BIO invokes Schad, Richardson, and Mathis and identifies aligned post-Ramos authority, including State v. Tran. I treat the claimed absence of contrary authority as a statement about the presented record, not an independently exhaustive survey. See BIO pp. 10–23.

I verified the disputed Richardson passage directly through CourtListener: *Richardson v. United States*, 526 U.S. 813, 820–22 (1999), opinion 118295. The majority discusses both the special evidentiary difficulties of child-abuse prosecutions and the then-unsettled incorporation of unanimity. That supports the petition's contention that subsequent incorporation matters, but it is not a holding invalidating Texas's statute. Conversely, the passage is not an unqualified constitutional endorsement of every continuous-abuse statute. This interpretive space makes plenary review more plausible than summary correction if the Court intervenes.

The lower opinion expressly treated Trice's unspecified challenge as **facial**, applying a demanding facial-validity standard. See petition appendix pp. 22a–23a and n.6. That is a vehicle disadvantage relative to a tightly preserved challenge to instructions on particular predicate acts, although I do not infer a forfeiture of the constitutional issue: the appendix addresses it on the merits. The separate juror-disagreement narrative should not be confused with a finding that jurors disagreed over predicate acts.
