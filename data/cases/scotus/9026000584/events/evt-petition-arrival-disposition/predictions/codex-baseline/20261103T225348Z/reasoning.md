## Prior and adjustments

The frozen `cert_anchor.rate` is 0.23515439429928742 under `sal-v6`, with band `state` and a ten-Term lookback. That is the appropriate state-petitioner arrival anchor. The salience table in `metrics/statpack.md` reports prior-Term state-band reached denominators totaling 421 for 2017–2025, consistent with an approximately 23.5% pooled grant-family rate. I exclude the current 2026 row rather than conditioning on this Term's partial outcomes. I do not substitute the terminal state-band rate, the general paid/private rate, or the zero-relist bucket for the arrival anchor.

The all-modern-cert table gives only about 2.8% grants plus GVRs across its much broader population. The Fourth Circuit origin cut similarly has approximately 2.6% grants plus GVRs among 3,436 resolved cases. Those are background checks, not appropriate replacements for the caption-class anchor. The case-specific adjustments below are judgment, not fitted likelihood ratios.

**Upward: a concrete conflict, now including a federal appellate holding.** The petition's first ground identifies Tennessee and Indiana on the government-speech side and Maryland and the Fourth Circuit on the private-speech side (petition pp. 7–10). The Fourth Circuit itself acknowledges this division, so it is not merely the petitioner's characterization (`decision-below.txt`, opinion pp. 10–11). A targeted CourtListener lookup independently confirms Tennessee's government-speech holding in *Gilliam v. Gerregano*, No. M2022-00083-SC-R11-CV (Tenn. Feb. 26, 2025), opinion ID 10806629. Another confirms Maryland's private-speech holding in *Mitchell v. Maryland Motor Vehicle Administration*, 148 A.3d 319 (2016), opinion ID 4093604. The dispute concerns a threshold First Amendment classification, not just the interpretation of one state's list of disallowed words.

**Upward: recurring stakes and an expressly unresolved boundary.** The question is whether privately selected registration characters on state-issued plates are government speech, not whether specialty plate designs are government speech. *Walker v. Texas Division, Sons of Confederate Veterans*, 576 U.S. 200 (2015), limits its inquiry to specialty designs rather than the personalization program; that distinction was verified in CourtListener opinion ID 2809762. The petition presents a plausible reason for further review, but Walker's different holding does not itself establish an obvious error warranting summary reversal. Virginia's claim that this question affects other states is reinforced by the conflicting decisions; I do not need to treat every program's mechanics as identical to recognize a substantial recurring issue.

**Downward: the interlocutory vehicle is a substantial, not cosmetic, obstacle.** The Fourth Circuit vacated a dismissal and remanded; it did not finally invalidate Virginia's restrictions or order the plate restored. Its concluding discussion leaves forum classification and the reasonableness/viewpoint-neutrality analysis to the district court (`decision-below.txt`, pp. 27–28; appendix pp. 34a–36a). Maryland's *Mitchell* decision is especially instructive: it treated the message as private speech yet upheld the restriction in a nonpublic forum. That independently verified holding makes Virginia's potential success on remand concrete. The Supreme Court can take this interlocutory legal issue, but it need not do so before knowing whether the disputed classification changes the result. The petition's argument that its legal holding is settled and may evade a later adverse judgment is meaningful, not dispositive (petition pp. 18–20).

**Downward: limited percolation and a recent related denial.** The corpus lookup for *Gilliam*, Supreme Court No. 25-107, reports a denial on December 8, 2025. That is a different petition's lawful historical context, not this case's outcome. It does not establish approval of Tennessee's rule. The new federal appellate decision makes this petition stronger than a simple rerun, but the prior denial tempers a claim that the underlying split alone makes review probable. The petition also reports a pending Seventh Circuit appeal argued September 17, 2026 (petition pp. 10, 16); I used that as a petition-reported possibility of further percolation and did not retrieve its subsequent history. The lower-court docket summary finds no rehearing petition in the three entries it examined, but its narrow coverage does not prove there was no possible additional rehearing record. The published panel opinion was unanimous, rather than accompanied by an appellate dissent.

Together, the developed split and national recurrence warrant a modest but material increase from the 23.5% state anchor to 32%. The remand posture, the possibility of further federal appellate development, and the earlier related denial keep the forecast well below even odds. The main uncertainty is whether the Court regards the fixed threshold holding as sufficient reason to overlook the unfinished case. I have not inferred any merits majority or ideological cert coalition from the subject matter.

## Additional claims

The `relist-increment` probability is 0.99 because the frozen distribution count is zero: the mechanical claim asks whether there will be at least one distribution from here, not whether the Court will revisit a petition after its first conference. Separately, I put the chance of at least one repeat distribution after the first at roughly 44%; that is an auxiliary procedural judgment, not the declared claim's number. Most likely the petition is denied after its first conference; review, if granted, is more likely to follow additional consideration.

The paid-segment terminal relist cut has 10,204 resolved zero-relist petitions, 2,532 with one, 495 with two, and 482 with three or more. Their approximate grant-family shares are 1.8%, 13.5%, 41.2%, and 37.1%, respectively. Those figures describe where cases ended and are not forward hazards or this arrival's grant anchor. The CVSG cut has 164 resolved CVSG petitions with about 34.8% grants plus GVRs, versus 13,549 without a CVSG and about 6.4%. This is a domestic constitutional dispute with little need for the federal executive's specialized policy views, so I forecast a low 3.5% chance of a new CVSG, not the grant rate conditional on one.

There are zero amicus entries in this arrival snapshot, but I do not treat that as a final lack of support. The amicus cut's zero bucket has 11,109 resolved petitions and approximately 3.8% grants plus GVRs; its one-brief bucket has 1,158 and approximately 14.8%; its two-or-more bucket has 1,446 and approximately 22.7%. These are terminal, marginal populations. They do not justify penalizing a newly docketed petition for briefs that could still arrive.

Conditional on a grant, I assign 9% to a summary disposition: there is no identified intervening controlling decision requiring a GVR, and the disputed Walker boundary calls for plenary treatment more naturally than a summary merits ruling. Conditional on denial, I assign 8% to a noted dissent or statement respecting denial. The per-Justice order-writing probabilities are small, independent forecasts of authorship, not cert votes or probabilities of joining another Justice's writing. They allow a minority possibility of a short writing without predicting one as the modal outcome.

The stakes score is 0.50: the legal boundary affects state identification programs and expressive freedoms nationally, but the immediate subject is narrower than a case restructuring a major nationwide regulatory regime. This score measures significance if decided, not the 32% chance of review.
