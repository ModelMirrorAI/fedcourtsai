## Why above the anchor, but below one-half

**The split is substantial and unusually direct.** The petition identifies Tennessee's Gilliam and Indiana's Vawter as treating owner-selected registration characters as government speech, versus Maryland's Mitchell and the Fourth Circuit treating them as private speech. The Fourth Circuit independently describes the disagreement in its opinion, including the contrary state-high-court decisions. CourtListener retrieval independently confirmed Gilliam's government-speech holding and Mitchell's express rejection of Vawter's reasoning. This is not merely a party announcing a split between dissimilar holdings. Federal appellate and state-high-court disagreement supplies a real review rationale even without a second federal circuit decision.

**The question is preserved, recurring, and separable.** The district court dismissed on government-speech grounds; the published appellate holding definitively rejects that ground. The question concerns a statewide program with more than 930,000 personalized plates according to the opinion, and similar programs elsewhere. The petition also identifies a Seventh Circuit appeal argued September 17, 2026; I treat its pendency as the petition's report and have not retrieved its later history. These features support a material increase over an undifferentiated state-petitioner prior.

**The remand remains the central reason not to predict a grant.** The court of appeals did not hold that Whateley must receive his chosen plate. It left forum classification, reasonableness, and viewpoint discrimination to the district court. Its own discussion of Mitchell illustrates the problem for immediate review: speech can be private while a particular restriction remains lawful. Virginia may therefore win after remand without a Supreme Court ruling on government speech. The petition's point that a favorable final judgment could leave Virginia unable to challenge the adverse rationale is real, but does not make interlocutory intervention inevitable. Petition pp. 18–20 offer examples of review after remand; they show possibility, not that this threshold issue demands immediate review. There is also a plausible incentive to await another appellate decision or a final judgment.

**An arguable merits disagreement is not a clear summary-reversal case.** Walker v. Texas Division, Sons of Confederate Veterans, Inc., 576 U.S. 200, 204 (2015), expressly confined its decision to specialty designs, not personalized character combinations; I verified that limitation in CourtListener opinion 2809762. The Fourth Circuit applied Walker, Shurtleff, and Matal rather than disregarding a directly controlling holding. Its reasoning about authorship and public attribution has support in the contrary side of the split. Virginia's ownership, registration-function, and simultaneous-message arguments deserve plenary consideration if review occurs, but do not identify an obvious intervening decision or an indisputable error suited to a routine GVR or summary reversal. These considerations produce my judgmental **36%**, 12.5 percentage points above the frozen anchor, rather than a purported fitted estimate.
