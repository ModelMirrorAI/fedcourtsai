I put the chance of any grant at **18%**. The starting point is the frozen 23.5% grant rate for paid petitions that reached the state-petitioner band in earlier Terms. The state's zero-amicus reference is 13.6% (220 resolved petitions). That group ended without an amicus brief. Virginia has none as of November 27, while the December 3 response deadline is still ahead. The broader Fourth Circuit rate, about 2.8% including grants to vacate and remand (3,287 resolved petitions), gives less weight to Virginia's position as a state defending its own program. Sources are `record/context.json`, the November 27 snapshot and `metrics/statpack.md`.

Virginia has a concrete reason for review. Tennessee in *Gilliam* and Indiana in *Vawter* treated driver-selected registration characters as government speech. Maryland in *Mitchell* and now the Fourth Circuit treat the driver's message as private speech. The classification matters because a state speaking for itself can choose its message, while regulation of a driver's speech requires a First Amendment justification. *Walker* concerned state-approved specialty plate designs and expressly reserved personalized numbers. The Court denied the Tennessee petition on December 8, 2025. Virginia now brings the first published post-*Walker* federal appellate decision squarely taking a side. The Seventh Circuit heard argument on the Wisconsin program in September. The CourtListener searches yielded no appellate opinion there, so I count a reason to await another court's analysis and give Virginia no credit for a conflict between two federal circuits. See `questions-presented.txt`, petition pp. 6–10, 15–20, and the *Gilliam* corpus lookup in `retrieval.md`.

The strongest reason for denial is what remains for the district judge. The published, unanimous opinion vacated dismissal of Whateley's challenge. It settled who speaks for purposes of Virginia's program but left open both what kind of forum Virginia created and whether the recall of “FTP&ATF” was permissible there. Maryland, for example, upheld a restriction even after classifying a driver's message as private. Virginia could therefore win this suit on remand. Its best answer is that a win would leave it bound by the Fourth Circuit's classification without an adverse final judgment to appeal. That gives the petition more force than an ordinary request for review midway through a case, but the Court can still await a final judgment squarely turning on the issue. The appendix (2a–7a, 34a–36a) and `decision-below.txt` agree on the July 31 judgment in No. 25-1751. The stakes score reflects the reach of a ruling across state registration programs, including over 930,000 personalized plates in Virginia alone.

The November 27 snapshot shows one distribution, zero relists, and no request for a response or for the Solicitor General's views. Whateley's November 20 waiver letter promises a response only if requested and gives no argument on the merits. The paid-segment tables show most resolved petitions ended with zero relists (10,311 of 13,840) and only 166 of 13,840 had an invitation to the Solicitor General. I put another distribution at 44% because the concrete conflict could prompt a call for a response and a return to conference even if the Court ultimately denies review. Federal input adds little on administration of state plates. *Walker* left this issue open, making full argument much more plausible than summary action if the Court grants. The recent denial in *Gilliam* and the procedural reason to wait support a low chance of a public objection to denial here.
