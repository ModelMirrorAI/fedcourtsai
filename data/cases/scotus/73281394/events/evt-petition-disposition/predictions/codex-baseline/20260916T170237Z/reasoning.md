## Adjustment to 16%

**Reasons to take the petition seriously.** Utah is the petitioner, represented by its solicitor general. The state supreme court divided 3–2 on how Miller, Montgomery, and Jones fit together. The petition's argument, particularly pages 8–21, identifies a real doctrinal tension between discretionary procedure and substantive limits after a finding of corrigibility. Its closest asserted contrary authority includes United States v. Grant; the BIO's effort to distinguish Grant on adequate consideration of youth does not establish that the underlying doctrinal disagreement is imaginary. The sentencing court had discretion and received mitigation evidence according to the petition, pages 20–21. These points sustain meaningful grant and summary-reversal possibilities rather than a near-zero forecast.

**Why below the state anchor.** The BIO, pages 6–8, raises a substantial finality objection: the state court vacated the sentence and ordered resentencing, with a new sentence still possible. Its application of the Cox exceptions is advocacy, not a jurisdictional ruling I treat as settled, but it identifies an avoidable threshold obstacle. The August reply is not provisioned, so I cannot assess Utah's latest response to it.

More importantly, the BIO's alternative-ground argument has support in the appended opinion itself. Petition Appendix A, paragraphs 78–82, pages 45a–47a, says there was no affirmative finding of corrigibility and separately questions whether the sentencing court properly considered youth rather than leaving that assessment to the parole board. The BIO, pages 14–17, says this unchallenged ground independently sustains resentencing. Utah argues the remand depends on the disputed federal interpretation (petition pages 20–22 and footnote 9). I do not assume the state procedural rule is an adequate independent state ground; the narrower concern is that the federal question presented might not dispose of the actual remand rationale even if Utah wins it.

The alleged 16–8 split also needs discounting. The BIO, pages 9–14, distinguishes cases involving no affirmative corrigibility finding, waiver, pre-Jones decisions, parole-eligible sentences, and independent state constitutional protections. I have not independently verified every cited decision, so I treat these as competing descriptions rather than a fully established tally. The BIO's account of Utah's prospective abolition of juvenile LWOP and limited remaining cases further reduces practical urgency, though it does not eliminate the national constitutional stakes. No amicus entry appears in the snapshot.

Jones v. Mississippi, 593 U.S. 98 (2021), majority slip opinion page 21, independently retrieved through CourtListener, requires discretionary sentencing but expressly leaves an as-applied disproportionality claim unaddressed. That limits confidence that this case is a mechanical summary-reversal candidate. Overall, the doctrinal attraction keeps the estimate material, while finality, ambiguous sentencing findings, and the potentially surviving remand ground move my assessment from 22.7% to **16%**. This adjustment is judgment, not a fitted model or measured likelihood ratio.
