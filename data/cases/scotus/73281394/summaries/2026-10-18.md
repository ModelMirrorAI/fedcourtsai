## In brief

Utah asks the Supreme Court to decide whether a judge may sentence a juvenile murderer to life without parole despite suggesting the juvenile could change.

## What happened

Morris Mullins was sentenced to life without parole for a murder he committed at 17. Decades later, he challenged the sentence, arguing it was illegal under later Supreme Court rulings limiting such sentences for juveniles. A divided Utah Supreme Court agreed and vacated his sentence, reasoning that the sentencing judge's comment expressing hope Mullins would have "a chance to change" raised concerns the judge believed Mullins capable of reform, making a life-without-parole sentence improper. Two justices dissented. Utah's request for rehearing was denied, and Utah asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's ban on cruel and unusual punishment forbids a sentencing judge from imposing life without parole on a juvenile convicted of murder once the judge has found, or suggested, that the juvenile is capable of change rather than permanently incorrigible. Mullins responds that the Court lacks authority to hear the case now because no final sentence has been imposed, and separately argues there is no real disagreement among lower courts on the question.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference multiple times, most recently for the October 16, 2026 conference, after earlier conferences in September and October 2026 were followed by rescheduling. The docket does not show any decision on the petition as of the snapshot date.

## What each outcome would mean

Utah, as petitioner, asks the Court to take up the case and reverse the Utah Supreme Court's decision. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the constitutional question itself, without deciding in advance who wins. If it denies review, the Utah Supreme Court's decision vacating Mullins's sentence stands, and his case returns to the sentencing court for a new sentencing proceeding. Mullins opposes review, arguing among other things that the Court lacks jurisdiction because his case has not reached a final sentence.
