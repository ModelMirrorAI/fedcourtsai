## In brief

Utah asks the Supreme Court to decide whether a juvenile convicted of murder can still receive life without parole even if the sentencing court suggested he could change.

## What happened

In 2002, Morris Mullins was sentenced to life without parole for a murder committed when he was 17, after the sentencing judge expressed hope he would have "a chance to change." Years later, Mullins argued this sentence was illegal under later Supreme Court rulings limiting when juveniles can receive that punishment. The Utah Supreme Court, divided 3-2, vacated his sentence, ruling there was a significant risk the sentencing judge had suggested Mullins was capable of change, which the majority believed made a life-without-parole sentence unconstitutional. Two justices dissented, reading the Supreme Court's precedents differently. The case was sent back for resentencing. Utah then asked the U.S. Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's ban on cruel and unusual punishment forbids sentencing a juvenile to life without parole whenever a court finds, or even just suggests, that the juvenile could change and is not permanently beyond hope of rehabilitation, or whether courts may still impose that sentence as long as they considered the juvenile's youth during sentencing.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026, after full briefing including a brief in opposition and a reply. The docket shows the case was rescheduled on that date, with no record yet of the Court's action on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Utah, which lost below, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the underlying question, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Utah Supreme Court's decision vacating Mullins's sentence stands, and his case would proceed to resentencing in Utah courts. Mullins's brief argues the Court lacks jurisdiction because there is no final sentence yet, and that no other court has actually decided the same question the same way.
