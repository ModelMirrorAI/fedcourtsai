## In brief

Utah asks the Court to decide whether a sentencing judge can still give a juvenile murderer life without parole even after suggesting the juvenile could change.

## What happened

Morris Mullins was sentenced to life without parole for a murder he committed at 17. Years later, citing Supreme Court rulings that limit such sentences for juveniles, he challenged his sentence in Utah courts. The Utah Supreme Court, divided 3-2, vacated his sentence, reasoning that the original sentencing judge's remark expressing hope Mullins would have "a chance to change" raised serious doubts about whether the judge properly weighed Mullins's youth as the Constitution requires. The court sent the case back for a new sentencing. Utah asked for rehearing, which was denied, and then asked the Supreme Court to review the decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the Constitution's ban on cruel and unusual punishment forbids sentencing a juvenile to life without parole whenever a judge's comments suggest the juvenile might be capable of change, even without a formal finding to that effect, or whether judges retain discretion to impose that sentence anyway as long as they considered the offender's youth.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference multiple times, most recently for the October 9, 2026 conference, and the docket shows the case was rescheduled rather than acted on at the two most recent conferences. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Utah, which lost in the Utah Supreme Court, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide the constitutional question itself, which would decide nothing in advance for either side. If the Court denies review, the Utah Supreme Court's decision vacating Mullins's sentence stands, and his case returns to the sentencing court for a new sentencing hearing. Mullins's brief also argues the Court lacks jurisdiction because there is no final sentence yet, a separate issue the Court could consider in deciding whether to hear the case.
