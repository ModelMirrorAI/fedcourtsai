## Record and information boundary

This is a forward, cert-stage distribution cell. I read the case-level snapshot `record/snapshots/2026-10-25.json`, the event definition, `record/context.json`, the questions presented, the document manifest, substantive portions of the petition, and Appendix A's full Ninth Circuit memorandum. The provisioned conditioning is `baseline` under `sal-v4`, Term 2026, one distribution, and no CVSG. I use that band without reclassifying the case. The baseline has no cutoff; the September response request and the extensions through October 20 are legitimate provisioned information, not later evidence fetched around a frozen moment.

The petition asks about uninjured members in Rule 23(b)(3) damages classes. The case concerns allegedly unlawful ten-cent bottle deposits. Kroger contends that pre-suit refunds left many purchasers without redressable injury. The Ninth Circuit's unpublished memorandum treats the named plaintiffs' standing as sufficient at certification and, separately, upholds predominance because sorting purchasers by refund status was not an especially involved inquiry. These are the appellate court's holdings, not an acceptance of every characterization in the petition.

`documents.json` lists usable, untruncated petition and appendix text, neither marked OCR-derived. It lists no brief in opposition, and none was provisioned. This is expected rather than a provisioning failure: the snapshot extends the response deadline to December 4, 2026. I therefore have the petitioner's advocacy and the lower court's account, not the respondents' cert-stage answer. No companion snapshot was provisioned or identified in context. I did not retrieve this case's disposition, subsequent docket, or other predictions, and I do not know its outcome.

This is a private petitioner's arrival/reached-baseline population, not the 1%-range terminal-baseline rate and not the overall historical SCOTUS mixture. ## Why move to 44%?

The adjustment above the roughly 5% anchor is large and deliberately case-specific. First, the question is not merely an advocate's claim of importance: the Court previously granted review of the same central issue in Laboratory Corp. of America Holdings v. Davis, 605 U.S. 327 (2025). I verified that prior decision through CourtListener, opinion 11065594, dated June 5, 2025. The Court dismissed the writ as improvidently granted. Kavanaugh's dissent attributes the dismissal to a threshold mootness issue and favors deciding the Rule 23 question. The Court's unexplained order itself does not establish his account of the majority's reasoning. This is evidence of prior institutional interest, not evidence that four Justices have committed to this petition or that the underlying legal answer is settled.

Second, the current petition alleges an entrenched division over whether absent-member injury is an Article III certification requirement, a predominance constraint with a de minimis allowance, or an issue manageable later. The appended Ninth Circuit decision directly rejects the restrictive Article III approach and relies on Olean for its Rule 23 analysis. That supplies a concrete conflict-facing ruling rather than merely an abstract policy dispute. Third, the petition's stated Rule 23(f) posture and continuing trial-court stay offer a facially more stable vehicle than the earlier proceeding. Fourth, the Court requested a response immediately after the initial distribution despite respondents' waiver. That is affirmative interest, stronger evidence than the waiver itself. Two filed business-group amicus entries corroborate broader practical stakes, although I did not read their briefs and do not count their existence as independent proof of certworthiness.

Several considerations keep the forecast below 50%. The lower decision is an unpublished application of existing precedent rather than a new circuit-wide rule. More importantly, the premise that the disputed class includes genuinely uninjured people is contestable: the panel emphasizes an actual common overcharge and relatively straightforward refund categories, while Kroger emphasizes missing records and contested individual proof. A disagreement over managing refunds may make this less clean than the categorical question suggests. The petition itself acknowledges other recent denials in this issue area; I have not independently retrieved those cases and treat that acknowledgment only as a reason not to assume prior interest forces a new grant. No opposition is yet available to test preservation, injury, class-definition, and vehicle objections. Nor is the response request a grant commitment. A future response could materially move this forecast in either direction.

Thus 44% reflects unusually strong issue-level interest with unresolved vehicle risk, rather than extrapolating an ordinary baseline petition's chances. Denial remains only narrowly favored. This subjective adjustment is not an empirically fitted response-request model.

## Other claims and significance

The 98% distribution increment is driven primarily by the interrupted briefing timetable: a requested response due after the listed conference ordinarily necessitates a later distribution. It should not be interpreted as the terminal relist table's hazard. The 2.5% CVSG estimate reflects little evident need for executive-branch input in this private procedural dispute. The 6% summary-route probability is conditional on a grant, not an unconditional 6% chance; an unresolved general legal question points to plenary review. The 16% writing probability is conditional on denial and allows for renewed concern from a Justice who has already addressed the subject without assuming that such concern produces a public writing every time.

The significance score is 0.79. The immediate consumer dispute is small, but a categorical rule for injury and damages-class certification could affect many subject areas and settlement incentives. This is an assessment of stakes if decided, separate from the 44% grant probability.
