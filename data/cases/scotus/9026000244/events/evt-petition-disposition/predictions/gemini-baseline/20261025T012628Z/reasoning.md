Petitioner The Kroger Co., doing business as Fred Meyer, Inc. ("Fred Meyer"), seeks review of an unpublished three-paragraph Ninth Circuit order denying discretionary permission under Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 23(f) to appeal a District of Oregon order certifying a Rule 23(b)(3) damages class. The petition frames a single question presented that is verbatim the question this Court granted certiorari to resolve in *Laboratory Corp. of America Holdings v. Davis*, 145 S. Ct. 1133 (2025), before dismissing that case as improvidently granted (*Labcorp*, 605 U.S. 327 (2025)): whether a federal court may certify a class action pursuant to Rule 23(b)(3) when some members of the proposed class lack any Article III injury.

Although the underlying doctrinal question is important, divides the courts of appeals into three camps, and is backed here by experienced Supreme Court counsel and two major business amicus briefs, *Solano* is a markedly poor vehicle for plenary review. First, the decision below is a summary, unpublished denial of discretionary interlocutory review under Rule 23(f) rather than a published appellate opinion addressing Article III or Rule 23(b)(3) on the merits. Second, the district court explicitly found that *every* member of the certified class suffered a concrete, ascertainable loss at the moment of purchase under Oregon law and Ninth Circuit standing precedent—meaning the case is mired in a threshold factual and state-law dispute over whether the class contains any uninjured members at all. Third, several published circuit opinions decided after *Labcorp* (including *Healy v. Milliman, Inc.*, 157 F.4th 1091 (9th Cir. 2025), and *Speerly v. General Motors, LLC*, 143 F.4th 306 (6th Cir. 2025) (en banc)) address the Rule 23(b)(3) predominance and standing framework directly on the merits without the Rule 23(f) and state-law obstacles present here. We therefore forecast denial of certiorari (`probability: 0.09`, reflecting roughly a 6% chance of plenary review and a 3% chance of a hold-and-GVR if the Court grants another vehicle on the *Labcorp* question).

## 3. Case Background and Procedural Posture

Under Oregon's "Bottle Bill," ORS 459A.700 *et seq.*, retailers must charge a refundable 10-cent deposit on covered beverage containers. Effective January 1, 2018, the Oregon Liquor Control Commission (OLCC) expanded the Bottle Bill to cover fruit juice containers between 4 ounces and 1.5 liters. Between January 1, 2018 and May 2018, eighteen Fred Meyer stores in Oregon erroneously charged the 10-cent bottle deposit on two varieties of half-gallon (64-ounce, or ~1.89-liter) Kroger Freshly Squeezed Orange Juice ("FNOJ 564" and "FNOJ 567"), which were exempt because they exceeded 1.5 liters (App. 5a, 7a–10a). In total, Fred Meyer sold roughly 11,452 half-gallon containers with the erroneous 10-cent charge—amounting to approximately $1,145 in actual overcharges across the entire putative class—before correcting the coding error in May 2018 (Pet. 6; App. 10a).

Respondents Elisha Solano, Kathleen Zack, and Denise Conroy filed this putative diversity class action in the District of Oregon asserting claims for (1) knowing or reckless violations of Oregon's Unlawful Trade Practices Act (UTPA), ORS 646.608(1)(s) and 646.638(1), which provides statutory damages of $200 per violation, and (2) common-law unjust enrichment (App. 12a–13a). Because statutory damages are $200 per violation, certification transforms $1,145 in 10-cent deposits into potential statutory damages exceeding $2.29 million plus punitive damages and attorney's fees (Pet. 6–7).

Fred Meyer opposed class certification and moved to deny certification, arguing that many putative class members suffered no Article III injury-in-fact (and no "ascertainable loss" under the UTPA) because customers could—and many did—either obtain a 10-cent refund at a Fred Meyer customer service desk or redeem the empty containers for 10 cents at BottleDrop redemption facilities after Fred Meyer temporarily instructed OLCC's container cooperative (OBRC) to accept FNOJ barcode redemptions (Pet. 7–8; App. 32a–35a).

On July 16, 2024, Magistrate Judge Jeff Armistead issued Findings and Recommendation recommending class certification under Rule 23(b)(3) of a class defined as all persons who, between January 1 and May 31, 2018, paid a bottle deposit on FNOJ 564 or 567 at an Oregon Fred Meyer store using a Fred Meyer Rewards card (App. 20a, 51a). Crucially, the Magistrate Judge rejected Fred Meyer's standing argument on two grounds:
1. Under Oregon UTPA precedent (*Pearson v. Philip Morris, Inc.*, 358 Or. 88 (2015); *Sharfstein v. BP W. Coast Prods.*, 292 Or. App. 69 (2018)) and Ninth Circuit Article III precedent (*Van v. LLR, Inc.*, 962 F.3d 1160, 1162–65 (9th Cir. 2020); *Ruiz Torres v. Mercer Canyons Inc.*, 835 F.3d 1125, 1137 (9th Cir. 2016)), **every class member suffered an immediate, concrete injury and ascertainable loss of 10 cents at the point of purchase** when overcharged, as well as the lost time-value of money or the burden of returning the bottle; whether any class member subsequently obtained a refund or redeemed the bottle goes only to *damages* offset, not *liability* or Article III injury (App. 34a–36a, 45a–48a).
2. Even if some class members were uninjured, Ninth Circuit precedent (*Olean Wholesale Grocery Coop., Inc. v. Bumble Bee Foods LLC*, 31 F.4th 651, 669 & n.12 (9th Cir. 2022) (en banc)) does not preclude certifying a class that arguably includes more than a *de minimis* number of uninjured members so long as common questions of liability predominate (App. 34a–38a).

On January 13, 2025, District Judge Michael W. Mosman adopted the Findings and Recommendation in full, emphasizing:
> "Defendant contends that its customers who received a refund through the state redemption system or from a Fred Meyer store were not injured. ... Both the caselaw and common sense contradict Defendant's theory of injury. In order to receive the ten-cent refund, Defendant's customers had to suffer the burden of returning the bottle ... or lose the time value of their money, as small an amount as it may be. Each of the putative class members suffered a concrete injury the moment they were wrongly charged the ten-cent fee." (App. 5a.)

Fred Meyer petitioned the Ninth Circuit for permission to appeal under Rule 23(f). The Ninth Circuit stayed consideration of the Rule 23(f) petition pending this Court's decision in *Labcorp* and the Ninth Circuit's decision in *Healy v. Milliman, Inc.*, No. 24-337. After *Labcorp* was DIG'd on June 5, 2025 and the Ninth Circuit decided *Healy v. Milliman, Inc.*, 157 F.4th 1091 (9th Cir. Oct. 24, 2025), the Ninth Circuit lifted the stay on March 26, 2026 and denied Fred Meyer's Rule 23(f) petition in a single sentence citing *Chamberlan v. Ford Motor Co.*, 402 F.3d 952, 959 (9th Cir. 2005) (App. 1a–2a). Rehearing en banc was denied on May 28, 2026 (App. 6a).

## 4. Analysis of Certiorari Factors

### A. Factors Weighing in Favor of Certiorari
1. **The Question Presented Was Previously Granted in *Labcorp* and Remains Unresolved**: In *Labcorp*, this Court granted certiorari on the exact question presented here (145 S. Ct. 1133 (2025)) before dismissing the writ as improvidently granted due to procedural complications arising from a superseded class certification order (605 U.S. 327 (2025)). Justice Kavanaugh's dissent from the DIG (joined by Justices Thomas and Gorsuch) confirms that at least three Justices are eager to resolve the question and hold that Rule 23(b)(3)'s predominance requirement bars certifying classes when individualized injury inquiries overwhelm common questions.
2. **Deep, Acknowledged Three-Way Circuit Split**: As detailed in the petition (Pet. 13–21), the courts of appeals are intractably divided over how Article III and Rule 23(b)(3) apply when a proposed damages class potentially includes uninjured members:
   - *Strict Article III Bar (2d & 8th Circuits)*: A class "cannot be certified if it contains members who lack standing" (*Denney v. Deutsche Bank AG*, 443 F.3d 253, 264 (2d Cir. 2006); *Johannessohn v. Polaris Indus. Inc.*, 9 F.4th 981, 987 (8th Cir. 2021)).
   - *De Minimis Ceiling (1st & D.C. Circuits)*: Rule 23(b)(3) predominance bars certification if the class includes more than a *de minimis* number (roughly 5–10%) of uninjured members without an administratively feasible mechanism to weed them out before trial (*In re Asacol Antitrust Litig.*, 907 F.3d 42, 53–58 (1st Cir. 2018); *In re Rail Freight Fuel Surcharge Antitrust Litig.*, 934 F.3d 619, 624–25 (D.C. Cir. 2019)).
   - *Permissive Predominance Standard (9th Circuit and others)*: Rejecting both a categorical Article III bar and a *de minimis* ceiling at the certification stage (*Olean*, 31 F.4th at 669; reaffirmed post-*Labcorp* in *Healy v. Milliman, Inc.*, 157 F.4th 1091, 1101–04 (9th Cir. 2025)).
3. **High-Caliber Counsel and Coordinated Business Amici**: The petition is signed by Joseph R. Palmore and James R. Sigel of Morrison & Foerster LLP, and supported by two cert-stage amicus briefs from repeat institutional players (the Chamber of Commerce, ATRA, and Business Roundtable; and The Retail Litigation Center).

### B. Factors Weighing Strongly Against Certiorari (Fatal Vehicle Flaws)
1. **Unpublished Denial of Discretionary Rule 23(f) Permission to Appeal**: The order under review is not a Ninth Circuit merits decision affirming class certification; it is a one-sentence, unpublished order denying permission to appeal under Rule 23(f) (App. 1a–2a). Under Rule 23(f), a court of appeals has broad, unfettered discretion whether to permit an interlocutory appeal at all (*Microsoft Corp. v. Baker*, 582 U.S. 23, 30–31 (2017); *Chamberlan*, 402 F.3d at 959). Although 28 U.S.C. § 1254(1) gives this Court jurisdiction over cases "in" the courts of appeals once a Rule 23(f) petition is docketed, the Court almost never grants plenary review from a circuit court's discretionary refusal to grant Rule 23(f) leave, because:
   - There is no appellate opinion below analyzing the question presented or applying Rule 23(b)(3) to the record; and
   - Strictly speaking, the only question decided by the Ninth Circuit was whether the district court's certification order warranted immediate discretionary interlocutory review under the *Chamberlan* factors—inviting the very sort of threshold procedural objection that derailed *Labcorp* after oral argument.
2. **Threshold Factual and State-Law Dispute Over Whether *Any* Class Members Lack Article III Injury**: After the *Labcorp* DIG, the Court will be especially vigilant for vehicle defects. Here, both the Magistrate Judge (App. 34a–36a) and District Judge Mosman (App. 5a) explicitly held that **every single member of the certified class suffered a concrete Article III injury and UTPA ascertainable loss at the moment of purchase** when wrongly charged the 10-cent fee, and that subsequent refunds or BottleDrop redemptions merely reduce or offset damages after the injury has already occurred. Under Ninth Circuit precedent (*Van v. LLR, Inc.*, 962 F.3d 1160, 1164 (9th Cir. 2020)) and *Tyler v. Hennepin County*, 598 U.S. 631, 638 (2023), even a temporary wrongful deprivation of money (or the time and effort required to haul empty containers to a redemption center to recoup 10 cents) is a concrete injury-in-fact, and a defendant's partial or post-transaction refund is a merits/damages defense rather than an absence of initial Article III injury. Consequently, to reach the Question Presented, the Court would first have to review and reverse the district court's threshold determination that all class members *were* injured at the point of sale—a question entangled with Oregon UTPA "ascertainable loss" law (*Pearson*, *Sharfstein*) and factual disputes over how Oregon's BottleDrop system works.
3. **Superior Published Vehicles Are Available**: Unlike *Solano*, multiple published circuit opinions decided since *Labcorp* squarely adjudicate Rule 23(b)(3) certification on the merits after full appellate review—including *Healy v. Milliman, Inc.*, 157 F.4th 1091 (9th Cir. 2025) (the very published Ninth Circuit decision for which the *Solano* panel held this Rule 23(f) petition), *Speerly v. General Motors, LLC*, 143 F.4th 306 (6th Cir. 2025) (en banc), and *Clippinger v. State Farm Mut. Auto. Ins. Co.*, 154 F.4th 420 (6th Cir. 2025). If the Court wishes to take up the *Labcorp* question again, it has every incentive to choose a published merits opinion where the presence of uninjured class members is undisputed and the circuit court actually ruled on Rule 23(b)(3), rather than an unpublished Rule 23(f) denial involving a four-month 10-cent orange-juice deposit error in 2018.
