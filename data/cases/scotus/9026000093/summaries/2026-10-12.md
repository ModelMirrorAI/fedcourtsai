## In brief

A home-distilling hobbyist asks the Court to strike down the federal ban on producing spirits at home, and to reconsider a major precedent on Congress's commerce power.

## What happened

John Ream wants to distill whiskey at home but federal law bans producing spirits in a dwelling house, with criminal penalties. He sued before distilling, arguing the ban exceeds Congress's power. A district court found he lacked standing to sue before being prosecuted. The Sixth Circuit disagreed on standing but ruled against him on the merits, holding the ban a valid way to prevent tax evasion. That decision conflicts with a Fifth Circuit ruling in a similar case, McNutt, which struck the same ban down. Ream asks the Supreme Court to resolve that split and address whether the ban is constitutional.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the home-distilling ban goes beyond the powers the Constitution gives Congress, including whether banning home production can be justified as a way of helping collect a tax on that same activity. Ream also asks the Court to reconsider or narrow an earlier ruling that gave Congress broad authority to regulate local, non-commercial activity under its power over interstate commerce. The federal government, while agreeing the tax question merits review, argues that review should happen through its own companion case, McNutt, rather than this one, and that the commerce-power question should not be reached here since no lower court in this case addressed it.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference more than once, including for the October 9, 2026 conference, and the docket shows no action on it since. Briefing is complete, including the government's response and Ream's reply.

## What each outcome would mean

Ream, who lost on the merits below, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear the case in full and decide whether the ban is constitutional and possibly whether to revisit the commerce-power precedent, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's decision upholding the ban stands for Ream's case, leaving the ban in effect for him despite the conflicting Fifth Circuit ruling in McNutt. The Court could also hold this case pending its decision in McNutt, as the government requests, which would delay any outcome here.
