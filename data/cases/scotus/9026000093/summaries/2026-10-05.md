## In brief

John Ream asks the Court to strike down the federal ban on distilling spirits at home as beyond Congress's constitutional powers.

## What happened

John Ream, an Ohio brewpub owner, wants to distill whiskey at home for personal use but says federal law makes that a felony. He sued the Treasury Department and related officials, arguing the home-distilling ban exceeds Congress's powers. A federal district court dismissed his case, ruling he lacked standing to sue because he had not yet bought a still. The Sixth Circuit Court of Appeals disagreed on standing, allowing the case to proceed, but then ruled against Ream on the merits, holding the ban was a valid way to help collect the federal excise tax on spirits. One judge dissented, saying Ream lacked standing. Ream notes the Fifth Circuit recently struck down the same ban in a similar case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the federal government has constitutional authority to ban distilling spirits in one's own home, given that Congress's power to tax that activity does not necessarily include power to prohibit it. A second question asks whether the Court should overturn or narrow an earlier ruling that gave Congress broad authority under its power to regulate interstate commerce to reach purely local, noncommercial activity.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for October 9, 2026, after an earlier conference date was rescheduled. The docket shows no action yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Ream is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear full arguments and decide whether the home-distilling ban is constitutional, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Sixth Circuit's ruling upholding the ban stands, leaving Ream unable to legally distill at home, at least within that circuit, while the Fifth Circuit's contrary ruling continues to apply elsewhere.
