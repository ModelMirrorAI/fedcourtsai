## Why 60% for any grant, but not for plenary review

The petition's strongest feature is an acknowledged conflict over the same federal home-distilling prohibition. The appended Sixth Circuit opinion upholds it under the taxing and Necessary and Proper Clauses. The petition describes the contrary Fifth Circuit result in McNutt. More importantly, the federal response itself accepts the conflict and the need for Supreme Court review, while asking the Court to take the government's McNutt petition and hold Ream. This is substantially stronger evidence of an issue likely to receive review than the petitioner's characterization alone. Sources: petition pp. 8–14 and appendix pp. 11–19; federal response pp. 8–11, 18.

That support does not transfer one-for-one to a plenary grant here. The Sixth Circuit majority found standing, but the district court and appellate dissent disagreed. The response identifies firmer standing facts in McNutt and presents it as the narrower vehicle. It also says the government is no longer advancing the commerce-power defense in this case. The reply contests the significance and permanence of that litigation position and argues that only Ream allows comprehensive review, including Raich. I find the narrower vehicle argument more persuasive as a forecast of case selection, without treating Ream's standing as definitively absent. Sources: federal response pp. 9–11; reply pp. 1–4, 7–9.

My principal forecast is therefore a hold followed by a GVR in light of McNutt, not an immediate plenary grant to reconsider Raich. A useful probability decomposition is approximately 48% summary grant-family disposition, predominantly GVR; 12% plenary grant; 39% denial; and 1% another nongrant termination. The first two sum to 60%, and 48/60 gives the 80% conditional summary-route claim. These are subjective forecasts, not estimated frequencies for companion petitions.

The largest uncertainty is what happens in McNutt, which I have not researched beyond its description in these pre-decision filings. If the Court takes it and sustains the prohibition, denial here becomes much more likely. If it rejects the taxing rationale, a GVR here becomes more likely, potentially leaving further proceedings over the alternative theory. I give the latter branch a modest edge, not certainty: the direct constitutional conflict supports review, but the government's historical tax-enforcement rationale remains substantial. An administrative hold alone is not a grant and contributes nothing to the 60% until an eventual grant-family disposition occurs.

## Other claims and limitations

I assign 85% to at least one additional distribution beyond the two recorded entries, chiefly because coordinating the companion petitions or disposing of a held case should create another distribution. This is a judgment about future docket entries, not a claim that a hold must generate a fixed number of relists. I assign 0.5% to a new CVSG because the federal respondents, represented by the Solicitor General, have already submitted their position. Conditional on denial, I assign 18% to any noted dissent or statement, reflecting the federalism dispute and organized interest while retaining no-writing as the modal result. No conditional-claim baseline was manufactured from the terminal signal tables.

The significance score of 0.75 reflects possible consequences for enumerated-powers doctrine and nationwide home-distilling rules, not the grant probability. A narrow tax-power ruling or companion GVR would realize less of that potential than reconsideration of Raich.

The missing provisioned response mattered: a petition-only read would overstate the chance of plenary review of the commerce-power question and overlook the government's express hold request. Direct retrieval repaired that gap, which is noted in flags.json. Web-tool attempts returned no usable content; the exact PDFs were instead fetched directly from the Supreme Court's public document store and read in memory. No CourtListener fallback or credentials were used.
