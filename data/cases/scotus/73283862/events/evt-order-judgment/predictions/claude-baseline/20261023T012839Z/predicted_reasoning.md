# Predicted outcome — Berry v. United States, No. 25-7026 (merits, grant moment)

**Judgment: vacated** (and remanded). The Court disturbs the Eleventh Circuit's denial of a
certificate of appealability. P(disturbed) = 0.80.

**Vote: 7–2.** Majority: Roberts, Sotomayor, Kagan, Gorsuch, Kavanaugh, Barrett, Jackson.
Dissent: Thomas and Alito, who would hold that a COA may be denied where binding circuit
precedent forecloses the claim — reasonable jurists in that circuit must follow it — and
who would stress the futility of a COA leading to a panel bound by the same precedent.
A 6–3 lineup with Gorsuch or Kavanaugh joining the dissent is the main alternative.

**Question reached.** The Court takes the single QP as written: whether a COA should issue
where the claim is foreclosed by home-circuit precedent but resolved for the prisoner by
another circuit. It does not reach the underlying Davis/Beeman burden-of-proof question
(the "more likely than not residual clause" rule), on which it has denied review repeatedly.

**Ground.** The § 2253(c)(2) "substantial showing" is measured by the Slack/Miller-El
debatability standard, and a precedential decision of another court of appeals going the
other way is the paradigm of reasonable jurists debating the issue. The COA stage is a
threshold screen, not a merits ruling under circuit law (Miller-El, Buck v. Davis), so the
Hamilton rule — no COA where circuit precedent forecloses the claim — is rejected. The
opinion likely notes that the denial-of-a-COA rule otherwise insulates circuit precedent
from en banc and certiorari review.

**Breadth.** A categorical rule for every COA application (state and federal prisoners):
out-of-circuit contrary precedent on the dispositive issue suffices to make it debatable,
regardless of home-circuit law. Narrow on the remedy: the government's alternative defenses
(procedural default tied to the dismissed Count 4, § 2255(f)(3) untimeliness, Taylor's
statutory character) are left for remand — hence **vacated** rather than reversed outright.

**Procedural exits.** A DIG is unlikely (~4%): the QP is cleanly presented and the
government did not dispute that. An equally divided Court is not live (no expected
recusal). An affirmance (~16%) would most plausibly come either from a majority adopting
the government's futility reasoning or from holding the claim non-debatable on an
alternative ground; because the government did not defend the decision below at the cert
stage, the Court may appoint an amicus to defend the judgment — which does not change my
call.
