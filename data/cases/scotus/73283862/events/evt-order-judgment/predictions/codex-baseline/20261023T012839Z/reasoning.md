I move from that approximately 0.70 anchor to **0.84** because the order below makes a discrete, reviewable legal move: it uses Hamilton to treat adverse binding circuit precedent as precluding a COA, despite contrary circuit authority. Appendix A, pages 1a–3a, makes this rationale explicit. The petition, particularly pages 29–36, argues that this conflates whether a panel must follow circuit law with whether reasonable jurists can debate it. The COA precedents discussed in both parties' papers—Slack, Miller-El, and Buck—supply a threshold-debatability framework that makes that distinction persuasive. I rely on their treatment in the provisioned filings, not on an independently retrieved opinion body.

The government offers a serious counterargument: certiorari and en banc mechanisms remain available, so mandatory appeals on settled circuit issues may consume resources without changing outcomes (opposition, pages 12–18). But practical redundancy is less compelling than the claimed mismatch between the controlling threshold and a geographically restricted definition of reasonable jurists. Correcting the COA standard also need not overrule Beeman or decide the underlying constitutional claim.

The main downward adjustment is vehicle risk. The opposition, pages 18–20, identifies procedural default and untimeliness, and the successive-motion restrictions complicate the underlying claim. The reply, pages 13–15, answers that neither court below decided those defenses and disputes their preservation. I treat waiver and default as contested advocacy, not established facts. That uncertainty supports a remand rather than a confident instruction to award substantive relief, and reserves a meaningful chance of affirmance or a DIG.
