## In brief

The Supreme Court agreed to hear a federal prisoner's case asking whether appeals courts must let him appeal when his claim conflicts with rulings from other circuits.

## What happened

Corey Berry pleaded guilty to carjacking offenses and a related firearm charge and was sentenced to 218 months in prison. After the Supreme Court later struck down part of the law underlying his firearm conviction, Berry asked a federal court to vacate that conviction. The district court denied relief, applying Eleventh Circuit precedent that requires prisoners in his position to prove their conviction rested solely on the invalidated part of the law. The Eleventh Circuit then refused to let Berry appeal that ruling at all, denying him a "certificate of appealability" because its own precedent foreclosed his argument, even though three other circuits apply a more lenient rule that would let him win. Berry asked the Supreme Court to take up his case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a prisoner must be allowed to appeal when the legal rule that defeated his claim in his own circuit is one that other circuits have rejected. More broadly, the question is whether appeals courts can deny permission to appeal simply because their own binding precedent forecloses the claim, even when judges elsewhere have reached the opposite conclusion on the same legal issue.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted Berry's petition for certiorari on October 13, 2026, agreeing to hear the case. Briefing and argument on the merits have not yet occurred according to the docket.

## What each outcome would mean

Berry, the prisoner, is asking the Court to overturn the Eleventh Circuit's refusal to let him appeal. If the Court rules for Berry, it would hold that a prisoner must be allowed to appeal when other circuits disagree with the rule that defeated his claim, letting his appeal go forward without deciding whether he ultimately wins. If the Court rules for the government, it would uphold the Eleventh Circuit's approach, meaning Berry's case stays closed and courts may keep denying permission to appeal based solely on their own binding precedent, regardless of conflicting rulings elsewhere.
