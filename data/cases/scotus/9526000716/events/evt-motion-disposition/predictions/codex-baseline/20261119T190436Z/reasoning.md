The Texas Court of Criminal Appeals dismissed Dennes’s first successive state application under article 11.071 § 5 and expressly declined to reach any of its five claims. His Supreme Court petition asks whether trial counsel conducted an adequate investigation before sentencing. Dennes anticipates the difficulty of obtaining review. He argues that the Texas exception he invoked requires a federal constitutional violation, so the dismissal necessarily depended on federal law. But the state court could have concluded that his evidence fell short of the separate requirement to show that no rational juror would have answered a sentencing question in the State’s favor. Evidence that might have persuaded one juror to choose life would support a claim of prejudice under *Wiggins* while still falling short of that gateway. That makes both Supreme Court review and reversal less likely. The harm of execution is irreversible. Dennes says the jury heard only brief accounts from his son and mother, and that a later neuropsychological examination documented severe difficulties with executive functioning. Testing and evidence of childhood abuse could have given a juror reasons to choose life beyond the family testimony counsel elicited. The petition also acknowledges the State’s contention that the jury had already heard some of the underlying events during the guilt phase. How much the omitted evidence would have changed the jury’s assessment is the main factual uncertainty. Even if it would have mattered, Dennes still needs enough Justices to see a fair prospect of reversing this particular dismissal.
