My **18%** unqualified-grant forecast is a judgmental upward adjustment, not a fitted estimate. The application argues that fact-intensive predictive detention judgments warrant deference under the Court's general standard-of-review cases (application pp. 14–30). The October 6 letter strengthens the equities relative to the application alone. The opposition nonetheless makes denial appreciably more likely. It argues that all circuits defer to historical factual findings, that differences concerning the ultimate detention judgment are narrower in operation than the applicant's three-way taxonomy suggests, and that this case turns on dangerousness and ineffective release conditions rather than a clean legal disagreement (opposition pp. 14–20). That does not eliminate the split, but it weakens the inference that identifying the split establishes likely review and reversal here. The government's allegations concerning threats, prior failures to appear, and evasion of monitoring support a plausible alternative route to detention even under more deferential review. These are contested advocacy and descriptions of the lower record, not my findings that the alleged conduct occurred.

The trial extension gives the Court more time, but not a normal merits calendar. As the opposition explains, a stay of detention would not itself stop trial from mooting the pretrial-release dispute (pp. 20–22). Its delay-based equity argument also matters, although I do not accept that agreeing to a continuance makes the loss of liberty harmless; the applicant directly answers that contention (application p. 32; opposition pp. 22–24). Taken together, the vehicle, timing, and public-safety obstacles keep my probability well below one-half despite the stronger-than-baseline attention signals.
