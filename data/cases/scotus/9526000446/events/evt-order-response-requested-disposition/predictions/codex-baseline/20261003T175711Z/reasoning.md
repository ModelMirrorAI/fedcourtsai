## Why 27%, rather than the anchor or an even chance

The strongest upward adjustment is the affirmative response request. It warrants moving above an unconditioned application rate, but a request is not a forecast of agreement. The application also identifies a concrete, recurring appellate-review issue rather than simply contesting an unfavorable fact assessment. Its account describes five circuits applying de novo review, four applying clear-error or abuse-of-discretion review, and three taking an intermediate approach (application pp. 2, 14–21). The lower-court stay-denial order reportedly acknowledges a split (pp. 23–25). The district court held a two-day hearing, fashioned GPS and internet restrictions, and ordered release; the Sixth Circuit reversed over a dissent (pp. 10–13). Those details make the asserted institutional-competence argument substantial.

The claim of irreparable harm is also concrete: continued pretrial confinement and impaired defense preparation (pp. 31–33). Nevertheless, the applicant must bridge the gap between a review-worthy legal question and entitlement to this immediate remedy. The application's discussion of the stay standard, citing Hollingsworth v. Perry, 558 U.S. 183, 190 (2010), requires prospects of certiorari and reversal as well as irreparable harm (p. 13). I do not infer likely relief solely from the liberty interest.

Three considerations restrain the adjustment. First, even the applicant's description recognizes clear-error review of historical factual findings in the Sixth Circuit, with de novo review of the ultimate detention determination (pp. 11–12); the dispute is subtler than complete appellate disregard of facts. Second, the panel majority reportedly concluded that the proposed conditions would not adequately mitigate dangerousness, and the stay-denial order disputed whether the standard determined the outcome (pp. 12, 25). Without the government's response, confidence that deference would require release is limited. Third, the application itself raises a vehicle problem: trial is scheduled for November 3, with the parties stipulating to a further 60-day extension, and the detention issue may expire after trial. Its requests for expedited review or treating the application as a petition seek to solve that problem but do not establish that the Court will do so (pp. 4–5, 22–23).

On balance, the response request, split, dissent, and concrete liberty injury justify roughly a sixteen-percentage-point judgmental increase from the published pool to 27%. This is not a fitted conditional estimate. Denial remains the modal disposition. The remaining 73% includes denial and small residual withdrawal, dismissal, or mixed-relief possibilities; it is not all asserted to be pure denial.
