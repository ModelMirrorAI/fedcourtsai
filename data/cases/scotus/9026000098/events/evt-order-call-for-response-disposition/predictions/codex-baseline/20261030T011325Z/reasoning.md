## Why move well above the anchor?

The strongest fact is an acknowledged appellate disagreement, not simply the petition's assertion of one. The question is whether the NVRA's public-inspection provision, 52 U.S.C. section 20507(i)(1), covers the statewide voter list itself. The petition, printed pages 14–19, argues a conflict with the First Circuit's Bellows decision and support from the Tenth and Fourth Circuits. The appended Ninth Circuit opinion, printed appendix pages 35a–37a, expressly rejects Bellows's treatment of the list as the output of maintenance activities and acknowledges reaching a different outcome. That supplies much stronger evidence of an actual split than advocacy alone.

The legal question is recurring and national. Access to the same kind of election-administration record turns on the circuit, and the competing readings of the same federal provision are clear. The three pre-request amicus filings and the request despite a waiver add evidence of outside interest and at least some judicial attention. Together these justify moving from a roughly 5% class anchor to a serious, but still sub-even, chance of review. There is no published response-request-conditioned rate in the pack: the size of this adjustment is judgment, not a measured likelihood ratio.

The vehicle also looks reasonably direct. The appellate opinion ultimately finds jurisdiction but rejects the claim on the merits, with instructions to dismiss with prejudice (appendix 39a). Its discussion of standing and ripeness, along with the petition's vehicle section at printed page 23, reduces the chance that the statutory issue is merely hypothetical. This is not proof that a respondent cannot revive a threshold objection.

## Why not make grant the favorite?

The evidence is still one-sided at the cert stage. The Ninth Circuit supplies an intelligible alternative reading: implementation records differ from the maintained list; the later statewide-list statute does not itself create the asserted public-access right; and privacy and state-law access matter (appendix 34a–38a). Those points may not defeat the petition on the merits, but they keep the case from looking like an obvious summary error correction.

The split should not be overstated. The Ninth Circuit's footnote distinguishes the Tenth Circuit case because its parties did not litigate the same implementation issue. The Fourth Circuit case involved applications rather than an entire statewide roll. The sharpest conflict is therefore with the First Circuit. The petition also identifies separate Michigan litigation involving more sensitive voter information, which may make the Court prefer further development or a different vehicle. I use that litigation only as described in this pre-decision petition, not as a retrieved later development.

A response call requires less agreement than a grant and does not establish four votes. The existing distribution preceded the scheduled conference, so it is not a recorded conference decision to hold or relist the petition. Missing opposition leaves meaningful uncertainty about alternative access, requested data fields, and threshold objections. I therefore stop at 42%, rather than extrapolating a grant from the split and the call alone.
