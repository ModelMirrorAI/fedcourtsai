# Predicted Court Reasoning and Procedural Trajectory: *Marshall Grace v. United States* (No. 26-459)

## 1. Procedural Trajectory: Distributions, Relists, and CVSG

As of the October 28, 2026 snapshot, the petition has been distributed **once** (on October 21, 2026, for the Conference of November 6, 2026) after the Solicitor General waived the United States' right to respond on October 16, 2026. On October 28, 2026—prior to the November 6 conference—the Court called for a response from the government, due November 27, 2026.

- **Further distributions (`relist-increment`: 0.99):** Because the Court called for a response after the initial distribution, the petition will be taken off the November 6, 2026 conference list and redistributed for a subsequent conference once the Solicitor General files the Brief in Opposition (likely after one or two routine 30-day extensions into winter 2026–2027) and petitioner files a reply brief. Once redistributed with the Brief in Opposition in hand, the most likely trajectory is resolution at that first post-BIO conference without multiple true post-BIO relists, though a single relist is possible if a chamber circulates a short dissent from denial or checks whether a GVR is warranted.
- **Call for the Views of the Solicitor General (`cvsg-increment`: 0.00):** The United States is the respondent in this federal criminal prosecution and is already represented by the Solicitor General, so a CVSG will not occur.

## 2. Disposition Forecast (`denied` at 88%, `gvr` at 6%, `granted` at 5%, `dismissed` at 1%)

The Court will most likely **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari after receiving the government's Brief in Opposition.

While the October 28, 2026 call for a response signals that at least one chamber flagged the petition—understandably so, given the Fifth Circuit's explicit acknowledgment that the district judge erred by questioning Juror 8 (*sua sponte*) to uncover a substitute justification for the strike, combined with the recent decision in *Pitchford v. Cain*, 608 U.S. 391 (2026)—the Brief in Opposition will expose substantial vehicle and record impediments that weigh heavily against plenary review or summary vacatur:

1. **No clean holding of *de novo* appellate credibility factfinding below:** Although petitioner frames the Fifth Circuit's decision as joining the Ninth Circuit in conducting first-instance *Batson* step-three factfinding, the Fifth Circuit's brief step-three discussion (App. 11a–12a) rests on two narrower propositions: first, that "the district court recognized that having two black jurors, and one black alternate juror, undermined Grace's claim that the Government had a 'system . . . to violate *Batson*'" (reflecting the district judge's statement at App. 18a expressly rejecting defense counsel's assertion that the prosecutors "unfairly targeted black people": *"Well, I disagree with that. They're not. I disagree with that."*); and second, that Grace failed to carry his step-three burden of persuasion because he offered no comparative juror analysis or other evidence showing that the prosecutor's stated concern about Davis "having seven sons" was pretextual on its face.
2. **Severe factual complications in the voir dire transcript:** The bench conference transcript (App. 20a–24a) reveals that when Juror 8 (Edward Davis) was questioned at the bench, he stated: *"Well, to me, you know, only God can judge people. You know, I can't really judge nobody. Only God can judge people."* (App. 22a). Furthermore, the prosecutor placed on the record—and the district court confirmed—that the court had twice asked the venire during general voir dire whether they or any family members had been arrested, and Davis had failed to disclose that his son spent two-and-a-half years in jail and five years on probation on a gun charge, prompting the district judge to find on the record that Davis *"was less than candid."* (App. 23a–24a). Even though those facts emerged during the district judge's *sua sponte* inquiry, they make this case a poor vehicle for testing the contours of *Batson* step-three appellate remedies.
3. **Distinctions from *Pitchford v. Cain*:** In *Pitchford*, a capital habeas case decided May 28, 2026, the state trial court cut off defense counsel twice before counsel could make a step-three pretext argument, and the state supreme court held the pretext argument waived. Here, defense counsel was permitted to argue at length before and after the bench examination (App. 15a–19a, 22a–23a), made no comparative-juror showing, and was not held to have waived his claim.

## 3. Conditional Route if Granted (`summary-disposition-route`: 0.55)

Conditional on the Court granting relief (`probability`: 0.11), a **summary disposition (specifically, a GVR)** is slightly more likely than plenary review (`0.55` conditional probability, representing ~6% unconditional GVR probability vs. ~5% unconditional plenary grant probability):

- **Summary GVR route:** The Fifth Circuit issued its panel opinion on March 27, 2026, and denied rehearing on May 5, 2026—three weeks before this Court decided *Pitchford v. Cain*, 608 U.S. 391 (May 28, 2026), another Fifth Circuit *Batson* step-three case. Moreover, if the Solicitor General's Brief in Opposition concedes that once the Fifth Circuit rejected the district court's *sua sponte* questioning as legal error it should have remanded for an untainted step-three finding rather than affirming in the first instance, the Court would likely GVR without merits briefing.
- **Question Presented if granted for plenary review:** If the Court instead grants plenary review, it will take the single Question Presented in the petition as written: whether a federal appellate court, after concluding that a federal district court failed to make necessary findings under step three of *Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986), may proceed to make those findings in the first instance rather than remanding.
- **Conditional merits ground:** If heard on the merits, the Court would likely hold that under *Batson*, *Pullman-Standard v. Swint*, 456 U.S. 273, 291–292 (1982), and *Flowers v. Mississippi*, 588 U.S. 284, 302 (2019), when a district court fails to make a valid step-three determination on the prosecutor's stated reasons and the record does not compel one outcome as a matter of law, the court of appeals must remand for the district court to make the step-three credibility determination in the first instance.

## 4. Separate Writings on Denial (`dissent-from-denial`: 0.04)

Conditional on certiorari being denied, the probability of a separate writing (a dissent from denial or statement respecting denial) is modest (`0.04`). If a separate writing occurs, it is most likely to come from **Justice Sotomayor** (`0.02`) or **Justice Jackson** (`0.02`), or less likely **Justice Kavanaugh** (`0.008`, who authored *Pitchford v. Mississippi*), criticizing the district court's *sua sponte* questioning of the struck juror and emphasizing that appellate courts should remand when trial judges depart from the three-step *Batson* framework.
