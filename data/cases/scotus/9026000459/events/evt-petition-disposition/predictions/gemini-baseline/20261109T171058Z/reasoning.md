# Prediction Reasoning: *Marshall Grace v. United States* (No. 26-459)

## 1. Case Overview and Procedural Posture

Petitioner Marshall Grace seeks a writ of certiorari to the United States Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit (`United States v. Grace`, No. 24-30730, decided March 27, 2026; rehearing denied May 5, 2026). After a traffic stop in New Orleans in which officers recovered approximately 441 grams of methamphetamine from Grace's trunk and obtained incriminating statements and text messages, a federal jury in the Eastern District of Louisiana convicted Grace of conspiracy to distribute and possess with intent to distribute at least 500 grams of methamphetamine under 21 U.S.C. § 846 and possession with intent to distribute at least 50 grams of methamphetamine under 21 U.S.C. § 841(a)(1), (b)(1)(B). He was sentenced to the mandatory minimum of 120 months' imprisonment.

During jury selection, the Government used two of its six peremptory strikes against Black prospective jurors (out of five Black prospective jurors in the 31-person strike zone). Grace raised a challenge under *Batson v. Kentucky*, 476 U.S. 79 (1986), focusing on the strike of prospective juror Edward Davis (Juror No. 8). When asked for a reason, the prosecutor stated that "there wasn't much to go on for Mr. Davis except for the fact that we knew he had seven sons and based on that information and not knowing much else about him, we chose that he would probably not be a great juror for the government."

The district judge initially expressed skepticism ("you're not convincing me that that is a proper reason to strike this African American gentleman when you have an African American defendant" and "I have a problem with that"). However, rather than sustaining the *Batson* challenge or evaluating the credibility of the "seven sons" explanation on the existing record, the district judge sua sponte called Davis up to the bench for additional questioning. That questioning revealed that one of Davis's sons had spent two-and-a-half years in pretrial detention on a gun-related charge before the charge was reduced, that Davis felt his son was not treated fairly, and that Davis believed "only God can judge people." Relying on those newly elicited facts ("had the government known this, now they know it, there would be grounds to use—exercise a peremptory challenge"), the district court denied the *Batson* challenge.

In a published opinion by Judge Stewart (joined by Judges Jones and Willett), the Fifth Circuit held that the district court erred by sua sponte questioning Davis to find a race-neutral justification on the Government's behalf, citing *Miller-El v. Dretke*, 545 U.S. 231, 252 (2005), and *Chamberlin v. Fisher*, 885 F.3d 832, 841 (5th Cir. 2018) (en banc). Nonetheless, the Fifth Circuit affirmed Grace's conviction at *Batson* step three, reasoning that: (1) two Black jurors and one Black alternate ultimately sat on the jury, which undermined the claim of a systematic *Batson* violation; and (2) Grace failed to show that the prosecutor's actual stated reason—having seven sons—was "on its face, 'clearly pretextual.'"

As of the November 9, 2026 docket snapshot (`2026-11-09.json`), the paid petition was docketed on October 7, 2026, represented by Paul W. Hughes and the Yale Supreme Court Clinic (with McDermott Will & Schulte LLP). On November 6, 2026, the Solicitor General obtained an extension of time to file the United States' response up to and including December 7, 2026. No amicus briefs have been filed, and the petition has not yet been distributed for conference.

## 3. Analysis of the Certiorari Factors

### A. Strength of the Legal Issue and Alleged Circuit Split
Petitioner frames the question presented around *Pullman-Standard v. Swint*, 456 U.S. 273, 291–92 (1982), and *Snyder v. Louisiana*, 552 U.S. 472, 477 (2008): when a district court fails to make the required credibility findings as to the prosecutor's actual stated reason under *Batson* step three, may a court of appeals make those findings in the first instance rather than remanding?

1. **Circuit Alignment:** Petitioner demonstrates a substantial division in appellate practice when a trial court commits legal or procedural error at *Batson* step three:
   - Most circuits (including the Second, Third, Fourth, Sixth, Seventh, Eighth, Tenth, and D.C. Circuits—recently illustrated by *Salters v. New York City Transit Authority*, No. 25-835 (2d Cir. Aug. 7, 2026), and *Carter v. City of Wauwatosa*, 114 F.4th 866 (7th Cir. 2024)) remand for the district court to conduct a proper step-three credibility determination (or hold a reconstruction hearing / grant a new trial) when the trial court failed to adjudicate the credibility of the proffered race-neutral explanation.
   - By contrast, the Ninth Circuit (*United States v. Alvarez-Ulloa*, 784 F.3d 558, 565–66 (9th Cir. 2015)) and the Fifth Circuit here have proceeded to evaluate step three in the first instance on the cold appellate record after concluding that the district court misapplied the *Batson* framework.
2. **Tension with *Batson* and *Pullman-Standard*:** The Fifth Circuit's approach is genuinely vulnerable. The transcript (App. 15a–24a) shows that the district court explicitly doubted the prosecutor's "seven sons" rationale ("you're not convincing me that that is a proper reason ... I have a problem with that") and never found that explanation credible; instead, it relied entirely on the post-hoc answers elicited during its own improper questioning. Moreover, the Fifth Circuit's statement that Grace could not show the "seven sons" reason was "on its face, 'clearly pretextual'" conflates *Batson* step two (facial race-neutrality under *Purkett v. Elem*) with step three (plausibility and credibility under *Miller-El* and *Snyder*). And the Supreme Court recently reaffirmed the centrality of proper trial-court step-three procedures in *Pitchford v. Cain*, 608 U.S. ___ (May 28, 2026) (reversing the Fifth Circuit in a *Batson* step-three habeas case).

### B. Vehicle and Merits Headwinds Weighing Against Full Grant
Despite the appealing doctrinal framing and elite Supreme Court clinic representation, several significant factors weigh against a full grant of certiorari (`granted`):
1. **No Dissent Below:** The Fifth Circuit panel—composed of Judges Stewart, Jones, and Willett (spanning ideological wings of the court)—was unanimous, and rehearing was denied without a noted dissent or poll.
2. **Preservation / Framing Below:** As the Fifth Circuit noted (App. 10a), Grace's appellate briefing primarily argued that the Government's "seven sons" explanation was pretextual and that the conviction should be vacated, rather than focusing squarely on the *Pullman-Standard* appellate-remedy question now foregrounded in the petition. The Solicitor General in the Brief in Opposition will likely argue that the Fifth Circuit did not announce a categorical rule permitting de novo appellate factfinding, but merely held that Grace had failed to carry his step-three burden of persuasion on the record he made in the district court (where defense counsel argued only that 2 of 4 or 5 Black venirepersons were struck and that Davis's sons were likely adults, without offering comparative juror analysis).
3. **Factbound / Harmless-Error Undertones:** Even though the district judge erred by questioning Davis sua sponte, that questioning revealed that Davis had failed to disclose during general voir dire that his son had been incarcerated for two-and-a-half years on a gun charge and that Davis believed "only God can judge people." While legally irrelevant to the prosecutor's prior state of mind under *Miller-El*, these facts make Grace an unattractive vehicle for plenary review because a remand would almost certainly focus on a narrow historical reconstruction of a minimal voir dire colloquy.
4. **Absence of Amicus Support:** No amicus briefs were filed at the cert stage.
