The roughly one-year gap after the amendment deadline makes an intermediate rule that considers diligence a genuine path to reversal.

The respondent nevertheless has several cross-ideological answers. Rule 8 prescribes a duty but no express consequence; Rules 12(h) and 71.1 expressly use waiver language elsewhere; Rule 61 addresses nonprejudicial procedural error; and precedents distinguish inadvertent forfeiture from intentional waiver. The record also makes the respondent's limiting principle concrete. Three courts found no material prejudice, while the district court refused to consider a different late defense that required factual development. Those facts make affirmance possible without endorsing strategic sandbagging.

The pre-argument amicus pattern modestly reinforces that adjustment: 17 States and Professor Derek Muller support respondent's merits-first approach, while Washington Legal Foundation takes no position on the ultimate rule but emphasizes forfeiture-of-forfeiture and appellate discretion. I therefore move from the 69.7% generic disturbed baseline to 39%, leaving substantial reversal probability because Rule 61 may not supply a cure for a Rule 8 forfeiture and because Rules 15 and 16 offer an obvious, textually specified mechanism.

My vote forecast is lower confidence than the judgment forecast. The dispute does not divide cleanly by ideology. The predicted majority combines the express-waiver comparison and harmless-error principle; the predicted dissent combines textual enforcement of Rules 8, 15, and 16 with concern that a multifactor personal-staff defense is not purely legal. The biggest uncertainty is whether the Court adopts respondent's narrow no-prejudice rule, requires constructive amendment under the ordinary Rules 15 and 16 standards, or fashions a middle rule that adds diligence and bad faith to prejudice.
