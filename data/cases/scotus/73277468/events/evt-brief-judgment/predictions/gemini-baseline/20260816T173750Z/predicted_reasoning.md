The Court will rule that Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 8(c) imposes a mandatory obligation on defendants to affirmatively state any avoidance or affirmative defense in their responsive pleading. The majority will reject the "no prejudice" standard adopted by the Eleventh Circuit and the majority of federal circuits, which permits defendants to raise unpleaded affirmative defenses at the summary judgment stage if the plaintiff is not unfairly surprised.

The majority's doctrinal ground will rest firmly on textualism. The Court will emphasize that Rule 8(c)'s plain text—"must affirmatively state"—leaves no room for judicial exceptions based on the "liberal spirit" of the rules. Furthermore, the Court will note that allowing unpleaded defenses at summary judgment effectively bypasses the procedural structure established by Rule 16(b)(4), which requires a showing of "good cause" and the judge's consent to modify a scheduling order.

The breadth of this ruling will be categorical. The Court will establish a bright-line procedural rule applying to all affirmative defenses in federal civil litigation, holding that a defendant who fails to plead an affirmative defense in their answer cannot later raise it as a basis for summary judgment without first properly moving to amend their pleading under Rule 15 and satisfying the good-cause requirement of Rule 16(b)(4) if the deadline has passed. The ruling will not rely on the specific facts, timeline, or subjective prejudice to the plaintiff in Dr. Younge's case.

The dissent will likely argue that the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure should be construed to secure the just, speedy, and inexpensive determination of every action (Rule 1), and that the majority's strict textualist approach elevates form over substance, punishing defendants for technical oversights even when the plaintiff had ample notice and opportunity to respond during discovery.
