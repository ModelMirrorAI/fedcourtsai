## In brief

Younge and the Fulton County DA's Office disputed whether an employer may rely on an affirmative defense never included in its answer, and the Court heard argument on that question.

## What happened

Jasmine Younge sued the Fulton Judicial Circuit District Attorney's Office under Title VII, claiming she was fired after announcing her pregnancy. The office's answer did not assert that Younge was exempt from Title VII as a member of the district attorney's "personal staff." Nearly a year later, after discovery had closed, the office raised that defense for the first time in a motion for summary judgment. The magistrate judge and district court allowed the defense, finding Younge was not prejudiced, and granted summary judgment for the office. The Eleventh Circuit affirmed, applying its rule that an unpled affirmative defense may be considered at summary judgment so long as the plaintiff was not prejudiced. Younge petitioned for review, pointing to disagreement among the circuits over this practice.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether a defendant who never included a defense in its answer can still rely on that defense to win a motion for summary judgment, without ever amending the answer to add it, and even if the rules governing late amendments would have blocked such an amendment.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition on March 30, 2026. The case was briefed on the merits, amicus briefs were filed, and the Court heard oral argument on November 2, 2026. A decision has not yet been recorded in the docket.

## What each outcome would mean

Younge, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to reverse. If the Court affirms, the Eleventh Circuit's rule stands: the DA's Office's win stands, and defendants in that circuit may rely on unpled affirmative defenses at summary judgment so long as the plaintiff shows no prejudice, without having to amend their answers under the usual rules for late amendments. If the Court reverses, Younge's case would return to the lower courts without the benefit of that defense, consistent with her position that defendants must plead affirmative defenses or formally seek to amend their answer under the normal standards. A vacate-and-remand would set aside the Eleventh Circuit's decision and send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
