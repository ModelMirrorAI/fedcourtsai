## In brief

Jasmine Younge asks the Court to decide whether a defendant who never pled an affirmative defense can still win summary judgment by raising it there.

## What happened

Jasmine Younge worked briefly for the Fulton County District Attorney's Office before being fired; she sued, alleging the firing violated Title VII's ban on pregnancy discrimination. The District Attorney's Office did not plead, as an affirmative defense, that Younge was exempt from Title VII as a member of an elected official's "personal staff." It raised that defense for the first time in a summary judgment motion, after the deadline for amending pleadings had passed. The district court and the Eleventh Circuit both allowed the defense, reasoning that Younge had not been prejudiced by the delay, and ruled for the employer on that basis.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a defendant who answered a complaint without raising a particular defense can nonetheless rely on that defense to win summary judgment, without ever amending its answer to include it, even where amending the answer at that point would not otherwise be allowed under the rules governing late amendments.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been briefed and argued; it was set for argument on November 2, 2026, and now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Younge, as petitioner, is asking the Court to rule that defendants must formally plead or seek to add such defenses rather than raise them for the first time at summary judgment. If the Court rules for Younge, the lower court's decision allowing the unpled defense would be reversed or vacated, which could revive her discrimination claim for further proceedings. If the Court rules for the District Attorney's Office, the lower courts' approach allowing unpled defenses without prejudice would stand, and the judgment against Younge would be affirmed. A vacate-and-remand would set the current ruling aside without deciding the issue for either side.
