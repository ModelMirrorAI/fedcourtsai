## In brief

The Court will decide whether an employer can raise an unpleaded legal defense at summary judgment, after a former employee's discrimination suit was dismissed on that basis.

## What happened

Jasmine Younge sued the Fulton Judicial Circuit District Attorney's Office for pregnancy discrimination under Title VII after her 2019 firing. The office's answer did not raise a defense that Younge's role made her exempt "personal staff" of an elected official, but the office raised that defense for the first time in a summary judgment motion, after discovery closed. The district court and the Eleventh Circuit both allowed the defense, reasoning that Younge suffered no prejudice from the delay, and ruled against her on the merits. Younge asked the Supreme Court to review that decision, arguing the circuits are split on whether such late defenses can be raised without formally amending the answer.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide whether a defendant who never included a legal defense in its answer can still rely on that defense in a summary judgment motion, without asking the court's permission to amend its answer, and even if such an amendment would normally be barred as too late under the court's scheduling rules.

## Where it stands

The petition was granted, and the case has been briefed and argued; it awaits decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Younge, as petitioner, asked the Court to rule that unpleaded defenses like this one cannot be used at summary judgment without formal amendment. If the Court sides with Younge, the case would return to the lower courts without the defense that ended her suit, letting her discrimination claim go forward. If the Court sides with the district attorney's office, the Eleventh Circuit's ruling allowing the defense stands, and the dismissal of Younge's claim remains in place. The Court could also vacate the lower ruling and send the case back without deciding the question for either side.
