# Predicted reasoning — Montoya Palacios v. Liggins (No. 25-1223), merits judgment

Forecast made at the grant moment (`moment: grant`, certiorari granted June 29,
2026). No merits briefing exists yet; petitioner's merits brief is due
August 31, 2026 and respondents' October 23, 2026.

## Judgment class

**Reversed.** The Court will hold that the Equal Access to Justice Act's
"any civil action," 28 U.S.C. § 2412(d)(1)(A), encompasses a habeas action
challenging civil immigration detention, reversing the Fourth Circuit's
summary affirmance (which rested on Obando-Segura v. Garland, 999 F.3d 190
(4th Cir. 2021)) and remanding for the fee application to be adjudicated.
P(judgment below disturbed) = **0.65**. Among disturbed outcomes, an outright
reversal is far likelier than a vacatur: the question is purely legal, was the
sole ground below, and needs no further factfinding — the Court either says
habeas-for-immigration-detention is a "civil action" or it is not.

## Vote lineup

**7–2**, Thomas and Alito dissenting on sovereign-immunity strict-construction
grounds (FAA v. Cooper). The majority coalition is text-first: Sotomayor,
Kagan, and Jackson are near-certain votes for the detainee's eligibility;
Gorsuch's "any means any" textualism and his record in statutory immigration
cases (Niz-Chavez, Dimaya) make him a likely fourth; Barrett and Kavanaugh
have both been skeptical of substantive canons that override otherwise-clear
text, and the century-old Tom Tong/Kurtz line saying a habeas petition *is* a
civil suit gives them a clean doctrinal path; Roberts follows the majority he
can shape. The closest analogue votes are the Court's prior EAJA cases —
Scarborough v. Principi (7–2) and Richlin Security Service v. Chertoff
(unanimous) — both claimant-friendly. If the case instead comes out for the
government, the likeliest shape is 5–4 or 6–3 built on Cooper's
ambiguity-resolving canon plus Schlanger/Stafford.

## Ground and breadth

The majority will take the question presented as written and decide it on the
narrower, immigration-specific rationale the Second, Third, and Tenth Circuits
share: habeas proceedings are civil in nature, immigration detention is itself
civil, so a habeas challenge to civil immigration detention is a civil action
whatever residual ambiguity "hybrid" criminal-custody habeas might present.
That framing lets the majority say the text is unambiguous — so Cooper's
clear-statement canon never bites — while reserving whether § 2254/§ 2255-type
petitions qualify. Schlanger v. Seamans will be distinguished as a holding
about nationwide service of process under § 1391(e), not a general definition
of "civil action." Expect the opinion to lean on "any" (United States v.
Gonzales) and on the express carve-out for "cases sounding in tort" as
evidence Congress knew how to except categories it wanted excepted.

## Separate writings

A dissent by Alito or Thomas developing the sovereign-immunity canon and the
Harris v. Nelson "unique writ" theme is likely. A short concurrence
(plausibly Gorsuch or Jackson) emphasizing habeas's historic office against
executive detention is possible but not necessary to the result.

## Procedural exits

A DIG is unlikely (< 5%): both parties told the Court the vehicle is clean,
the issue was pressed and passed on below, petitioner's prevailing-party
status is uncontested, and the government has not appealed the underlying
habeas grant. An equally divided affirmance requires a recusal for which
nothing on this record provides a basis.

With merits briefing extended to late October 2026, expect argument in the
January or February 2027 sitting and a decision by June 2027. (Timing is
