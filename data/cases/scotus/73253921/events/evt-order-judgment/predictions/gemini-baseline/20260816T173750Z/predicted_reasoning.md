The Court will reverse the judgment of the Fourth Circuit, concluding that a habeas corpus proceeding challenging civil immigration detention is a "civil action" within the meaning of the Equal Access to Justice Act (EAJA).

The core of the dispute turns on whether the waiver of sovereign immunity in the EAJA unequivocally covers immigration habeas cases. The government's position—that habeas proceedings are historically unique and neither strictly civil nor criminal—was credited by the lower court under a strict construction of sovereign immunity. However, the Court is likely to adopt the view of the Second, Third, and Tenth Circuits, holding that the plain text of "any civil action" leaves no room to carve out immigration habeas proceedings. Because immigration detention is inherently civil rather than criminal, a challenge to it is properly classified as a civil action. 

Justice Gorsuch, who often writes to clarify the scope of immigration statutes and emphasizes plain textual readings over general presumptions, is a likely author for the majority. Justice Thomas and Justice Alito are expected to dissent, emphasizing the traditional uniqueness of habeas corpus under *Schlanger v. Seamans* and insisting on a more explicit, unequivocal waiver of sovereign immunity by Congress before awarding attorney's fees against the United States.
