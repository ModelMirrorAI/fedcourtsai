## In brief

The Court will decide whether a noncitizen who wins release in a habeas case over immigration detention can recover attorney's fees under a federal fee-shifting law.

## What happened

Kevin Isaac Montoya Palacios, held by immigration authorities despite a prior grant of protection from removal, filed a habeas corpus petition and won release in federal district court. He asked for attorney's fees under the Equal Access to Justice Act (EAJA), which lets prevailing parties recover fees from the government in "any civil action." The district court denied fees, bound by Fourth Circuit precedent holding that immigration habeas cases are not "civil actions" under EAJA. The Fourth Circuit summarily affirmed. Montoya Palacios asked the Supreme Court to resolve a disagreement among the circuits on the question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the law's fee-shifting language, covering "any civil action" against the federal government, includes a habeas case brought to challenge immigration detention, making a winning detainee eligible for attorney's fees.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition on June 29, 2026. Briefing is complete, and the case awaits oral argument and a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

Montoya Palacios, as petitioner, asked the Court to rule that immigration habeas cases count as civil actions under the fee-shifting law. If the Court affirms the lower court's reading, immigration detainees who win habeas cases in the Fourth and Fifth Circuits would remain unable to recover attorney's fees, while those in circuits that read the law differently could still seek them. If the Court reverses, such detainees could seek fee awards when they win and the government's position was not substantially justified. A vacate-and-remand would set aside the Fourth Circuit's ruling and send the case back without deciding the fee question either way.
