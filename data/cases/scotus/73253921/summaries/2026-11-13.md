## In brief

A man who won his immigration detention case asks the Supreme Court to decide whether he can collect attorney's fees from the government under a federal fee-shifting law.

## What happened

Kevin Isaac Montoya Palacios, held in ICE custody after years on supervised release, won a habeas corpus case in federal district court, which ordered his release because his removal was not likely to happen soon. He later sought attorney's fees under the Equal Access to Justice Act (EAJA), which lets a prevailing party recover fees from the government in "any civil action." The district court denied fees, citing Fourth Circuit precedent holding that a habeas case is not a "civil action" under that law. The Fourth Circuit affirmed. Montoya Palacios asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling, and the government agreed the case was worth taking up because federal appeals courts disagree on the question.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law letting winning parties collect attorney's fees from the government in "any civil action" covers lawsuits in which immigration detainees go to court to challenge their detention. The government argues habeas cases are too distinctive to count as ordinary civil actions, while Montoya Palacios argues the law's broad wording and long legal tradition both treat habeas cases as civil actions.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been argued and is awaiting decision. Briefing and oral argument are complete; the case was argued on January 11, 2027.

## What each outcome would mean

Montoya Palacios, who won his case below, is now asking the Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit's ruling that blocked his fee request. If the Court reverses, he and similarly situated detainees in the Fourth and Fifth Circuits could seek attorney's fees after winning habeas cases against the government. If the Court affirms, the Fourth Circuit's rule stands, and detainees there remain unable to recover fees even after winning release, while the law would still allow fees in other circuits that read it differently. A vacate-and-remand would set the lower ruling aside without deciding the fee question for either side.
