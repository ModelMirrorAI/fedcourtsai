## In brief

Montoya Palacios asks the Court to decide whether a federal law letting prevailing parties recover attorney's fees from the government applies to immigration habeas cases.

## What happened

Montoya Palacios, held in ICE detention after years under supervised release, won a habeas corpus case arguing his continued detention was unlawful because removal was not likely anytime soon. A federal district court ordered his release, and after the government failed to comply and briefly placed him on a deportation flight, the court ordered his release again. He then sought attorney's fees under the Equal Access to Justice Act (EAJA), which lets a prevailing party recover fees from the government in "any civil action." The district court denied fees, bound by circuit precedent holding that habeas cases are not "civil actions" under EAJA, and the Fourth Circuit summarily affirmed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether EAJA's fee-shifting provision, which applies to "any civil action" against the government, covers lawsuits seeking release from immigration detention through a habeas corpus petition. Courts of appeals disagree: some hold immigration habeas cases count as civil actions eligible for fees, while others hold they do not because habeas proceedings are unique, hybrid proceedings that are not clearly "civil actions" under the law.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari, briefing is complete, and the case was argued on January 11, 2027. It now awaits decision.

## What each outcome would mean

Montoya Palacios, the side that asked the Court to hear the case, is also the side asking the Court to reverse the lower court's ruling that denied him fees. If the Court affirms, Montoya Palacios and similarly situated immigration detainees who win habeas cases in the Fourth and Fifth Circuits would remain ineligible for attorney's fees under EAJA. If the Court reverses, he and others in those circuits could recover fees after winning such cases, aligning those circuits with others that already allow it. A vacate-and-remand would set aside the Fourth Circuit's ruling without deciding the fee question for either side, sending the case back for further proceedings.
