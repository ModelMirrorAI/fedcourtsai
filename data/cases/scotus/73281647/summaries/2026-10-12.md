## In brief

Carlos Pena asks the Court to revive his claim that Los Angeles owes him compensation after police destroyed his print shop pursuing an armed fugitive.

## What happened

An armed fugitive fleeing police forced his way into Carlos Pena's print shop and barricaded himself inside. After a standoff, an LAPD SWAT team fired tear gas canisters into the shop, causing extensive property damage. Pena does not dispute that the officers acted reasonably and lawfully, but he sued the City of Los Angeles, arguing the Constitution's Takings Clause still requires compensation when the government destroys private property, even for a good reason. The district court ruled against Pena, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that reasonable, necessary destruction of property to protect public safety falls outside the Takings Clause. A separate concurring judge reasoned instead that a longstanding legal privilege allowing police to search and arrest means no property right was violated at all.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether property owners must be compensated when law enforcement officers intentionally destroy their property while reasonably pursuing a criminal suspect, and whether "necessity" can excuse the government from paying for property it destroys, even though several appeals courts have reached conflicting rules on this question.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for the October 9, 2026 conference, after full briefing including an opposition brief, a reply, and multiple amicus briefs. The docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Pena, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the Takings Clause requires compensation in these circumstances; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling against Pena stands, and Los Angeles owes him nothing for the destruction of his shop. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right, only that the case ends there.
