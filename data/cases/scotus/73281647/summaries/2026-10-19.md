## In brief

The Court declined to hear a print shop owner's bid to require compensation for property that police destroyed while pursuing an armed fugitive.

## What happened

Carlos Pena owned a print shop that an armed fugitive fleeing police invaded and barricaded himself inside. After a standoff, Los Angeles police fired tear gas into the shop, destroying Pena's equipment and inventory. Pena agreed the police acted reasonably, but sued the City under the Constitution's Takings Clause, which requires compensation when the government takes private property for public use. The district court ruled for the City, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that property destroyed through reasonable, necessary police action to protect public safety falls outside that clause. One judge agreed with the result but reasoned instead that a longstanding legal privilege allowing police to search and arrest meant no property right was ever infringed.

## What the Court is being asked

Pena's petition asked the Court to decide whether the government must pay compensation when police intentionally destroy an innocent bystander's property while trying to catch a fugitive, and whether a "public necessity" defense can excuse the government from that payment obligation. Pena pointed to disagreement among federal appeals courts over how far the government's law-enforcement powers limit this compensation requirement.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference multiple times between September and October 2026. On October 19, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Pena, as the party who asked the Court to take the case, sought to overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling and revive his claim for compensation. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Ninth Circuit's decision in place: Pena receives no compensation under the Takings Clause, and the City's win stands. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that ruling undisturbed, and the broader disagreement among appeals courts on this question remains unresolved.
