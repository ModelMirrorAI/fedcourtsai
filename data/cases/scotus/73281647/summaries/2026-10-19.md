## In brief

The Court declined to hear a print shop owner's bid for compensation after police tear gas, used to flush out a fugitive, destroyed his store.

## What happened

Carlos Pena owned a print shop where an armed fugitive fleeing police barricaded himself. After a standoff, a Los Angeles police SWAT team fired tear gas canisters into the shop, destroying Pena's equipment and inventory. Both sides agree the police acted reasonably and lawfully. The city never paid Pena for the damage, so he sued under the Takings Clause, which requires compensation when the government takes private property. The district court ruled for the city, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that reasonable, necessary property destruction in defense of public safety falls outside the Takings Clause. One judge agreed with the result but reasoned instead that a long-recognized privilege allowing police to search and arrest meant no property right was ever infringed.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether the government must pay compensation when police intentionally destroy an innocent person's property while trying to catch a fugitive, and whether a "public necessity" defense can excuse the government from paying compensation it would otherwise owe. Pena argued that courts of appeals disagree on these questions, with some recognizing a broad exemption for actions taken under police power, others applying a narrower necessity-based exemption, and one applying a search-and-arrest privilege, while at least one circuit rejects any such exemption.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference three times between September and October 2026. On October 19, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Pena asked the Court to take up his case and overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling. The denial leaves the Ninth Circuit's decision in place, meaning Pena will not receive compensation for his destroyed shop, and the city's win stands. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Ninth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision as the final word in Pena's case, while the broader disagreement among lower courts over whether the Takings Clause covers this kind of police destruction remains unresolved.
