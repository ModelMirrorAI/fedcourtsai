## In brief

Carlos Pena asks the Supreme Court to revive his claim that Los Angeles owes him compensation after police tear-gassed his shop pursuing a fugitive.

## What happened

An armed fugitive fleeing police forced his way into Carlos Pena's print shop and barricaded himself inside. After a standoff, an LAPD SWAT team fired tear gas canisters into the shop, destroying Pena's equipment and inventory. Pena agrees the police acted reasonably and lawfully, but sued the City of Los Angeles, arguing the Constitution's Takings Clause required compensation for his property anyway. The district court ruled against him, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding that property destroyed through reasonable, necessary police action in defense of public safety falls outside the Takings Clause. A separate concurring judge reasoned instead that a long-standing legal privilege allowing police to search and arrest meant no property right was ever infringed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the government must pay compensation when police intentionally destroy an innocent person's property while trying to catch a fugitive, and whether a "public necessity" defense can excuse the government from paying for property it damages or destroys.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a brief opposing review and a reply. It was distributed for conference three times, most recently October 16, 2026, and the docket shows no action on it since.

## What each outcome would mean

Pena, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the Constitution requires compensation in this kind of case, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If the Court denies review, the Ninth Circuit's ruling against Pena stands, and Los Angeles owes him nothing for the destroyed shop; such a denial would not mean the Court agrees the lower court was right, only that its decision remains in place.
