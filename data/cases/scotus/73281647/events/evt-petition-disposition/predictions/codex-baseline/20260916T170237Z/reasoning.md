The reasons against a grant remain substantial and keep denial well above 50%:

- The strongest opposition argument is that the supposedly conflicting approaches mostly reach the same result for reasonable police damage. The City argues that differences among police-power, necessity, and search/arrest-privilege rationales have not produced a square outcome conflict on materially comparable law-enforcement facts. See BIO pp. 22–25 and 31–32. A disagreement in reasoning matters, but is weaker than opposing judgments under the competing rules.
- The petition invokes Alford as contrary authority; the opposition distinguishes a sustained occupation of beachfront property under COVID restrictions from damage while apprehending a fugitive. That distinction is facially material. I do not count Alford as an independently verified, fact-matched circuit split. See petition pp. 5, 10–11; BIO pp. 25–27.
- The concurrence described in the petition, pp. 3–4, reached the same result on the property's historically limited right to exclude lawful police activity. The City's opposition also raises a state-property-right issue, expressly noting that it was not raised below (BIO p. 31 n.10). I treat that latter point as a contested additional complication, not an established preserved alternative ground.
- The Ninth Circuit's necessity rationale and the recent denials in related litigation, as described in the supplied briefs, leave continued percolation plausible. A Court willing to revisit this subject could also select the companion rather than this docket. The absence of a relist at the initial distribution supplies no affirmative Court-interest signal yet.
