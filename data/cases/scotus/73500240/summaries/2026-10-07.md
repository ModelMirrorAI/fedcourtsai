## In brief

A police officer asks the Court to revive his qualified immunity defense after a lower court let a fatal shooting lawsuit go to trial.

## What happened

Officer Jonathan Granado shot and killed Estevan Ramirez, a passenger who fled on foot after a car chase, firing six shots as Ramirez ran away. Ramirez's mother, Juanita Ramirez, sued, claiming the shooting violated the Fourth Amendment's ban on unreasonable seizures. A district court granted Granado summary judgment based on qualified immunity, a legal protection that shields officials from lawsuits unless they violated clearly established law. The Fifth Circuit reversed, finding disputed facts—including whether Granado knew Ramirez was armed and whether Ramirez swung his gun toward officers—that a jury, not a judge, must resolve. The full Fifth Circuit narrowly declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the shooting violated the Fourth Amendment, whether lower courts correctly identified the law that officers must follow in these situations, and more broadly, whether courts besides the Supreme Court can establish such legal rules at all. Granado also asks the Court to reconsider the historical basis for qualified immunity itself.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for conference twice, most recently for the November 6, 2026 conference, after full briefing including an amicus brief supporting Granado and the record from the lower courts was obtained. No decision on whether to hear the case has been recorded yet.

## What each outcome would mean

Granado, the officer, asks the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions itself, without yet deciding who wins. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Ramirez's lawsuit proceeds to trial and Granado does not get immediate immunity from suit. Ramirez's brief also notes the case's interlocutory posture—no final judgment has been reached—as a reason the Court might decline review regardless of the legal questions raised.
