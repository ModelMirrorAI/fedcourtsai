## In brief

A police officer asks the Court to review a ruling that let a fatal shooting lawsuit against him proceed to trial rather than ending it through qualified immunity.

## What happened

Officer Jonathan Granado shot and killed Estevan Ramirez, a passenger who fled on foot after a car chase, firing six times as Ramirez ran away. Ramirez's mother, Juanita Ramirez, sued on behalf of his estate, claiming the shooting violated the Fourth Amendment. A federal district court granted Granado summary judgment based on qualified immunity, a legal shield that protects officials unless they violated clearly established law. The Fifth Circuit reversed, finding genuine factual disputes—including whether Granado knew Ramirez was armed and whether Ramirez ever pointed the gun at officers—that a jury must resolve. The full Fifth Circuit declined to rehear the case, by a 9-8 vote.

## What the Court is being asked

Granado asks the Court to decide whether his use of deadly force was unconstitutional, whether the appeals court wrongly made him disprove immunity instead of requiring Ramirez's estate to prove the law was clearly established, and whether lower courts besides the Supreme Court itself can even set "clearly established" law for immunity purposes. He separately asks the Court to reconsider the foundations of qualified immunity itself.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including an opposition brief and an amicus brief, and was distributed for the Court's September 28, 2026 conference. The docket shows no record yet of a decision on the petition since that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

Granado, the officer, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions; a grant alone would not determine who wins. If it denies review, the Fifth Circuit's decision stands, meaning the lawsuit continues and a jury would weigh the disputed facts about the shooting, as Ramirez's estate has urged. Either way, the decision so far only concerns whether the case proceeds to trial, not whether Granado is ultimately liable.
