## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear two counties' challenge to a ruling letting a former detainee's lawsuit over inadequate jail medical care proceed against them.

## What happened

David Ray Gunter, who had a mechanical heart valve requiring daily blood-thinning medication, was held in Davie County and Stokes County jails in North Carolina for fifteen days in 2012. Both counties had contracted with Southern Health Partners (SHP) to provide medical care. Gunter's lawsuit says he was not given his medication for several days and was underdosed the rest of the time, leading to blood clots requiring surgery. A federal district court granted summary judgment to the counties, but the Fourth Circuit Court of Appeals reversed, holding the counties could be held liable under federal civil rights law because their contracts gave SHP final authority over medical decisions. Gunter has since died; his estate's administrator, Juliana Swink, now represents his claims. The counties asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The counties asked the Court to decide whether a local government can be held responsible for a private healthcare contractor's harmful customs or practices simply because it hired that contractor to run medical care at its jail, or whether this amounts to forbidden liability based solely on an employment relationship. Swink answered that every appeals court to consider the issue agrees that a government can be held liable when it hands over final decision-making authority over inmate healthcare to a contractor, and that this is not the same as liability based solely on employment.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, the Court asked for a response, briefing was completed over the summer, and the Court denied the petition on October 5, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Davie County and Stokes County, as petitioners, asked the Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit's decision reviving Swink's claims against them. By denying the petition, the Court left the Fourth Circuit's ruling in place, meaning Swink's lawsuit against the counties can continue in the lower courts. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fourth Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision standing without ruling on the underlying legal question.
