## Case-specific adjustments

The strongest upward evidence is the combination of a death sentence, an initial seven-to-five jury division, and allegedly powerful omitted mitigation. The petition describes severe childhood abuse and evidence contextualizing the sole prior robbery conviction. It invokes dissents by Judges Rosenbaum and Abudu and argues that the jury's initial division is record evidence within an already preserved ineffective-assistance claim, not a new claim that could be waived (petition pp. 3–13 and 23–32, especially footnote 8). These are substantial reasons to take the petition seriously rather than assigning a generic negligible prisoner-petition probability.

The principal downward adjustments are vehicle quality and the uncertain reality of the asserted split. The opposition argues that the jury-hesitation contention was not raised or exhausted, that the majority alternatively considered the initial division and found it insufficient, and that many cited circuit cases involved pre-AEDPA law, de novo review, or materially different facts (BIO pp. 14–26). Its account emphasizes that the jury ultimately recommended death eleven-to-one after approximately an hour of deliberations. The competing readings are allegations and arguments in the briefs; I have not independently adjudicated preservation or exhaustion. Even if Davis is right that hesitation is merely evidence within a preserved claim, the asserted alternative holding remains an obstacle to obtaining a clean answer to question one.

The split is less clean than a conflict over a categorical legal rule. Treating jury hesitation as relevant is not necessarily inconsistent with declining to make it presumptively decisive, particularly when reviewing a state prejudice determination. The petition's citations and the opposition's distinctions mix standards of review and different evidentiary records. On question two, the opposition points out that Rompilla's prejudice inquiry was de novo, whereas the state court here decided prejudice (BIO pp. 26–31). That limits the petition's claim that similarity in the omitted evidence compels identical treatment.

For general context only, I retrieved Strickland's discussion at 466 U.S. 694–96 through CourtListener. It directs a totality-of-evidence assessment and distinguishes record evidence from extrarecord information about the actual decision process. This gives Davis a credible textual argument about considering the jury note, but does not by itself establish that the asserted failure warrants habeas relief or certiorari. My small net adjustment from roughly 5.1% to 6% reflects substantial mitigation concerns counterbalanced by these vehicle and review-standard difficulties; it is a judgment, not a fitted effect estimate.
