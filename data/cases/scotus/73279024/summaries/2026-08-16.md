## In brief

The Air Force asks the Court whether its hazardous-waste permit renewal application is reviewable now, and whether it must study environmental effects first.

## What happened

Since the early 1980s the Air Force has destroyed waste munitions by open burning and detonation at Tarague Beach on Guam, under a permit renewed every three years under a federal hazardous-waste law. In 2021 the Air Force applied to renew its permit without first preparing a review of environmental effects under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA). Prutehi Guåhan, a Guam-based environmental group, sued, arguing NEPA required that review first. A district court dismissed the suit, but the Ninth Circuit reversed, holding the permit application was a final, reviewable decision and that NEPA still applied despite the separate permitting law. The Air Force asked the Supreme Court to review both rulings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions. First, whether an agency's submission of an application to renew a hazardous-waste permit is a final decision that courts can review right away, or whether review must wait until the permitting authority acts. Second, whether a law specifically governing hazardous-waste permits relieves the Air Force of NEPA's general requirement to study environmental effects before proposing such activity, even though the permitting process involves its own environmental review.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review in March 2026. Briefing is complete, and the case was set for argument on October 7, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Air Force, as petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling. If the Court affirms, Prutehi Guåhan's suit proceeds, and the Air Force would need to comply with NEPA's environmental-review process before seeking permit renewals of this kind. If the Court reverses, the suit would be dismissed, either because the permit application is not yet reviewable in court or because NEPA does not apply before the permitting process runs its course. A ruling vacating the decision would send the case back without resolving these questions for either side.
