## In brief

The Air Force asks the Court to decide whether its munitions-disposal permit application counted as a reviewable decision and whether it needed a separate environmental review before submitting it.

## What happened

Since 1982 the Air Force has destroyed hazardous-waste munitions by open burning and detonation at a beach on Andersen Air Force Base in Guam. In 2021 it applied to renew its waste-disposal permit for another three years without first preparing an environmental review under a federal law called NEPA. Prutehi Guahan, a Guam environmental group, sued, arguing the Air Force needed to study the plan's environmental effects first. A district court dismissed the suit, but the Ninth Circuit reversed, ruling the permit application was a reviewable final decision and that a separate waste-disposal law did not excuse the Air Force from NEPA's requirements. The full Ninth Circuit declined to rehear the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things: whether the Air Force's submission of its permit-renewal application counted as a final, reviewable decision rather than merely a preliminary step, and whether the Air Force had to complete a general environmental review before submitting that application, given that a separate waste-disposal law also requires environmental analysis before granting the permit.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the petition in March 2026. Briefing is complete, the case has been set for argument on October 7, 2026, and it now awaits that argument and a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

The Air Force and other federal petitioners asked the Court to take up the case after losing before the Ninth Circuit. If the Court affirms, Prutehi Guahan's lawsuit continues, and agencies in similar situations would need to conduct environmental review before submitting permit applications like this one. If the Court reverses, Prutehi Guahan's suit would be cut off, either because the permit application is not yet reviewable or because the separate permitting law satisfies NEPA's concerns. If the Court vacates, the Ninth Circuit's ruling would be set aside and the case sent back for further proceedings without a final decision for either side.
