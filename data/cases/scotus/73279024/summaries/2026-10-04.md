## In brief

The Supreme Court heard a dispute over whether the Air Force must complete environmental reviews before seeking to renew a permit to destroy hazardous munitions on a Guam beach.

## What happened

Since the 1980s, the Air Force has disposed of hazardous-waste munitions by open burning and detonation at a site on Tarague Beach in northern Guam, operating under periodically renewed permits issued under a federal hazardous-waste law. In 2021, without preparing an environmental review under a separate federal environmental law, the Air Force applied to renew its permit for another three years. Prutehi Guahan, a Guam-based environmental organization, sued, arguing the Air Force needed to study environmental effects first. A federal district court dismissed the suit, but the Ninth Circuit reversed, ruling that the Air Force's application was a reviewable final decision and that the environmental-review law still applied despite the separate permitting law.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court must decide two things: first, whether the Air Force's submission of a permit-renewal application counts as a completed, challengeable agency decision, or whether it is just a preliminary step with no legal effect until the permitting authority acts on it; and second, whether the law requiring agencies to study environmental impacts before acting still applies when a separate hazardous-waste law already requires its own environmental review process for the same activity.

## Where it stands

The case was argued on October 7, 2026, after full briefing by both sides and multiple outside groups, and now awaits a decision.

## What each outcome would mean

The Air Force, as petitioner, is asking the Court to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling in favor of Prutehi Guahan, which allowed the lawsuit to proceed. If the Court affirms, Prutehi Guahan's suit continues, and the Air Force may need to complete environmental review before future permit applications. If the Court reverses, the suit would be dismissed, finding either no reviewable action or that the hazardous-waste law's review process suffices. If the Court vacates, it would send the case back without resolving these questions for either side.
