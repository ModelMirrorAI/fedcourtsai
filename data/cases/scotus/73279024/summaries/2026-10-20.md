## In brief

The Air Force asks the Supreme Court to decide whether its permit renewal application was reviewable and whether NEPA applied before it sought that permit.

## What happened

Since the 1980s, the Air Force has destroyed hazardous-waste munitions by open burning and detonation at Tarague Beach on Guam, under permits issued by Guam's environmental agency under a federal hazardous-waste law. In 2021, the Air Force applied to renew its permit without first conducting an environmental review under a separate federal law requiring agencies to study the environmental effects of major actions. Prutehi Guahan, a Guam-based nonprofit, sued, arguing that review was required first. A federal district court dismissed the suit, but the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit reversed, ruling that the permit application was reviewable and that the environmental-review law still applied. The Air Force appealed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two questions: first, whether an agency's request to renew a hazardous-waste permit counts as a final decision that courts can immediately review, before the permitting agency has acted on it; and second, whether an agency applying for such a permit must separately complete a general environmental study, even though the permitting process itself requires some environmental review.

## Where it stands

The case was argued on October 7, 2026, after the Court granted review in March 2026. It now awaits a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

The Air Force, as petitioner, is asking the Court to overturn the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which favored Prutehi Guahan. If the Court affirms, the Ninth Circuit's decision stands: the permit application is reviewable now, and the Air Force must comply with the environmental-review law before seeking such permits, meaning Prutehi Guahan's suit can proceed. If the Court reverses, it would mean either that the application cannot be challenged until the permitting agency acts, or that no separate environmental study was required, cutting off Prutehi Guahan's claim. A vacatur would send the case back without resolving these questions for either side.
