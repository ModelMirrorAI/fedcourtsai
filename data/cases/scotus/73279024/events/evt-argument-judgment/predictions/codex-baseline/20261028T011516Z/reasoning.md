## Why the threshold ground leads

The petitioners' opening brief describes a district-court dismissal on both finality and NEPA/RCRA grounds, followed by a Ninth Circuit reversal. The relevant action is therefore central: reversing the court of appeals on finality can restore the dismissal without adopting a substantive exemption from NEPA.

The respondent has a serious response. Its brief argues that the Air Force completed its own decisionmaking when it chose continued open detonation at this location; another regulator's eventual approval does not necessarily make that decision tentative. It also identifies an immediate consequence: submission of a renewal application helps continue the previous permit beyond its original expiration. The strongest affirmance route combines that completed operational commitment with that continuation consequence. I retain 11% undisturbed probability largely for this reason, not because the case lacks environmental stakes.

But the transcript repeatedly narrows the dispute to **submission of the application itself**. Respondent's counsel eventually accepts that formulation while arguing it is inseparable from the operational decision. Thomas presses the distinction between an applicant asking permission and a regulator granting it, and repeatedly asks for an analogous final application in the Court's cases. Jackson independently stresses the potential for Guam EPA to revise the terms substantially and contrasts that situation with a completed decision within an agency's own control. Barrett asks both what precise action is challenged and whether the continuation consequence actually arises from the application rather than Guam EPA's inaction and the previous permit. Those are concrete weaknesses in respondent's finality theory, rather than generic hostility to environmental litigation.

Kagan poses the best objection to an overbroad government rule: large federal projects routinely require regulatory approvals, and that fact cannot make every underlying federal decision unreviewable. The government's broader suggestion that proprietary conduct lies outside agency action is correspondingly vulnerable. Yet Kagan also explores whether review could occur after the permit's terms are known. Sotomayor likewise distinguishes the submission question from a separate decision. I read those exchanges as room for a narrow unanimous holding, not as endorsement of the government's most expansive propositions.

On the second question, Gorsuch explicitly questions why compliance with both statutes should be excused absent conflict; Jackson and Sotomayor make related textual points. Kavanaugh is more receptive to displacement and worries about two rounds of review and unresolved future litigation. That division makes threshold resolution a useful coalition-forming route. The government's rebuttal concedes that a later order directing operations could be a final agency action, which helps answer the concern that its position necessarily forecloses all APA review forever. It does not settle which future action this respondent could successfully bring.
