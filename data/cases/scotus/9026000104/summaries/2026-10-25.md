## In brief

The Supreme Court agreed to decide which detention rule applies to a long-time resident facing deportation who entered the country without inspection.

## What happened

Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha entered the United States without inspection around 2004 or 2005 and has lived here since, applying for asylum in 2016. In 2025 immigration officers arrested him, and the government argued a statute covering people "seeking admission" required his detention with no bond hearing. He filed a habeas petition, and a district court ruled that a different statute, allowing bond, governed his case instead. The Second Circuit affirmed, agreeing he was not subject to mandatory detention. The government, represented by officer Philip L. Rhoney, then asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is which of two immigration detention rules applies to someone in Barbosa da Cunha's position: one that mandates detention without bond for people seeking to enter the country, or one that allows release on bond for people already present pending removal proceedings. The government argues the stricter, no-bond rule applies to anyone who has not been formally admitted, regardless of how long they have lived in the country; Barbosa da Cunha argues that rule applies only to people actively trying to enter.

## Where it stands

The Supreme Court granted the government's petition on October 1, 2026. The case has not yet been briefed or argued on the merits, and the government has since asked for an extension of time for merits briefing.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, through officer Rhoney, is asking the Court to reverse the Second Circuit's ruling, which favored Barbosa da Cunha. If the Court reverses, Barbosa da Cunha and others like him would be subject to mandatory detention without bond while removal proceedings continue. If the Court affirms, the lower court's ruling stands, and people in his situation remain eligible for bond hearings. If the Court vacates, it would send the case back without deciding who is right.
