The government's strongest route is to read the broad statutory definition of applicant for admission together with the detention command, treating legal admission status rather than present location as controlling. The lower opinion reports that two other circuits accepted the government's interpretation; I use that only as evidence, available in April, that the competing reading has substantial judicial support, not as a count of the current split. See Second Circuit opinion pp. 11, 14–20. A majority could treat section 1226(a) as a general detention provision that does not displace the more specific command for this class.

I nevertheless adjust downward from the general merits baseline because the respondent has a serious textual, not merely equitable, defense: 'seeking admission' can impose a distinct present-tense condition, and section 1226(a) is not expressly confined to previously admitted noncitizens. The unanimous lower panel also invokes statutory structure, longstanding administrative practice, and constitutional avoidance; its separate concurrence reinforces the textual objection. See Second Circuit opinion pp. 3, 12–21 and separate concurrence pp. 5–8 (PDF pp. 66–69). A change in administrative interpretation alone does not establish that the new interpretation is correct. These points create a substantial affirmance path even if the Court is generally receptive to immigration-enforcement arguments.

The forecast of a broad statutory holding is distinct from a prediction that every possible period or circumstance of detention is constitutional. I expect the Court to separate statutory classification from any individualized constitutional challenge. The correspondence mentioned in the snapshot is not available in substance, so I cannot assess whether it identifies a changed personal circumstance or vehicle issue. The small DIG allocation accounts for unresolved vehicle risk without claiming such a defect exists.
