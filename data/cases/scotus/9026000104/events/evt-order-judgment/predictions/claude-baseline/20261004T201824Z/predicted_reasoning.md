# Forecast: what the Court will do in Rhoney v. Barbosa da Cunha, No. 26-104

## Judgment class

**Reversed.** The Court will hold that 8 U.S.C. § 1225(b)(2)(A) mandates the
detention, pending removal proceedings, of a noncitizen who entered without
inspection and is present in the United States without having been admitted,
and will reverse the Second Circuit's judgment affirming the grant of habeas
corpus. Because the district court never reached the respondent's Fifth
Amendment claim, the judgment will be reversed and the case remanded for
consideration of that claim rather than decided outright for the government.
The label I commit to is `reversed`; a `vacated` label is the next most likely
outcome, principally through a Munsingwear vacatur if the respondent's removal
proceedings end, or he is granted asylum, before the Court decides.

This is a close call. I put the probability that the judgment below is
disturbed at 0.55, not higher, because the respondent's textual case is strong,
eight of ten circuits have rejected the government's reading, and the Court
chose a vehicle that presents only the statutory question. A 5-4 affirmance is
the clear second scenario, and what separates the two is the votes of Justices
Gorsuch and Barrett. A dismissal as improvidently granted is remote: the
question is clean, the respondent did not oppose review, and the issue affects
thousands of pending habeas cases.

## Vote lineup

**5-4.** Majority: Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Kavanaugh,
and Barrett. Dissent: Justices Gorsuch, Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson. Justice
Alito, who wrote Jennings v. Rodriguez and DHS v. Thuraissigiam, is the likely
author; the principal dissent is most likely from Justice Sotomayor, with a
separate textualist dissent or concurrence in the dissent from Justice Gorsuch
on the "seeking admission" language. The three Democratic appointees are near
certain to vote for the respondent; Justices Thomas and Alito are near certain
to vote for the government. The outcome turns on Justices Gorsuch and Barrett,
both of whom sided with the noncitizen on textual grounds in Niz-Chavez v.
Garland over a dissent by Justice Kavanaugh joined by the Chief Justice and
Justice Alito. I forecast that one of the two (Barrett) accepts the
government's deeming-clause argument and the other (Gorsuch) does not. If both
join the government, the case is 6-3; if both join the respondent, the judgment
is affirmed 5-4. I regard each of those as less likely than the split I name,
but not by much.

## The ground the majority rests on

The majority will treat § 1225(a)(1) as doing the decisive work: Congress
provided that "an alien present in the United States who has not been
admitted" "shall be deemed for purposes of this chapter an applicant for
admission," and the majority will read "an alien seeking admission" in
§ 1225(b)(2)(A) as referring to that deemed applicant rather than as imposing a
separate requirement that the person be actively requesting entry at a port.
It will say that the Second Circuit's present-participle reading gives the
"seeking admission" phrase a meaning the deeming clause forecloses, that the
1996 Act's stated purpose was to end the advantage noncitizens gained by
entering without inspection rather than presenting at a port, and that Jennings
already characterized § 1225(b)(2) as "a catchall provision that applies to all
applicants for admission not covered by § 1225(b)(1)." It will treat § 1226(a)
as a general arrest-and-detention authority that yields to the specific
mandate of § 1225(b)(2)(A) for applicants for admission, and will describe the
thirty years of contrary practice as agency choice that cannot alter the text,
with In re Yajure Hurtado getting no deference after Loper Bright and no
independent weight. It will not decide the due process question, leaving it for
remand, and will note that § 1182(d)(5) parole remains available.

## Breadth

The rule will be categorical. The majority will not distinguish long-term
residents from recent entrants, will not adopt a "threshold of initial entry"
limit, and will not condition the holding on how long the noncitizen has been
present or where the arrest occurred. The respondent's twenty years of
residence, his pending asylum application since 2016, his work authorization,
and his lack of any criminal record will appear in the dissent, not as
limits on the holding.

## Separate writings

The dissent will rest on the plain-text reading the Second Circuit adopted:
"seeking admission" is a present-tense condition the deemed status does not
supply; the government's reading makes the phrase surplusage; § 1226(a)
expressly reaches "an alien" pending a removal decision whether charged as
inadmissible or deportable; § 1226(c), including the Laken Riley Act's 2025
additions mandating detention of certain inadmissible noncitizens charged with
theft offenses, would be nearly pointless if every noncitizen present without
admission were already mandatorily detained under § 1225(b)(2)(A); and thirty
years of consistent executive practice under both parties, announced in the
1997 regulatory preamble, confirms the reading. Expect the dissent to invoke
the constitutional-avoidance canon and to call the government's reading the
largest detention-without-bond mandate in the Nation's history. I expect no
separate concurrence from the majority side limiting the holding.

## Timing

Argument in the February 2027 sitting (possibly January), decision in June
2027. The Court granted at its first conference by a standalone order on
October 1 rather than waiting for the October 5 order list, which is consistent
with scheduling the case for this Term. The Sixth Circuit (No. 25-1415) and
Fifth Circuit (No. 26-43) petitions will be held and disposed of in light of
this decision.
