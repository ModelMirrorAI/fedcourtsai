## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear a couple's bid to revive their challenge to a Virginia adoption order that transferred a child they had raised in Afghanistan to another family.

## What happened

A.A. and F.A. say they were raising a young girl, orphaned in Afghanistan, as kin and caretakers when a Virginia court granted Joshua and Stephanie Mast's petition to adopt her, without notifying A.A. and F.A. After the child came to the United States with them in 2021, the Masts took custody based on that adoption order. A.A. and F.A. then sued in Virginia courts to undo the adoption, citing fraud and lack of notice. A trial court and the Virginia Court of Appeals sided with them on state-law grounds, but the Supreme Court of Virginia reversed, ruling the challenge was barred by a six-month state filing deadline and that A.A. and F.A. had no constitutional right to notice because they were not the child's legal parents.

## What the Court is being asked

The question is whether, and under what circumstances, people who are not a child's legal parents but who have cared for the child have a constitutional right to be notified and heard before a court issues an adoption order that permanently removes the child from their care. The Masts countered that A.A. and F.A. had not properly raised this specific argument in the Virginia courts and that the Virginia Supreme Court's decision rested on an independent state-law waiver finding the U.S. Supreme Court could not review.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference more than once, drew responses and multiple amicus briefs, and on October 19, 2026, the Supreme Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

A.A. and F.A., as petitioners, asked the Court to take up their case and overturn the Virginia Supreme Court's ruling against them. Denial leaves that ruling in place, meaning the Virginia Supreme Court's decision rejecting A.A. and F.A.'s challenge stands, and the adoption order giving the Masts legal custody of the child remains valid. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Virginia court's reasoning; it simply ends the case without a ruling on the constitutional question A.A. and F.A. raised.
