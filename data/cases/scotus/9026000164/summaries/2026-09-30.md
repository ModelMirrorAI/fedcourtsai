## In brief

A.A. and F.A. ask the Court to revive their challenge to a Virginia adoption that gave the Masts the Afghan child the A.s had been raising.

## What happened

A.A. and F.A. say they were raising an orphaned infant in Afghanistan after the Government of Afghanistan identified them as her relatives. They say the Masts, a Virginia couple, separately obtained a Virginia adoption order without telling them or the United States, then took the child after the family evacuated to the United States together. A.A. and F.A. sued in Virginia state court to undo the adoption. A trial court and the Virginia Court of Appeals sided with them, but a divided Virginia Supreme Court reversed, holding Virginia's six-month deadline for challenging adoptions barred their state-law claims and that they had no constitutional right to notice because they were not the child's legal or biological parents. The Masts dispute much of this account and say the lower courts found A.A. and F.A. had not proven they were related to the child or had any legal relationship with her.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether, and under what circumstances, people caring for a child but who are not her legal parents have a constitutional interest strong enough to require that they get notice and a chance to be heard before a court permanently approves someone else's adoption of that child.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed, and the Masts waived their right to respond, but the Court then asked them to respond anyway. Both sides and several outside groups have filed briefs, and the petition was distributed for conferences on September 28 and October 16, 2026, with no action yet recorded on the docket.

## What each outcome would mean

A.A. and F.A., as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the constitutional question itself, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies review, the Virginia Supreme Court's ruling stays in place, leaving the adoption order intact and the child in the Masts' custody. A denial would not mean the Court agrees with the Virginia court's reasoning, only that its decision stands.
