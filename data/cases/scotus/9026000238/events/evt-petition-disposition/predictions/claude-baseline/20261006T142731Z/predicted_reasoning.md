# Forecast: Broadcast Music, Inc. v. North American Concert Promoters Association (No. 26-238)

**Bottom line.** The petition is more likely than not to be denied, but the grant family is live at roughly one in five — and within that family a hold-then-GVR is the likelier shape, not plenary review.

**Where the docket stands.** One distribution so far (September 2, for the September 28 conference). Respondent waived; on September 10 the Court called for a response, which pulled the petition from that conference. The response is now due November 2, 2026, after an extension. One amicus brief (ASCAP) is on file. No CVSG.

**Relists.** Expect at least one further distribution with near certainty: the brief in opposition arrives in early November, BMI replies, and the petition is redistributed for a conference in December 2026 or January 2027. So the increment from the single distribution shown is the ordinary consequence of the response request, not a relist signal in itself. Past that redistribution, the likely paths diverge: either a denial at the first conference where the full briefing is considered, or the petition is **held** for *RiseandShine Corp. v. PepsiCo* (No. 24-1016, certiorari granted June 29, 2026), which BMI expressly asks for in Part IV of the petition. A hold would mean no further conference action until *RiseandShine* is decided, probably by late June 2027, followed by a disposition in the summer-2027 clean-up orders. I put the hold at roughly 40 percent, so a long gap with no new distribution entries after the first redistribution is a real possibility; several more "relists" in the classic sense (repeated consecutive conferences) are less likely than either a prompt denial or a hold.

**CVSG.** Unlikely (about 8 percent). The United States is a party to the BMI consent decree and the Antitrust Division administers it, and the federal government litigates fair-market-value questions constantly (takings, tax, forfeiture), so an invitation is conceivable if the Court is weighing plenary review. But the likelier paths — denial, or a hold for a case already set for decision — need no invitation, and the question is one of appellate procedure the Justices can assess without the SG.

**Which question, if any.** If the Court grants plenary review it would take the question as written — whether a district court's fair-market-value determination and its component parts (benchmark selection, revenue base) are reviewed de novo or for clear error — probably trimming the "component parts" clause in the opinion rather than at the grant stage. There is one question presented, so no narrowing among questions is in play.

**Summary disposition versus plenary review.** Conditional on any grant, I put the cert-order route (a GVR in light of *RiseandShine*) at about 58 percent, above plenary review. The mechanics: if the petition is held and *RiseandShine* reverses the Second Circuit on a *Lakeridge*-style law/fact analysis, a GVR for reconsideration under that decision is the natural clean-up disposition; the Second Circuit's own de novo framing of benchmark selection is the kind of premise a GVR invites the lower court to revisit. Plenary review on this petition's own force is the smaller slice (roughly 9 percent unconditional): the Court has already taken one Second Circuit standard-of-review case this Term, the posture is interlocutory (the Second Circuit vacated and remanded for a new rate determination, so proceedings continue below), and the asserted nine-circuit split is assembled from takings, tax, bankruptcy, and Copyright Royalty Board cases rather than from courts deciding the same question — all of which the brief in opposition will press.

**If granted on the merits.** The likely ground would be that *U.S. Bank v. Village at Lakeridge* governs: a mixed question that turns on marshalling record evidence (which agreements are comparable, how to weight them, what revenue a willing licensee would pay on) is reviewed deferentially, with de novo review confined to the legal framework. The Court would most likely vacate rather than reverse outright, leaving the Second Circuit to re-review under clear error.

**If denied.** Expect no separate writing. A dissent from, or statement respecting, denial is unlikely (about 5 percent): this is a commercial rate dispute on a standard-of-review question, with no criminal-procedure, constitutional, or government-overreach hook that typically draws one, and a denial that follows *RiseandShine* would come in a clean-up order list where separate writings are rarer still.
