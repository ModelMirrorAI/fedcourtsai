## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear the Clemente family's bid to revive takings and immunity claims over Puerto Rico's use of Roberto Clemente's name.

## What happened

Roberto Clemente's sons and related companies, who control trademarks tied to his name and image, sued Puerto Rico's government and officials, saying a license-plate and registration-tag program honoring Clemente infringed their trademark rights and took their property without compensation. A federal district court dismissed the case, and the First Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed dismissal of the takings claim and most trademark claims, ruling Puerto Rico has sovereign immunity (protection from certain lawsuits) that the Lanham Act does not clearly remove, while reviving some individual-capacity trademark claims for further proceedings.

## What the Court is being asked

The petition asks the Court to decide two things: whether a rule requiring full compensation for government seizures of property should apply to intangible property like trademarks, not just physical property, and whether Puerto Rico can claim the same immunity from lawsuits that states have, and if so, whether a federal trademark law already removes that immunity.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference three times, most recently November 6, 2026, and the Court denied the petition on November 9, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Clemente Properties and the family members asked the Court to overturn the First Circuit's ruling. By denying the petition, the Court leaves that ruling in place: the dismissal of the takings claim and most trademark claims against Puerto Rico and its officials stands, while the individual-capacity claims the First Circuit had revived continue in the lower courts. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the First Circuit's reasoning; it simply leaves that decision undisturbed.
