I raise the grant probability from approximately 17% to **32%**. This is a judgmental adjustment, not a fitted model or an empirical conditional rate. The first relist is already reflected in the frozen elevated conditioning and is not counted again as a separate multiplier. The reasons for the additional uplift are:

- **A substantial property-law question.** The petition argues that appropriating a trademark's exclusionary interest should not lose categorical takings protection merely because the property is intangible. Its cited Horne, Cedar Point, and Monsanto arguments present a genuine doctrinal issue beyond the amount of this family's alleged loss. The reply directly answers the BIO's claim that declining to pursue Penn Central balancing makes the question unsuitable: choosing a categorical theory does not by itself prevent review of whether that theory governs. [Petition pp. 12–25; BIO pp. 8–17; reply pp. 3–8.]
- **An unresolved, potentially review-worthy second question.** The petition places Puerto Rico itself and its officials before the Court and expressly challenges the immunity premise the Court left undecided in Financial Oversight. That is a meaningful difference from merely rearguing PROMESA's abrogation language. The lack of competing circuit holdings matters less for a territory concentrated within one circuit, although it still removes an ordinary reason for certiorari. [Petition pp. 26–34; BIO pp. 18–22; reply pp. 9–12; Financial Oversight, 598 U.S. at 346 & n.2.]
- **Concrete indications of institutional interest.** The three amici and the supplemental brief's account of USPTO interest support taking the issues seriously. The USPTO material particularly increases the plausibility of requesting the government's views. It is still petitioners' advocacy, not a filed government brief, and I do not infer that the Solicitor General favors review. [Snapshot; September 21 supplemental brief pp. 1–2.]

Denial remains substantially more likely because the claimed split is not a clean pair of opposite holdings on governmental trademark use. The petition's Federal Circuit example, King, rejected a pension-related takings claim; its language concerning intangible property is not a holding for a trademark owner. The Texas copyright example largely aligns with the judgment below, while Judge Selya's contrary position is a concurrence. This is stronger evidence of doctrinal tension than of an entrenched outcome-determinative circuit split. [Petition pp. 15–16; reply p. 7; BIO pp. 10–17.]

The opposition also identifies real complications: petitioners retain use and enforcement of their marks; the underlying infringement claims survive in part against officials individually; and the immunity question is entangled with the choice between Lanham Act subsections, the clear-statement requirement, and preservation of a Territory Clause argument. I do not accept the BIO's characterization of all these grounds as independently dispositive without qualification: the reply explains why rejecting state-equivalent immunity could undermine some of those premises. Nevertheless, the Court can prefer a cleaner case or await further proceedings. The existence of one Justice's previously expressed interest does not establish four votes to grant. [BIO pp. 8–9, 15, 18–22; petition pp. 9–11, 29–32; reply pp. 11–12.]
