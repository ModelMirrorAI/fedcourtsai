## In brief

The Supreme Court agreed to hear and temporarily paused a lower-court order limiting how the government carries out deportations to countries not named in a person's removal order.

## What happened

Four immigrants with final deportation orders sued the Department of Homeland Security, arguing it must give people facing removal to a "third country" more notice and a better chance to raise fears of torture or persecution there. A federal district court in Massachusetts agreed, ordering DHS to follow specific notice and waiting-period procedures and declaring its policy unlawful. The First Circuit Court of Appeals largely upheld that ruling. DHS asked the Supreme Court to pause the order while it appealed, saying the lower courts lacked power to issue such broad relief and that the challenged policy is lawful.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the lower court had power to hear this lawsuit at all, whether it could issue relief covering an entire class of people rather than just individuals, and whether the government's approach to removals to third countries violates immigration law, constitutional due-process protections, or treaty-based torture protections. The Court also left room for the government to raise other related questions.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the stay DHS requested, pausing the district court's order, and at the same time treated the stay application as a petition for review and granted it. The case is now scheduled for full briefing and oral argument in December 2026; three justices noted they would have denied the stay.

## What each outcome would mean

DHS, the applicant and petitioner, sought both the pause and Supreme Court review. The stay now in effect keeps DHS's third-country removal policy operating as before while the case proceeds, rather than the stricter procedures the lower courts ordered. Once argued, if the Court sides with DHS, the lower courts' rulings limiting the policy would be reversed. If it sides with the immigrants, those rulings restricting the policy would stand. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the questions itself.
