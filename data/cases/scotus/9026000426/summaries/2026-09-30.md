## In brief

The government asked the Supreme Court to pause a court order limiting third-country deportations, and the Court granted the stay and agreed to hear the underlying case.

## What happened

DHS policy lets the government deport certain immigrants with final removal orders to a country other than the one in their original order, sometimes with limited advance notice. Several immigrants sued, arguing this violates due process and immigration law. A federal district court in Massachusetts ruled for the immigrants, declaring the policy unlawful on a class-wide basis and requiring more notice and a chance to raise fears of persecution or torture. The First Circuit largely affirmed. After initially pausing that ruling while the appeal proceeded, the First Circuit lifted its pause, and the government asked the Supreme Court to step in again, as it had twice before during earlier stages of this litigation.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the lower courts had authority to hear this case at all, whether they could issue the broad, class-wide relief they ordered given a law limiting such orders in immigration cases, and whether the government's notice-and-fear-screening policy for third-country removals violates immigration law, constitutional due process protections, or treaty-based torture protections.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the government's request to pause the district court's order while the case continues, and it also granted the government's petition to hear the case fully. The case is scheduled to be briefed and argued in the December 2026 argument session; Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson noted they would have denied the stay.

## What each outcome would mean

DHS and other federal officials, as applicants, sought the stay and asked the Court to take the case; D.V.D. and the other immigrants opposed both. The stay now in effect lets the government resume third-country removals under its existing policy while the case proceeds, rather than following the extra notice and screening procedures the lower courts ordered; it will end once the Supreme Court issues its judgment. When the Court later decides the case itself, affirming would uphold the lower courts' restrictions on the policy, reversing would uphold the government's policy as lawful, and vacating would send the case back for further proceedings without resolving the dispute for either side.
