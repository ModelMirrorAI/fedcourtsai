## In brief

The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether courts can limit how the government carries out removals of immigrants to countries other than those named in their removal orders.

## What happened

Four immigrants with final removal orders sued DHS, arguing its policy for sending them to "third countries" lacked adequate notice and a chance to raise fears of persecution or torture. A federal district court in Massachusetts certified a class and, after earlier injunctions this Court had stayed, issued a final judgment declaring the policy unlawful and setting it aside. The First Circuit largely affirmed. After that court dissolved its own stay of the judgment, DHS asked the Supreme Court to pause the judgment while it appealed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide three questions: whether the district court had authority to hear this case at all, given statutory limits on court review of immigration enforcement actions; whether the district court could order relief covering the whole class, given a statute restricting class-wide court orders in immigration cases; and whether the government's policy on removing people to third countries violates immigration law, due process protections, or treaty-based rules against sending people somewhere they may be tortured.

## Where it stands

The Court granted DHS's stay request, pausing the lower court's judgment, and treated the stay application as a petition for review, which it granted. The case is scheduled for briefing and argument in December 2026, with the stay remaining in effect until the Court rules.

## What each outcome would mean

DHS, the applicant and petitioner, sought to pause the lower court's judgment and have the Supreme Court take up the case; that request was granted, so the third-country removal policy may continue operating while the case proceeds. The Court has not yet decided who is right. If it ultimately affirms, D.V.D. and the other immigrants would win, and the policy's current notice procedures would be struck down. If it reverses, DHS's policy would stand as lawful. If it vacates, the case would return to the lower courts for further proceedings without a final answer from the Supreme Court on either side's claims.
