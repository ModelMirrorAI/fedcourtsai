## In brief

The Supreme Court is reviewing the government's third-country removal policy for immigrants with final deportation orders, after lower courts found it unlawful.

## What happened

DHS adopted a policy letting it deport immigrants with final removal orders to countries not named in those orders, sometimes with little notice, if the destination country has given diplomatic assurances against torture or persecution, or with brief notice and a fast screening if it has not. A group of immigrants sued, arguing the policy denies them required notice and a chance to raise fears of persecution or torture before removal. A federal district court ruled for the immigrants, declaring the policy unlawful and setting it aside for a certified class; the First Circuit mostly affirmed. The government asked the Supreme Court to step in after the First Circuit lifted its own hold on that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court must decide three questions: whether the lower courts had authority to hear these claims at all, given statutes that limit court review of decisions to execute removal orders; whether a federal law barring courts from blocking removal policies on a group-wide basis also barred the declaration and vacatur issued here; and whether the policy itself violates immigration statutes, treaty-implementing law, or constitutional due process protections by not giving immigrants adequate notice and opportunity to contest removal to a specific country.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the government's stay application, treated it as a petition for review, and agreed to hear the case, pausing the lower court's order in the meantime. Briefing is complete and argument is scheduled for December 9, 2026; the case awaits that argument and a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

The government is asking the Court to reverse the lower courts' rulings. If the Court affirms, the policy would remain blocked and DHS would have to follow added notice-and-hearing procedures before third-country removals, as the immigrants sought. If the Court reverses, DHS could resume removals to third countries under its existing policy without those added procedures. If the Court vacates, the lower court rulings would be set aside and the case sent back without resolving whose position is correct.
