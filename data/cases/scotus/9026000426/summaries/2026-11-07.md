## In brief

The Supreme Court agreed to decide whether the government's policy for deporting people to third countries, not named in their removal orders, is lawful.

## What happened

Immigrants with final deportation orders sued, claiming the government's policy for removing them to "third countries" not named in their orders denies them adequate notice and a chance to raise fears of persecution or torture there. A district court sided with the immigrants, declaring the policy unlawful and setting it aside, and the First Circuit largely affirmed. The government asked the Supreme Court to pause that ruling, arguing it lacked jurisdiction and that its policy satisfies the law. This followed two earlier rounds in which the Supreme Court had already stayed related orders in the same case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked three things: whether courts had authority to hear this lawsuit at all given limits Congress placed on reviewing immigration enforcement actions; whether courts could order relief covering the whole class of affected immigrants, given a law restricting such broad orders; and whether the government's notice-and-opportunity procedures for third-country removals violate immigration statutes, due process protections, or treaty-based torture protections.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the government's stay request, pausing the district court's order, and treated the stay application itself as a petition for certiorari, which it granted. The case has been fully briefed and was scheduled for argument in December 2026, with a decision still pending.

## What each outcome would mean

The government (Homeland Security and related officials) asked the Court to pause the lower court's order and is now asking the Court to reverse the rulings against it. If the Court sides with the government, the third-country removal policy would stand, and courts could not block it on a class-wide basis or on the grounds the lower courts used. If the Court instead affirms the lower courts, the policy would remain blocked, and the government would have to follow court-ordered notice and hearing procedures before removing people to third countries. A ruling vacating the lower decisions would send the case back without resolving the dispute for either side.
