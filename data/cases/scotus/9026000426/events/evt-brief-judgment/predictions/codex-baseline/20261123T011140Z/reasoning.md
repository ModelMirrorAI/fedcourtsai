The September 29 order is the largest adjustment. After the First Circuit upheld three declarations on notice and the opportunity to seek protection, along with the order setting aside DHS’s guidance, the Supreme Court stayed the district court’s entire final judgment. Sotomayor, Kagan and Jackson expressly opposed the stay. The Court had already stayed an earlier preliminary injunction. The second intervention carries more weight because the Justices had the appellate court’s reasoning and the final, differently framed relief before them. The two stay orders arise from the same dispute, and the government’s evidence about interrupted flights and diplomatic negotiations bears on interim harm. I would discount any inference that six Justices have already agreed on a ground for reversal. The First Circuit had itself set aside two separate declarations on the order in which DHS must try possible destinations for lack of standing. The live dispute concerns what happens before transfer to a newly selected country. (November 17 snapshot, September 29 entry. First Circuit No. 26-1212, pp. 3–4, 54–55, matched to the snapshot and briefs.)

DHS’s best point is that even the narrow reading of § 1252(g) in *Reno v. American-Arab Anti-Discrimination Committee* covers execution of an order. Choosing where to take a person under an existing order is one necessary step in carrying it out. That makes the connection closer than it was for the benefits program in *DHS v. Regents*. The harder case for DHS is O.C.G.’s. Respondents say the immigration judge granted him protection from removal to Guatemala, Mexico was first selected after his hearing, and Mexico sent him on to Guatemala. His original hearing offered no effective opportunity to present a claim about Mexico. This gives force to the First Circuit’s distinction between a challenge to removal itself and a challenge to a policy that prevents anyone from asking for the protection Congress provided. It also makes the government’s alternative reliance on the petition-for-review process under § 1252(b)(9) less persuasive. (Petitioners’ merits brief, pp. 15–25. Respondents’ merits brief, pp. 11–12, 20–30. First Circuit opinion, pp. 16–27.)

Two features keep the chance of some change to the judgment high even if DHS loses on jurisdiction. Under § 1252(f)(1), a majority could find that setting the policy aside and declaring steps DHS must take for the whole class effectively restrains execution. *Garland v. Aleman Gonzalez* gives DHS support on classwide injunctions, though plaintiffs obtained different remedies here. On the merits, the majority could also reject a single procedural requirement across the guidance’s two branches. When DHS accepts diplomatic assurances it allows transfer without further process. Otherwise it ordinarily gives 24 hours’ notice and refers a person who expresses fear to an asylum officer. Respondents argue that a blanket assurance cannot address individual danger and that the second branch can allow only six hours. Those facts, together with the Court’s insistence on meaningful notice in *A.A.R.P. v. Trump*, leave room to preserve part of the judgment or send it back for a narrower remedy. The First Circuit based its protection holding on § 1231(b)(3) and FARRA, using constitutional avoidance for the latter. A ruling for DHS on jurisdiction would leave the underlying protections open. (Petitioners’ merits brief, pp. 8–10, 25–42. Respondents’ merits brief, pp. 16–20, 30–53.)

Sotomayor’s opposition to the stay and her separate writing in *Aleman Gonzalez* make her the most likely dissenting author. Assignment remains substantially less certain than the lineup.
