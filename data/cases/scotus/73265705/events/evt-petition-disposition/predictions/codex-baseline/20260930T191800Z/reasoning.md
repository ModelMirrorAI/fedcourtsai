## Adjustment from the anchor to 16%

**Reasons to retain meaningful grant probability.** Google's two questions concern whether the PTO may deny IPR institution based on a patent owner's settled expectations and whether a court can review institution denials resting on allegedly unlawful grounds. The petition's introduction and Parts I–II argue that a patent-age presumption supplies a repose rule Congress withheld, and that agency discretion does not include discretion to exceed statutory limits. This is a concrete challenge to a consequential policy, not merely disagreement with a patentability assessment. The June 12 response request after the PTO's waiver demonstrates interest beyond passive docketing. Seven amicus filings, including patent practitioners, academics, drug-market and technology interests, corroborate cross-industry stakes. Those are filing signals only; I do not attribute unseen arguments to the amici.

Google's strongest distinction is that a challenger need not possess a right to institution to possess a right against application of an impermissible criterion. Its request to reconsider without that criterion is therefore not fully answered merely by observing that institution is discretionary. The petition also plausibly explains why exclusive Federal Circuit review can make a patent-specific circuit split unlikely and why mandamus is not inherently an unsuitable vehicle. I do not equate absence of a direct split with absence of certworthiness.

**Reasons denial remains substantially more likely.** The case arrives through extraordinary mandamus, and the federal opposition, printed pages 10–17 and 22–23, identifies obstacles independent of the broad policy question: alternative validity litigation, absence of a clear and indisputable entitlement, and the petition's failure to engage the mandamus requirements. These are respondents' arguments, not findings I independently established, but they give the Court substantial vehicle-based reasons to decline review. The federal opposition also characterizes the decision as unpublished and nonprecedential and distinguishes Google's asserted APA conflicts from this statute-specific institution dispute.

VirtaMove's introduction and vehicle discussion, and the federal opposition at printed pages 19–22, dispute Google's portrayal of a categorical six-year bar. Google says patent age was the only identified ground; the respondents describe a holistic assessment involving a fourteen-year-old patent and agency resources. Without the appendix or reply, I cannot resolve that disagreement. A contest over whether the premise is a categorical rule or a discretionary factor makes this a less clean vehicle for the broad questions than the petition suggests. The alternative APA procedural challenge described by VirtaMove is likewise its litigation position, not an assurance that Google can obtain the same substantive relief elsewhere.

The independently retrieved majority passage in **Cuozzo Speed Technologies, LLC v. Lee, 579 U.S. 261, 275 (2016)** preserves possible review of agency action beyond statutory limits in the context of final-decision review under section 319, while excluding ordinary institution-related statutory challenges. That supports taking Google's ultra-vires theory seriously, but does not itself establish review of an institution denial by mandamus. The provisioned federal opposition's discussion of Thryv and SAS presses this same distinction. I used those other cases as characterized in the briefs, not as newly retrieved holdings settling this petition.

Finally, the two distributions overstate the evidence of repeated merits attention: the response was requested three days after the initial distribution, extensions followed, the government opposed on September 11, and the next distribution followed the reply. The record does not establish an actual substantive June conference consideration. Thus, the combination of substantial importance and a requested response keeps the probability near the 17.2% reached anchor, but briefing-completion redistribution, government opposition, and mandamus/vehicle difficulties lead to **16% for any grant**, including any GVR or summary reversal. This is a judgmental adjustment, not an estimated regression or a mechanical conversion of legal merits into cert odds.

## Other probabilities and stakes

- **Another distribution: 24%.** Serious legal issues and amici make further examination plausible, but the existing count alone does not establish a genuine repeated-conference trajectory. This is an increment beyond two, not a forecast that two distributions will exist.
- **New CVSG: 0.5%.** The government is already a respondent and its views have arrived. The June response request does not count as a CVSG, and a new formal invitation would add little.
- **Summary route given a grant: 12%.** A narrow remand on reviewability is possible, but the parties dispute the statutory framework and the mandamus vehicle; plenary review is the likelier grant route. This is conditional, not 12% of all possible dispositions.
- **Separate writing given denial: 16%.** Judicial control of agency discretion may interest a Justice even without four votes to hear this vehicle. That possibility is not an individualized vote prediction.
- **Stakes: 0.67.** The breadth of affected patent challengers, drug competition, and institutional review questions warrants a substantial score, independently of the low grant probability. Specialized procedure and uncertainty over the reach of the actual policy keep it below a broadly transformative constitutional case.

The principal limitations are the unavailable reply, unexamined appendix and amicus texts, the parties' conflicting descriptions of the policy and agency order, and the absence of a matched empirical cohort for this particular mandamus posture. Those limitations warrant discounting precision, not treating missing material as adverse evidence.
