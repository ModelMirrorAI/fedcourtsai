## In brief

The Court declined to review Google's challenge to the Patent Office's practice of denying patent review requests based on a patent's age.

## What happened

Google asked the Patent and Trademark Office (PTO) to review the validity of a VirtaMove patent issued in 2009. The PTO refused, citing the patent's long-standing "settled expectations" due to its age along with other factors. Google asked the Federal Circuit for a writ of mandamus, an order forcing the PTO to reconsider without regard to patent age. The Federal Circuit denied relief, following its own prior rulings that such PTO decisions are not reviewable this way. Google then petitioned the Supreme Court for review. The PTO and VirtaMove separately opposed the petition.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises whether the PTO exceeded its authority by treating a patent's age as a reason to deny review, when the governing patent law sets no such age limit, and whether courts have any power to check that kind of decision at all, given a law that makes the agency's review decisions final and unappealable.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference in June 2026, after which the Court asked for responses; both the PTO and VirtaMove filed briefs opposing review, and Google filed a reply. The Court denied the petition on October 19, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Google, as the petitioner, asked the Court to take up the case. With the petition denied, the Federal Circuit's ruling stands: Google's patent challenge was not revived, and the PTO's practice of considering a patent's age when deciding whether to review it remains in place, at least as applied in this case. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed the PTO acted lawfully; it simply leaves the lower court's decision undisturbed, and Google could still pursue other routes, such as challenging the patent's validity in its ongoing infringement litigation.
