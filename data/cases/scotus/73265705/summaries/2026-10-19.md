## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear Google's challenge to the Patent Office's practice of denying patent review requests based on a patent's age.

## What happened

Google asked the Patent Office to review two VirtaMove patents it saw as invalid, through a process called inter partes review. The Office refused, citing VirtaMove's "settled expectations" in a patent that had been in force over fourteen years. Google asked the Federal Circuit for a writ of mandamus, an order directing the agency to reconsider without weighing the patent's age. The Federal Circuit denied relief, relying on its recent decisions holding that such challenges to the "settled expectations" factor cannot succeed on mandamus review. Google then asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The case raises two questions. First, whether the Patent Office has the legal authority to turn down review requests simply because a patent has been in effect a long time, even though the patent law does not set any age-based cutoff for seeking this kind of review. Second, whether courts have any power at all to check the Patent Office when it denies review for reasons Google says conflict with the statute, or whether that decision is entirely beyond judicial review.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court later asked for a response from the respondents. After the Solicitor General's office and VirtaMove filed briefs opposing review and Google filed a reply, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Google, which lost in the lower court, asked the Supreme Court to take up its case. Because the Court denied the petition, the Federal Circuit's decision stands: the Patent Office's refusal to review VirtaMove's patents remains in place, and Google's mandamus challenge is over. Google may still pursue other avenues, such as arguing patent invalidity as a defense in the ongoing infringement lawsuit VirtaMove brought against it, or separately challenging the Patent Office's policy. The denial does not mean the Supreme Court agreed with the Patent Office's approach; it simply leaves the lower court's ruling undisturbed.
