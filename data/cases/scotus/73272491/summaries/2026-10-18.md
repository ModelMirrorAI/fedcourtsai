## In brief

A man convicted of money laundering asks the Court to require juries, not judges, to decide the facts behind criminal forfeiture orders.

## What happened

Martin Mizrahi was convicted at trial of conspiracy, fraud, and money laundering charges for converting fraud and drug-trafficking proceeds into cryptocurrency for other defendants. The trial judge, not the jury, found by a preponderance of the evidence that $4,545,704 in proceeds was tied to his offenses and ordered that amount forfeited. The Second Circuit affirmed, noting that circuit precedent, following this Court's decision in Libretti v. United States, does not require forfeiture facts to be decided by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt. Mizrahi petitioned for Supreme Court review after rehearing was denied.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether to overturn its own earlier ruling that the right to a jury trial does not extend to the factual findings underlying criminal forfeiture, in light of later decisions requiring juries to find facts that increase criminal penalties, including fines.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond, but after the petition was distributed for conference the Court asked for a response; after several extensions, the government filed a brief opposing review on October 13, 2026. The docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Mizrahi, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether juries must decide forfeiture facts, without yet deciding who is right. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Mizrahi's forfeiture order and the lower court's approach to forfeiture fact-finding in place.
