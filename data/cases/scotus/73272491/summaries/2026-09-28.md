## In brief

Martin Mizrahi, convicted of money laundering, asks the Court to overturn a 1995 precedent and require juries, not judges, to decide facts underlying criminal forfeiture penalties.

## What happened

Mizrahi was convicted of helping launder money for two men involved in fraud and, the government alleged, drug trafficking. A judge, not the jury, found by a preponderance of the evidence how much of the laundered money came from drug proceeds versus fraud, and ordered Mizrahi to forfeit over $4.5 million on that basis. The Second Circuit affirmed, holding that circuit precedent and a Supreme Court case called Libretti allow judges to make these findings without a jury. Mizrahi asked for rehearing, which was denied, and now petitions the Supreme Court.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it should abandon its own past ruling that forfeiture amounts in a criminal case can be decided by a judge rather than a jury. Mizrahi argues that more recent decisions requiring juries to find facts that increase criminal fines should apply equally to forfeiture, since forfeiture is a form of fine, and that only the Supreme Court can set aside its earlier ruling that lower courts still consider binding.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for conference, and the Court then asked the government to respond. The government's deadline to respond has been extended three times and is now due October 13, 2026; the case awaits that response before further consideration.

## What each outcome would mean

Mizrahi, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether judges may continue finding forfeiture facts without a jury, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies the petition, the Second Circuit's ruling upholding Mizrahi's forfeiture order stands, and the case ends there.
