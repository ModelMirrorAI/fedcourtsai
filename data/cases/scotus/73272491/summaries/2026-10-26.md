## In brief

Martin Mizrahi asks the Court to overturn a ruling that let a judge, not a jury, decide facts supporting his criminal forfeiture order.

## What happened

Martin Mizrahi was convicted of fraud and money-laundering charges after a jury trial in New York. The trial judge, not the jury, found by a preponderance of the evidence that Mizrahi had laundered drug-trafficking proceeds and fraud proceeds, and ordered him to forfeit $4,545,704. The Second Circuit affirmed, citing this Court's 1995 decision in Libretti v. United States and circuit precedent holding that facts supporting criminal forfeiture need not go to a jury or be proved beyond a reasonable doubt. Mizrahi's petition says this rule cannot survive the Court's later decisions applying tougher jury-trial protections to other criminal penalties, including fines.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the rule allowing judges, rather than juries, to find the facts that support a criminal forfeiture order should be overturned, so that such facts would instead have to be found by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, as they are for certain other criminal penalties.

## Where it stands

The United States waived its right to respond, but the Court then asked for a response, which the government filed opposing review; Mizrahi has since filed a reply. The petition awaits a decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Mizrahi, who lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it will hear the case in full and decide whether judges may continue finding the facts behind criminal forfeiture orders; a grant does not decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling upholding Mizrahi's forfeiture order stands, and the government's position prevails for now.
