## In brief

Martin Mizrahi asks the Court to decide whether juries, rather than judges, must find the facts that determine how much property a convicted defendant must forfeit.

## What happened

After a jury trial, Mizrahi was convicted of fraud, money laundering, and related crimes in New York federal court. The judge, not the jury, found by a preponderance of the evidence that Mizrahi had laundered certain drug-trafficking and fraud proceeds, and ordered him to forfeit $4,545,704. The Second Circuit affirmed, noting that Mizrahi conceded this result was dictated by existing circuit law and a 1995 Supreme Court decision, Libretti v. United States, holding that the Sixth Amendment's jury-trial right does not cover forfeiture amounts. The Second Circuit denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

Mizrahi argues that later Supreme Court rulings on juries and criminal punishment have undercut the reasoning behind that 1995 decision, and he asks the Court to overrule it and hold that a jury, not a judge, must find beyond a reasonable doubt the facts that set the amount of criminal forfeiture. The government answers that the 1995 ruling remains sound and that every appeals court to consider the issue agrees forfeiture facts need not go to a jury.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, with amicus briefs supporting Mizrahi, a brief opposing the petition from the federal government, and a reply from Mizrahi. The docket does not yet show the case distributed for a conference since briefing closed, so it is awaiting the Court's decision on whether to hear it.

## What each outcome would mean

Mizrahi, who lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether juries must decide forfeiture facts, deciding nothing yet about who is right. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving Mizrahi's forfeiture order in place and leaving judges, not juries, to find such facts in future cases unless the issue returns to the Court later.
