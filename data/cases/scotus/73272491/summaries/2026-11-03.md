## In brief

A man convicted of money laundering asks the Court to require juries, not judges, to decide facts that set the amount of criminal forfeiture.

## What happened

Martin Mizrahi was convicted at trial of fraud, money laundering, and related crimes for helping two other men convert fraud and drug-trafficking proceeds into Bitcoin. At sentencing, the trial judge, rather than the jury, found by a preponderance of the evidence that Mizrahi had laundered $4,545,704 in proceeds and ordered that amount forfeited. Mizrahi argued the Constitution required a jury to find those facts beyond a reasonable doubt. The Second Circuit affirmed, following a 1995 Supreme Court decision, Libretti, that forfeiture facts need not go to a jury, and the Second Circuit denied rehearing.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether its 1995 ruling that forfeiture amounts need not be decided by a jury should be overturned, in light of later rulings requiring juries to find facts that increase a defendant's punishment, including fines. Mizrahi argues forfeiture is really a fine and should get the same jury-trial protection. The government responds that forfeiture has no capped statutory maximum, so those later rulings do not apply, and that every appeals court to consider the issue agrees with that view.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including an amicus brief from former federal judges and others supporting review, and was distributed for the justices' conference on November 13, 2026. The docket does not yet show a decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Mizrahi is asking the Court to take up his case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on whether juries must decide the facts underlying criminal forfeiture, deciding nothing yet about who wins. If it denies review, the Second Circuit's ruling stands, leaving in place the forfeiture order against Mizrahi and the current rule that judges, not juries, may find those facts by a lower standard of proof.
