## In brief

The Supreme Court declined to hear Martin Mizrahi's challenge to his multimillion-dollar criminal forfeiture, which argued that juries, not judges, must find forfeiture facts beyond a reasonable doubt.

## What happened

Martin Mizrahi was convicted in federal court of fraud and money-laundering offenses after a jury trial. The trial judge, rather than the jury, found by a preponderance of the evidence that Mizrahi had laundered about $4.5 million, including drug-trafficking proceeds, and ordered that amount forfeited. The Second Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, relying on a 1995 Supreme Court decision, Libretti v. United States, which held that the right to a jury trial does not cover forfeiture facts. Mizrahi, noting the circuit had to follow Libretti even while questioning it, asked the Supreme Court to take the case and overrule that precedent.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court was asked whether the right to a jury trial requires that the facts supporting a criminal forfeiture be decided by a jury beyond a reasonable doubt, rather than by a judge using a lower standard of proof, and whether an older ruling allowing judges to make that decision should be overturned.

## Where it stands

The United States initially waived its right to respond, but the Court later requested a response, which the government filed opposing review. After Mizrahi's reply and further briefing, the case was distributed for conference, and the Court denied the petition on November 16, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

Mizrahi, who lost in the lower courts, asked the Court to take up his case. Because the Court denied the petition, the Second Circuit's decision stands: Mizrahi's forfeiture order, based on the judge's own factual findings rather than a jury's, remains in place. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the lower court's reasoning: it simply leaves that ruling, and the underlying precedent Mizrahi sought to overturn, undisturbed.
