## What happened

Martin Mizrahi was convicted at trial of money laundering and related fraud charges after helping two con men, Joel Zubaid and David Goran, convert stolen and allegedly drug-related money into Bitcoin. At sentencing, the district court found by a preponderance of the evidence—not a jury verdict beyond a reasonable doubt—that Mizrahi laundered proceeds from wire fraud and narcotics trafficking, and ordered him to forfeit $4,545,704. Mizrahi argued the forfeiture amount should have required a jury finding, but the Second Circuit affirmed, holding that circuit precedent, following this Court's 1995 decision in Libretti, does not require jury fact-finding for criminal forfeiture. The Second Circuit denied rehearing, and Mizrahi asked the Supreme Court to take the case.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether it should abandon its own 1995 ruling that decisions about how much property a convicted defendant must forfeit do not need to go before a jury. Mizrahi's petition argues that later rulings, which require juries—not judges—to find facts that increase a fine or prison sentence beyond a set maximum, logically extend to forfeiture as well, since forfeiture works like a fine. Lower courts have kept applying the older rule, reasoning that only the Supreme Court itself can discard it.

## Where it stands

The government waived its right to respond, but the Court later requested a response, which has now been postponed three times, with the response currently due October 13, 2026. The petition has not yet been distributed for the Court's consideration since that request.
