# Predicted Court Disposition and Procedural Trajectory

The Supreme Court will **deny** the petition for a writ of certiorari on the order list following the December 4, 2026 Conference, without requesting a response, relisting the case, or issuing any separate writing.

## Conference Trajectory (`relist-increment` and `cvsg-increment`)

- **Current state and further distributions (`relist-increment`: 0.025):** The docket currently records **one** distribution (`distribution_count: 1`), entered on November 17, 2026 for the December 4, 2026 Conference, four days after the Solicitor General waived the Commissioner's right to respond. The Court will almost certainly dispose of the petition at this first conference without any further distribution. Neither a chamber call for a response (which would reset briefing and require a second distribution) nor a post-conference relist or hold is expected: there is no circuit conflict on 26 U.S.C. § 7623(b)(4), no pending Supreme Court merits case on Tax Court jurisdiction for which this petition could be held, and the Court has recently denied certiorari in counseled petitions raising the same jurisdictional question (*Li v. Commissioner*, No. 22-185; *Shands v. Commissioner*, No. 24-715). Only a small residual hazard (~2.5%) of a clerical reschedule or cautious call for a response separates this petition from a single-conference denial.
- **No CVSG (`cvsg-increment`: 0.001):** The respondent is the Commissioner of Internal Revenue, already represented on the docket by the Solicitor General of the United States. Because the federal government is a party rather than a non-party, the Court does not issue an invitation to the Solicitor General to file an amicus brief (CVSG); if the Court wanted the government's views notwithstanding its waiver, it would call for a party brief in opposition instead. A CVSG order is structurally inapplicable save for a negligible docket-anomaly tail.

## Conditional Scope and Route If Granted (`summary-disposition-route`: 0.75)

- **Question presented if taken (context-only):** In the remote event the Court granted plenary review, it would take only a narrowed version of Question Presented 1—whether a formal "denial" letter issued by the IRS Whistleblower Office after internal classification review constitutes a reviewable "determination regarding an award" under 26 U.S.C. § 7623(b)(4) when the IRS initiates no administrative or judicial action against any taxpayer—and would leave behind Question Presented 2's fact-bound administrative-record challenge and Question Presented 3's unsubstantiated circuit-uniformity framing. If reached on the merits, the Court would likely affirm or adopt the D.C. Circuit's reading under *Commissioner v. Zuch*, 605 U.S. 422 (2025), that § 7623(b)(4) confers Tax Court jurisdiction only over award determinations anchored to an IRS action under § 7623(b)(1)–(3), or else affirm on the alternative ground that a mandatory award is statutorily zero where no proceeds were collected.
- **Summary disposition vs. plenary review conditional on grant (`summary-disposition-route`: 0.75):** Conditional on any grant occurring (`P(grant) = 0.004`), a summary disposition in the certiorari order itself—specifically a grant, vacate, and remand (GVR)—is roughly three times likelier than setting this *pro se* petition down for full merits briefing and oral argument. Because petitioner proceeds self-represented on his sixth federal action advancing the same industry-wide healthcare-billing theory and undisputedly generated zero collected tax proceeds, the Court would strongly disfavor this case as an argued vehicle; a grant would much more plausibly arise only if the Court later granted a counseled Tax Court jurisdiction case during OT2026 or the Solicitor General suggested a narrow remand after a called-for response.

## Separate Writings on Denial (`dissent-from-denial`: 0.002)

- **No separate writing expected:** When the Court denies certiorari, no Justice is expected to note a dissent or file a statement respecting the denial (`0.002`). Although Justice Gorsuch dissented alone in *Commissioner v. Zuch*, 605 U.S. at 432, advocating a broader reading of Tax Court "determination" jurisdiction under 26 U.S.C. § 6330(d)(1) (`order-writers` probability `0.001`, highest on the bench), neither *Li* nor the far stronger counseled petition in *Shands* drew a noted dissent or statement, and the absence of any IRS enforcement action or collected proceeds here forecloses any practical relief for petitioner even if Tax Court jurisdiction existed.
