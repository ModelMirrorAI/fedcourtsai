I put the chance of any grant at 0.3%. Meidinger’s first question asks whether the Tax Court may review an IRS whistleblower award denial when the agency collected no money. The consolidated D.C. Circuit judgment provides a direct test of that premise. On Patrick Kennedy’s third claim, the IRS audited a taxpayer and collected nothing. The court exercised jurisdiction and Kennedy lost on the merits. On Meidinger’s claim, the IRS forwarded the tip to an operating division, which surveyed a return and declined to open an audit. The IRS’s final letter gave that decision to forgo action as the reason for rejecting his claim. The panel held that the referral and survey did not amount to action against a taxpayer under § 7623(b)(1). A ruling that collection is unnecessary for jurisdiction would leave that holding intact. See `questions-presented.txt`, `decision-below.txt`, pp. 6–7, 10–15, 24–26, and the matching judgment and IRS letter in `appendix.txt`, apps. A and C.

The best argument for review is the breadth of “any determination regarding an award” in § 7623(b)(4). The IRS itself told Meidinger he could petition the Tax Court. A court-appointed amicus developed the jurisdiction argument below, and a ruling could affect whistleblowers whose tips receive internal review nationwide. But the petition identifies tension between two decisions of the same circuit, *Li* and *Lissack*. The panel explained how both apply, and the December 19 rehearing orders in appendix A carry no dissent. The petition cites general principles of administrative review without identifying another court of appeals that would hear this claim. *Boechler v. Commissioner*, 596 U.S. 199 (2022), illustrates the Court’s interest in statutory limits on Tax Court jurisdiction. Its holding concerned a filing deadline and leaves open what qualifies as a reviewable award determination here.
