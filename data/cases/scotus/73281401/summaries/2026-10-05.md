## In brief

The Court declined to hear three sheriff's deputies' bid to revive their bid to dismiss a fatal-shooting lawsuit over how courts may treat police video at an early pleading stage.

## What happened

Deputies Zook, Martinez, and Guzman shot and killed Jason Roybal after a vehicle pursuit in Santa Fe, New Mexico. Roybal's estate, represented by Scott Fuqua, sued the deputies for excessive force. The complaint alleged the deputies shot Roybal while he was unarmed and fleeing on foot; it did not mention that he had earlier fired a BB gun at officers or that he was running toward an occupied vehicle, facts shown on video. The deputies asked the district court to dismiss the case and to consider that video, but the court declined and let the case proceed. A divided panel of the Tenth Circuit affirmed, over a dissent.

## What the Court is being asked

The deputies asked the Court to decide whether judges may look at video recordings when deciding, at the earliest stage of a lawsuit, whether a complaint describes a plausible claim, even if the complaint itself does not mention the video. They also asked whether a complaint can meet legal pleading standards by leaving out facts the plaintiff knew about, and whether a single past Supreme Court ruling on police shootings was specific enough to show these deputies clearly broke the law.

## Where it stands

The Court considered the petition at more than one conference, requested a response from Fuqua, and received supplemental briefing from the deputies. On October 5, 2026, the Court denied the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

The deputies asked the Court to take up the case and overturn the Tenth Circuit's ruling, which had let Fuqua's lawsuit continue. By denying the petition, the Court leaves the Tenth Circuit's decision in place: the case returns to the lower courts, where Fuqua's lawsuit against the deputies can proceed without the video being considered at this dismissal stage. The denial does not decide who is right on the underlying legal questions; it simply leaves the Tenth Circuit's ruling standing.
