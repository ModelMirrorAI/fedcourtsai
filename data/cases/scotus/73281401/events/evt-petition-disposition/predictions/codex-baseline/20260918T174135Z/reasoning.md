## Why 12%, rather than 17.2% or the whole-docket rate

**There is a real review argument.** Question 1 concerns the treatment of police video on Rule 12(b)(6) motions, not just the correctness of one excessive-force ruling. The petition, printed pages 7–13, identifies Sixth and Eleventh Circuit approaches and a divided panel below. Johnson's actual opinion, especially pages 2 and 13–16, supports a material part of that account: the Eleventh Circuit allowed central, authentic video under its incorporation doctrine even though the complaint did not mention it. This prevents dismissing the split argument merely by distinguishing an earlier Sixth Circuit case whose complaint expressly referred to video. The requested opposition is also an affirmative attention signal. These considerations keep the probability well above a routine petition's.

**But a favorable procedural rule may not change this result.** The opinion under review, CourtListener opinion 11196917, pages 14–16, expressly applied the Sixth Circuit test in the alternative and found no sufficient contradiction. It distinguished a suspect's movement toward an occupied car from movement in that car's general direction and considered the dropped weapon. That is a substantial vehicle obstacle even if the circuits differ methodologically. The petition and dissent dispute that assessment, but settling their disagreement would require the Court to engage contested case-specific facts rather than simply announce the governing pleading rule. I have not watched the videos and do not adopt either side's characterization as independently verified fact.

**The omitted-facts issue is less clean after amendment.** The opposition, printed page 6, reports that a third amended complaint was allowed without petitioners' opposition and added allegations about the chase and BB gun. I treat this as the respondent's reported development, not an independently inspected amendment order or a conclusive mootness determination. Even on that limited footing, it undermines the petition's portrayal of the originally omitted facts as a durable, clean basis for review. The opposition also argues, pages 2–5, that a change in the video rule would not alter the result; the lower opinion independently supports that narrower objection.

**The immunity question depends on the same disputed factual universe.** The petition's third question and discussion at printed pages 20–24 frame Garner as insufficiently particularized. That argument gains force if the officers' version of the encounter is accepted. At the pleading stage, however, the question is not whether their account ultimately warrants immunity but whether the allegations survive under the proper Rule 12 standard. Summary judgment remains a distinct opportunity to evaluate the evidence. I therefore do not count the three questions as three independent chances of review.

**The apparent relist signal deserves caution.** May's distribution was followed by a response request before the scheduled conference; July's distribution followed the requested BIO and the summer interval. Two distribution entries remain the correct scored starting count, but they are weaker evidence of repeated merits consideration than two post-BIO conferences. I keep the harness band intact while reducing its predictive weight for this particular sequence.

These vehicle and procedural qualifications outweigh the upward split, dissent, and response-request signals. My subjective adjustment from **17.2% to 12%** is judgmental, not a fitted numerical model. Denial is the modal disposition; the 88% complement includes a small residual possibility of dismissal or withdrawal rather than assigning every non-grant to denial.
