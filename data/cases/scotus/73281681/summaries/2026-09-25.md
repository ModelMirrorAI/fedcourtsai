## What happened

Property owners in New York's SoHo and NoHo neighborhoods live in units historically restricted to certified artists. A 2021 city rezoning let owners convert these units to unrestricted residential use if they pay a one-time fee to a municipal arts fund. A coalition of owners and residents sued, arguing the fee amounts to an unconstitutional condition on a land-use permit. New York's trial court rejected the claim, an intermediate appellate court agreed with the owners, and the New York Court of Appeals then reversed, ruling for the city on two independent grounds: the owners never had a property right to unrestricted use in the first place, and separately, the fee wasn't the kind of monetary demand that triggers heightened scrutiny. The owners then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the constitutional rule limiting what governments can demand from property owners in exchange for development permits applies only when the demand is a substitute for handing over land itself, or whether it also covers straightforward monetary payments unconnected to any land demand. The owners argue that money is property too and that limiting the rule to land-swap situations lets cities sidestep it by demanding cash instead. The city answers that its fee is a standalone payment unlike the situations the Court's past decisions addressed, and that a separate, unchallenged ruling below—that the owners had no property right at stake to begin with—independently defeats the claim regardless of how the money question is resolved.
