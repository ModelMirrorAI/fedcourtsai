## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to review a discovery order requiring the U.S. DOGE Service to produce records in a records-access lawsuit brought by a watchdog group.

## What happened

Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW) filed a Freedom of Information Act request seeking records about the U.S. DOGE Service (USDS), a body within the Executive Office of the President. The government argued USDS only advises the President and is not covered by the law. A district court found USDS likely does have independent authority and ordered limited discovery. After a earlier trip to the Supreme Court narrowed that discovery, the case returned to the D.C. Circuit, which again denied the government's request to block the remaining discovery through a writ of mandamus, an extraordinary court order compelling or halting action. The government now asks the Supreme Court to step in again.

## What the Court is being asked

The government asks whether the appeals court properly followed the Supreme Court's earlier instructions and the separation-of-powers principles that limit court-ordered discovery into presidential advisors, and whether courts may order broad discovery into an executive-branch advisory body to figure out whether records-disclosure law applies to it at all, when doing so might hand over much of the same information sought in the underlying request.

## Where it stands

The petition has been fully briefed, including a reply from the government, and the docket shows it was distributed for the Court's conference on September 28, 2026, with no action recorded since.

## What each outcome would mean

The federal government, through USDS and related officials, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the discovery dispute on the merits; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the D.C. Circuit's decision allowing the narrowed discovery to proceed stands, and the underlying lawsuit, including that discovery, continues in the lower courts. A denial would not mean the Court agrees the discovery was proper, only that it leaves that ruling in place.
