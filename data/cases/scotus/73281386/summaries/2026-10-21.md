## In brief

The federal government asked the Court to review a lower court's order allowing limited discovery into whether a government efficiency unit must comply with public records requests.

## What happened

Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW), a watchdog group, filed a public-records request with the U.S. DOGE Service (USDS), a unit within the White House, and then sued when USDS argued it was not covered by public-records law. A district court found USDS likely was covered and later allowed CREW limited fact-finding, including a deposition and document requests, to test the government's claims about USDS's authority. The government sought emergency relief, and the Supreme Court earlier narrowed part of that fact-finding and sent the case back. On a second review, the D.C. Circuit again allowed most of the fact-finding to proceed, and the government asked the Supreme Court to step in again.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked two things: first, whether the appeals court properly followed the Supreme Court's earlier instructions and respected separation-of-powers limits on gathering evidence from presidential advisory bodies; second, whether courts may ever allow this kind of fact-finding against a White House advisory unit to determine if public-records law applies, when doing so might give the requester much of the same information it is separately seeking under that law.

## Where it stands

The Court granted the government's petition on October 13, 2026. The case has not yet been briefed or argued on the merits; most recently, the government asked for an extension of time connected to merits briefing.

## What each outcome would mean

The U.S. DOGE Service and other government petitioners asked the Court to take up the case. If the Court ultimately rules for the government, it would limit or bar the fact-finding CREW has sought, making it harder for CREW to show USDS is covered by public-records law. If the Court rules for CREW and the other respondents, the lower court's approval of limited fact-finding would stand, allowing that information-gathering to continue. The Court could also send the case back without deciding the question either way.
