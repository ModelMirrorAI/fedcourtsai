## In brief

The federal government asks the Court to block discovery into whether its DOGE Service must comply with public-records requests like other federal agencies.

## What happened

A watchdog group, Citizens for Responsibility and Ethics in Washington (CREW), filed a public-records request with the U.S. DOGE Service (USDS) and sued when USDS refused to process it, arguing USDS merely advises the President and is not subject to the public-records law. A district court found USDS likely does exercise independent authority and ordered limited discovery into USDS's structure and operations. The government sought mandamus (an order forcing a lower court to change course) in the D.C. Circuit, lost, and then won a narrower order from the Supreme Court directing the appeals court to pare back discovery involving internal recommendations. On remand, CREW withdrew those specific requests, and the D.C. Circuit again denied the government's mandamus request as to the rest. The government now asks the Supreme Court to take the case again.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the appeals court properly followed the Supreme Court's earlier instructions and respected the separation of powers in letting the remaining discovery proceed, and whether courts may ever order discovery into an executive office entity's operations to figure out if public-records law applies, when that discovery might reveal much of the same information sought in the underlying records request.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for the Court's conference of September 28, 2026; the docket does not yet show any action taken at or after that conference.

## What each outcome would mean

The U.S. DOGE Service and other federal officials, as petitioners, are asking the Court to step in. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full briefing and argument and decide the discovery dispute itself, deciding nothing in advance about who wins. If it denies the petition, the D.C. Circuit's order allowing the narrowed discovery to proceed stands, and the lawsuit continues in the district court, though a denial would not mean the Court agrees that the discovery was proper.
