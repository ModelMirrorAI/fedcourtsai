## Why the estimate is far above the anchor

The **72%** estimate is a deliberately large, judgmental adjustment, not a fitted statistical effect. The ordinary baseline pool does not capture this record's combination of federal prosecution and the Court's own prior intervention in the same discovery dispute.

The strongest evidence is primary material already provisioned: **petition Appendix A, pp. 1a–2a**, the June 6, 2025 order in the earlier petition 24-1246/application 24A1122. It granted relief, required narrowing, invoked separation-of-powers concerns, and continued the discovery stay through disposition of a timely subsequent cert petition. Its recorded disagreement also indicates that the earlier intervention was contested. This historical order is legitimate pre-decision procedural evidence for the new petition, not the new petition's outcome; it strongly shapes my forecast. I do not read the continuing stay as a promise to grant again.

**Appendix B, pp. 3a–5a**, independently confirms what the D.C. Circuit actually did on July 14, 2025: grant mandamus as to the withdrawn recommendation requests, deny it otherwise, and refer back to its earlier conclusions. The government plausibly argues that this treatment did not sufficiently examine the remaining requests for directives, internal structure, and testimony. Its petition, pp. 14–25 and 32–34, explicitly seeks either plenary review or summary correction. The prior order's concern about internal communications extends beyond the single word “recommendations,” giving the Court a plausible supervisory reason to intervene without an established circuit split.

I give substantial weight to the contrary reading. **BIO pp. 16–24 and 29–35** argues that the appellate court complied with a targeted narrowing mandate, that the remaining requests concern actual authority rather than persuasive influence, and that the petition identifies no clean conflict requiring plenary review. Particularly important, BIO pp. 29–30 distinguishes the agency's designated organizational witness from Gleason's personal testimony about the declaration the government itself placed in evidence. I therefore do not adopt the petition's characterization of every deposition topic as an indiscriminate examination of the administrator. The unpublished mandamus posture, preservation objections, and factual disputes are real vehicle limitations. A Court satisfied that its first order was obeyed can deny review without endorsing a sweeping discovery rule.

My probability allocation is approximately 43% GVR, 14.6% summary reversal, 14.4% plenary grant, 27.5% denial, and 0.5% other nongrant procedural exit. Thus GVR is the largest individual category and grants total 72%. The 80% conditional summary-route forecast equals 57.6% divided by 72%; it does not say that unconditional summary disposition is 80% likely. A remand permits the Court to insist on tailored reconsideration without conclusively deciding all FOIA-coverage questions, which is why I prefer GVR to summary reversal.

The 65% additional-distribution probability reflects the likelihood of preparing a short corrective disposition and possible disagreement, not an observed relist hazard from the statpack. The 0.5% CVSG probability reflects the Solicitor General's existing participation, not lack of federal interests. Conditional on denial, 12% for a separate writing leaves silent denial the dominant forecast. The 0.86 significance score concerns executive accountability and confidentiality across government, while discounting that the immediate question is interlocutory discovery rather than a final FOIA merits holding.
