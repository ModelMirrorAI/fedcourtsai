## In brief

Two police officers ask the Court to review a lower court's refusal to shield them from a lawsuit over a fatal shooting during a welfare-check encounter.

## What happened

Officers Jessica Pitts and William Lewis responded to a call about Thomas Gay behaving erratically at his father's house. Within minutes, Lewis repeatedly used a taser on Gay, and Pitts shot and killed him. Taylor Burke, representing Gay's estate, sued the officers for excessive force. The district court denied the officers' request for qualified immunity, a legal protection that shields officials from suits unless they violated clearly established rights, finding that a jury could conclude the officers acted unreasonably. The Tenth Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding it lacked authority to revisit the disputed facts and that prior cases clearly established the officers' conduct was unlawful. The officers then asked the Supreme Court to review that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the appeals court wrongly treated a general rule against escalating encounters as clearly established law, whether the court defined the relevant legal rules too broadly rather than tying them to precedent closely matching the specific situation the officers faced, and whether the court improperly avoided reviewing the officers' legal arguments by labeling them factual disputes.

## Where it stands

## What each outcome would mean

Pitts and Lewis, the officers, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide the legal questions, without yet determining who wins. If it denies review, the Tenth Circuit's decision stands, meaning Burke's lawsuit against the officers may proceed toward trial. Either way, no ruling has yet been made on whether the officers are entitled to immunity from the suit.
