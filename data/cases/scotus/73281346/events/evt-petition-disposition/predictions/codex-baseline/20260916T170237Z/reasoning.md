## Substantive adjustment

The petition presents a credible route to the Court's attention: qualified immunity in a fatal police encounter, an allegation that the Tenth Circuit again used overly general precedent, and an effort to obtain clarification of officer-created danger after Barnes. Petition pages 10–21 repeatedly request summary correction and invoke the Court's prior interventions. In **City of Tahlequah v. Bond, 595 U.S. 9 (2021)**, independently retrieved through CourtListener, the Court granted certiorari and reversed a Tenth Circuit qualified-immunity decision because its precedents did not sufficiently match the officers' conduct. That makes summary reversal a real possibility here, not merely a generic tail risk.

But the opposition identifies substantial obstacles, and the provisioned appellate opinion supports several of them. The dispute concerns what the officers knew about a weapon, whether Gay threatened them, and how he moved when shot. The opposition, pages 14–17, explains why the autopsy's description does not necessarily settle those disputes without trajectory or expert evidence. I treat both sides' factual narratives as advocacy, not independent findings. An argument framed as purely legal is less attractive if it depends on replacing the summary-judgment factual assumptions.

The appellate opinion also expressly found inadequate briefing of further constitutional-reasonableness arguments (petition appendix 30a–31a), while separately deciding clearly established law. This is a vehicle obstacle, not a claim that every qualified-immunity question was forfeited. The opinion's specific comparisons for the shooting include Tenorio and Carr as well as Ceballos (appendix 37a–41a), and it separately evaluates the taser use. The case therefore offers more than a bare invocation of general excessive-force principles.

I do not accept the opposition's assertion that escalation played no role without qualification. Appendix 28a, footnote 12, expressly invokes the circuit's officer-created-danger doctrine, and appendix 38a–39a uses Ceballos's escalation reasoning. Petitioners have identified real language to challenge. Nonetheless, the court also reasons from an assumed unarmed, unthreatening person at the time force was used and cites additional analogies. Removing the escalation rationale may not dispose of both officers' claims. In Bond, moreover, Ceballos postdated the underlying shooting; the present incident occurred in 2019, so Bond's temporal objection cannot simply be transplanted to this record.

**Barnes v. Felix, 605 U.S. 73 (2025)** rejected a moment-of-threat-only time frame while leaving officer-created danger unresolved, as confirmed by the retrieved Supreme Court report's syllabus. That reservation creates an arguable issue, but does not itself hold the Tenth Circuit's doctrine invalid. Barnes also predates the November 4, 2025 decision below, which discussed it. There is no identified intervening decision requiring a routine Barnes GVR. The petition's reasons section does not develop a concrete cross-circuit conflict; its strongest route is alleged misapplication of existing law, not a demonstrated split requiring plenary resolution.

Taken together, I move modestly below the 17.2% reached-band anchor to **12%**. The call for a response and qualified-immunity summary-reversal precedent keep the number meaningfully above an ordinary unselected petition's rate. The factual disputes, limited preservation, multiple alternative analogies, and lack of a developed split prevent those signals from overcoming the denial-heavy prior. This adjustment is judgmental, not a fitted model or measured effect of those features.
