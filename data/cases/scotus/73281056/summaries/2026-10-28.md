## In brief

The Republican National Committee asks the Supreme Court to let Arizona require proof of citizenship on state voter forms and purge suspected noncitizens near elections.

## What happened

After the 2020 election, Arizona passed laws requiring voters who register using the state's own form to show documents proving citizenship, and directing county officials to periodically check databases and cancel the registrations of people found not to be citizens, including within 90 days of an election. Civil rights groups, the Democratic National Committee, Arizona's Democratic Party, and the United States sued, arguing these measures violated a federal voting-rights law and an earlier consent decree. A federal district court agreed in part, and the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit largely upheld that ruling, finding the citizenship-document requirement barred by both the consent decree and federal law, and finding the near-election purges barred by a federal 90-day cutoff on systematic voter-roll removals.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks the Court to decide two things: first, whether federal voter-registration law or an old settlement agreement stops Arizona from demanding proof of citizenship on its own state registration form, even though federal law lets states ask for information needed to check voter eligibility; and second, whether that same federal law stops Arizona from systematically removing people identified as noncitizens from the voter rolls in the 90 days before a federal election, a period when the law otherwise restricts bulk removals of voters.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued on December 8, 2026. It now awaits a decision on the merits.

## What each outcome would mean

The Republican National Committee asked the Court to take the case and is asking it to reverse the Ninth Circuit's ruling, which favored Mi Familia Vota and the other challengers below. If the Court affirms, Arizona would remain unable to require citizenship documents on its state registration form or to remove suspected noncitizers from the rolls within 90 days of a federal election. If the Court reverses, Arizona could enforce both measures. The Court could also send the case back for further proceedings without deciding the questions for either side.
