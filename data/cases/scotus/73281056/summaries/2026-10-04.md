## In brief

The Supreme Court is reviewing whether Arizona can require proof of citizenship for state voter registration and remove noncitizens from voting rolls close to elections.

## What happened

Arizona enacted laws requiring voters who register using the state's own form to provide documentary proof of citizenship, and directing officials to periodically check voter rolls and cancel registrations of people found not to be citizens, with no exception for the 90 days before a federal election. Mi Familia Vota and other groups, along with the Democratic National Committee, Arizona Democratic Party, and the United States, sued, arguing these provisions violate the National Voter Registration Act and a 2018 consent decree. The district court agreed in part and issued an injunction. The Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding the NVRA and the consent decree bar the proof-of-citizenship requirement for the state form, and that the law improperly allows removing voters within 90 days of an election. The Republican National Committee, which had intervened to defend the laws, petitioned for review.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal voter-registration law or an earlier court-approved settlement prevents Arizona from requiring documentary proof of citizenship for people who register using the state's own form, and separately, whether that same federal law stops Arizona from canceling the registrations of people identified as noncitizens if the cancellation happens within 90 days of a federal election.

## Where it stands

The Court granted certiorari on June 29, 2026. Briefing is complete, and the case was set for oral argument on December 8, 2026.

## What each outcome would mean

The Republican National Committee asked the Court to take up the case after the Ninth Circuit ruled against Arizona's laws, which the RNC had intervened to defend alongside Arizona legislative leaders. If the Court affirms the Ninth Circuit, Arizona would remain barred from requiring proof of citizenship on its state registration form and from canceling noncitizens' registrations within 90 days of a federal election, leaving Mi Familia Vota and the other challengers' win in place. If the Court reverses, Arizona could enforce both requirements. If the Court vacates, the Ninth Circuit's decision would be set aside and the case sent back without a final ruling on these questions.
