## In brief

The Republican National Committee's challenge to Ninth Circuit rulings against two Arizona voter-registration laws is awaiting oral argument before the Supreme Court.

## What happened

After the 2020 election, Arizona passed laws requiring proof of citizenship for state-form voter registration and directing officials to remove apparent noncitizens from the voter rolls, including within 90 days of federal elections. Mi Familia Vota, the Democratic National Committee, the United States, and other groups sued, arguing the laws violated the National Voter Registration Act and a 2018 consent decree. The district court sided largely with the challengers after a trial, and the Ninth Circuit affirmed, holding the proof-of-citizenship requirement barred by both the consent decree and the registration law, and the removal program barred by a federal provision limiting voter-roll purges close to an election. The Republican National Committee, which had intervened to defend the laws, asked the Supreme Court to review both rulings.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked to decide two questions: first, whether federal voter-registration law or an older consent decree bars Arizona from requiring documentary proof of citizenship from people who register using the state's own form; and second, whether that same federal law stops Arizona from canceling the registrations of people identified as noncitizens in the 90 days before a federal election.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case has been fully briefed and argued is scheduled for oral argument on December 8, 2026; merits briefs from both sides, the federal government, and numerous outside groups have been filed, and the record has been transmitted from the lower courts.

## What each outcome would mean

The Republican National Committee, which is asking the Court to overturn the Ninth Circuit's rulings, would see Arizona's citizenship-verification and voter-roll-removal laws upheld and the lower-court injunctions lifted if the Court rules in its favor. If the Court instead affirms, the Ninth Circuit's decision blocking both the proof-of-citizenship requirement and the pre-election removal program would stand, leaving Mi Familia Vota, the Democratic National Committee, and the other challengers' win in place. A ruling vacating the decision would set it aside and send the case back without resolving the dispute for either side.
