## In brief

The Republican National Committee asks the Supreme Court to let Arizona require proof of citizenship for state voter registration and remove noncitizens from voter rolls near elections.

## What happened

After the 2020 election, Arizona passed laws requiring proof of citizenship for voters registering with the state's own form and directing officials to check voter rolls and cancel registrations of people found not to be citizens, including close to federal elections. Mi Familia Vota and other groups, along with the Democratic National Committee and the United States, sued, arguing these laws violated federal voter-registration law and a 2018 consent decree. After a trial, a federal district court blocked enforcement of several provisions, and the Ninth Circuit mostly upheld that ruling, with a dissent. The RNC, which had intervened to defend the laws, appealed.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal law governing voter registration, or an older court-approved settlement, prevents Arizona from requiring documentary proof of citizenship for people who register using the state's own form rather than the federal form. The Court is also asked whether that same federal law stops Arizona from canceling the registrations of people identified as noncitizens if that happens within 90 days of a federal election.

## Where it stands

The Court granted review, and the case was argued; briefing, amicus filings, and preparations for argument are reflected in the docket, and the case is scheduled for oral argument on December 8, 2026, with no decision yet recorded.

## What each outcome would mean

The Republican National Committee, which petitioned the Court, asks it to reverse the Ninth Circuit's decision. If the Court sides with the RNC, Arizona could require proof of citizenship for state-form registrants and resume removing noncitizens from voter rolls even close to federal elections, and the lower court's injunction blocking those laws would be lifted. If the Court affirms the Ninth Circuit instead, Arizona would remain barred from enforcing those specific requirements, consistent with the injunction already in place, and the decree and federal-law interpretations relied on by Mi Familia Vota and the other challengers would stand. A ruling to vacate would set aside the Ninth Circuit's decision and send the case back for further proceedings without resolving which side is correct.
