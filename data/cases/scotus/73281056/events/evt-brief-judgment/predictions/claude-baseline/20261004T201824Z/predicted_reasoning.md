# Forecast: what the Court will do in RNC v. Mi Familia Vota (No. 25-1017)

**Judgment class.** The Court will **reverse** the Ninth Circuit's judgment on both
questions presented and remand. Reversal in full is the single most likely label
(roughly 45%), ahead of a vacatur with instructions (about 25%) and an
affirmed-in-part / reversed-in-part judgment (about 20%) in which the Court
upholds the consent-decree ground while rejecting the NVRA holdings. An outright
affirmance is unlikely (about 7%), and a dismissal as improvidently granted is a
real but small risk (about 3%), driven by the respondents' challenge to the RNC's
standing as a lone intervenor-appellant on the 90-day question.

**Vote lineup.** 6–3. Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Thomas, Alito, Gorsuch,
Kavanaugh, and Barrett in the majority; Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson in
dissent. The weakest link in that lineup is Justice Barrett, who would have denied
the 2024 stay in full; the alternative is a 5–4 judgment with her concurring in
part or dissenting on the 90-day question. The least likely fracture is on the
NVRA state-form question, where Inter Tribal Council's text may draw Justice Kagan
into partial agreement, so the registration holding could command more than six
votes even while the judgment line splits 6–3.

**Which question the Court reaches.** Both. On the first question the Court will
decide the NVRA issue rather than resting on the consent decree, because the
Ninth Circuit's NVRA holding is an independent ground that would keep the
injunction alive regardless of what happens to the decree. It will reject the
Ninth Circuit's "necessary means essential" and "equivalent means virtually
identical" readings, drawing on Inter Tribal Council's description of the state
form as a more demanding alternative to the federal backstop, and it will add that
the statute must be read to avoid doubts about the States' power over voter
qualifications. On the consent decree it will hold that a decree entered on
consent alone, which disclaimed any federal violation and was premised on
compliance with then-existing state law, cannot support a new injunction against
a later-enacted statute, citing Horne v. Flores, Rufo, and Frew; it will point the
parties to Rule 60(b) in the issuing court for the decree's own future rather than
declaring the decree void. On the second question it will adopt Judge Bumatay's
structural reading of §20507: noncitizens who were never eligible are not
"ineligible voters" within the 90-day provision, so a program to cancel their
registrations may run inside the window.

**Breadth.** The rule will be categorical rather than tied to Arizona's statutory
definition of eligibility or to the trial record. The Court will say in terms that
States may require proof of citizenship on their own forms for federal elections
and that the 90-day bar does not reach noncitizen removals. The consent-decree
holding will be narrower, confined to this decree's features.

**Separate writings.** Expect at least one concurrence from the Thomas/Alito/
Gorsuch wing pressing the constitutional point that Congress cannot stop a State
from enforcing its qualifications, and a principal dissent (most likely by Justice
Kagan or Justice Sotomayor) arguing that "necessary" means essential given the
word "only," that the 1993 Congress built the attestation as the citizenship
check, and that "any program" to remove "ineligible voters" plainly covers a
noncitizen purge. A possible partial writing by Justice Barrett on the standing of
the RNC to press the 90-day question alone.

**Procedural exits.** Standing is the only live one. The respondents argue that
the RNC, an intervenor-defendant, needs independent Article III standing to appeal
the 90-day ruling because no state party appealed it. The Court granted without
adding a standing question, which signals it does not see a jurisdictional
problem; the likeliest treatment is a paragraph finding standing on the RNC's
organizational and candidate interests or on the legislators' parallel appeal.
A DIG or a partial vacatur for want of jurisdiction is possible but not expected.

**Timing.** Respondents' adverse merits briefs are due October 13, 2026, so
argument falls in the December 2026 or January 2027 sitting and the judgment in
the spring of 2027, most likely May or June. Nothing resolves this horizon claim;
it is stated because it disciplines the rest.
