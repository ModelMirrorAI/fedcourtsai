## Why 0.84 rather than the 0.698 baseline

The strongest upward adjustment comes from the state-form issue, not from relists, the caption, or the fact of certiorari alone. The RNC merits brief, printed pp. 14–18, invokes the NVRA's separate state-form authorization and the federal-form backstop. Arizona's partial-support brief, printed pp. 12–20, independently agrees that the Ninth Circuit's statutory ruling is wrong. I checked the underlying passage in **Arizona v. Inter Tribal Council of Arizona, Inc., 570 U.S. 1, 12–13 (2013)** through CourtListener, lead opinion 9515775. That passage distinguishes state-specific forms from the federally guaranteed registration alternative. It supports a substantial prospect of correction without requiring the Court to abandon its federal-form precedent. It does not itself decide every necessity or equivalence issue now presented.

The decree is the main obstacle to converting a statutory victory into the particular judgment forecast. The RNC brief, printed pp. 33–40, argues that an earlier consent decree cannot independently justify a new statewide injunction after the law changes. Arizona's brief, printed pp. 20–27, makes the stronger counterargument that a federal decree remains binding until properly modified, and identifies the missing Rule 60(b) analysis. I treat that counterargument seriously. A remand for decree-specific analysis or a partial affirmance is more plausible than an opinion declaring all consent decrees automatically displaced by later state statutes. This is why P(disturbed) is appreciably higher than my probability of outright reversal.

On the 90-day issue, the RNC's argument distinguishes never-eligible noncitizens from persons who later lost eligibility (merits brief, printed pp. 41–44 and summary pp. 16–17). The retained opposition gives a substantial textual answer: the provision covers systematic removal of ineligible voters, and its enumerated exceptions do not expressly include citizenship-based removals (retained BIO, printed pp. 11–16). Administrative error can affect citizens even if actual noncitizens cannot vote. The existence of that counterargument, and the lack of an opposing merits brief on this issue, prevent a near-certain forecast. My prediction that the RNC ultimately prevails here is less secure than the state-form prediction; it is a forecast of the Court's likely reading, not a conclusion that the competing textual argument is weak.

For clarity, my approximate judgment allocation is: reversed 0.56, vacated 0.18, affirmed-in-part-reversed-in-part 0.10, affirmed 0.14, and DIG 0.02. The first three sum to the committed 0.84. An equally divided outcome has negligible weight in this rounded allocation absent evidence of a recusal. These are subjective forecast weights, not observed frequencies or fitted estimates.

This is an inferential lineup, not a report of their views in this case; I have no argument transcript or justice-specific merits statements. Writing roles are left unspecified.
