I reduce the 5.12% anchor to **2% for any grant** for the following case-specific reasons:

- The petition identifies its issues as first-impression questions, not a developed conflict between courts deciding materially identical federal questions. Its argument emphasizes error correction and prospective financing consequences. The cited older restitution cases support its merits position but do not, by themselves, demonstrate a present square split. Sources: QPs and petition pp. 9–19.
- The petition describes the Colorado appellate opinion as unpublished and nonprecedential under the cited state rule. That limits the demonstrated breadth of the asserted adverse rule and makes further percolation more plausible. Source: petition p. 1.
- Vehicle complexity is substantial: the levy was recharacterized as a special assessment in earlier litigation; another parcel was excluded; the remaining parcel changed hands; and the purchaser allegedly knew both the levy and the absence of further improvements. The petition itself quotes the lower court's reliance on Colorado due-process jurisprudence and state restitution doctrine. Whether there is a clean, independently dispositive federal question is therefore a concern, not a jurisdictional defect I claim to have established. Sources: petition pp. 6–10, 13, 16–19.
- Both respondents waived responses and the supplied record shows no subsequent response request. That is a modest negative attention signal, not proof that the Court has rejected the petition, and the first conference remains in the future. Source: snapshot proceedings.

There is a real counterweight. The petition reports approximately $30.485 million in bonds and argues that repudiation of assessment-backed obligations threatens broader municipal financing. Its second question invokes a recognizable restitution principle rather than merely a disagreement over damages. Those considerations keep the grant probability above a negligible floor. Source: petition pp. 3–6 and 15–19. The dollar figure and asserted spillovers are attributed to the petition, not verified market measurements.

For a limited doctrinal check I retrieved only the historical precedent Palazzolo v. Rhode Island, 533 U.S. 606, 626–630 (2001), through CourtListener. Its rejection of a categorical bar against subsequent owners' regulatory-takings claims makes the petition's reliance on the buyer's prior knowledge less obviously decisive. This is an analogy, not a holding about special assessments or this debt. I do not infer that notice is irrelevant under Penn Central or that Palazzolo settles the present due-process issue. The check supports caution about summary correction rather than a confident merits forecast. CourtListener lead-opinion ID 9434149, notice-search excerpts, supplies the primary text.
