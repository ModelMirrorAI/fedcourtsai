## In brief

Southern Company asks the Court to review a ruling that pension plans must use reasonable assumptions when calculating certain retirement benefits under federal law.

## What happened

William Drummond and Richard Odom, participants in the Southern Company Pension Plan, sued the company, its pension plan, and its benefits committee, claiming the plan used outdated mortality and interest-rate assumptions that reduced their monthly retirement payments below what federal pension law requires. A district court dismissed their claims, ruling that the law does not require plans to use any particular or reasonable assumptions. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, holding that the law's requirement that two forms of retirement benefit be of equal value implicitly requires reasonable, realistic assumptions, and that Southern Company's benefit calculations could therefore proceed past dismissal.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal pension law provision requiring two forms of retirement payout to be of equal value also requires that the assumptions used in that calculation be reasonable, even though the law's text does not use the word "reasonable" in that provision.

## Where it stands

Southern Company filed its petition on September 29, 2026. Drummond and Odom waived their right to respond, but the Court later asked for a response, now due December 18, 2026, after a filing deadline extension. The case has not yet been scheduled for the Court to decide whether to hear it.

## What each outcome would mean

Southern Company, which lost in the lower court, is asking the Court to take up the case and reverse. The petition also asks the Court to hold this case until it rules on two similar pending petitions raising the same question. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments and decide whether reasonableness is required; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Eleventh Circuit's ruling favoring Drummond and Odom stands, and their lawsuit continues in the lower courts.
