## In brief

Southern Company asks the Court to decide whether pension law requires retirement benefit calculations to use realistic, reasonable assumptions about life expectancy and interest rates.

## What happened

William Drummond and Richard Odom, participants in the Southern Company Pension Plan, sued the company, its pension plan, and its benefits committee, claiming the plan calculated their monthly retirement payments using mortality tables and interest rates that were decades out of date. They argued this shortchanged them under a federal pension law provision requiring that a married couple's joint payment option be the "actuarial equivalent" of a single retiree's payment, and under a related rule against forfeiting earned benefits. The district court dismissed their claims, ruling the law does not require any particular assumptions. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, holding that calculations must use actuarial assumptions a reasonable actuary would consider current and realistic.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a federal pension law's requirement that two retirement payment options be of equal value also means the assumptions used to calculate that value must be reasonable and up to date, or whether plans may use any assumptions they choose as long as they are written down in advance, however outdated.

## Where it stands

Southern Company's petition was filed September 29, 2026; Drummond and Odom waived their right to respond. The case was distributed for the Court's November 6, 2026 conference, and the docket shows no action since. The petition also asks the Court to hold the case pending its decision in two other petitions, FedEx v. Watt and Bakery Confectionery v. Reichert, raising the same question.

## What each outcome would mean

Southern Company, the plan, and its benefits committee are asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear the case and decide what the pension law's equivalence requirement means; a grant would not itself decide who is right. If the Court denies the petition, the Eleventh Circuit's ruling favoring Drummond and Odom stands, and their case continues in the lower courts. The Court could also hold the petition pending related cases, or grant, vacate, and send it back for reconsideration without deciding the question itself.
