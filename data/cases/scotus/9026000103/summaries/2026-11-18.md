## In brief

Owners of seized Iranian oil cargo ask the Court to hear their challenge to how broadly federal courts may read Congress's power to regulate foreign commerce in terrorism-related forfeiture cases.

## What happened

The United States seized over 700,000 barrels of crude oil from two tankers, alleging the oil belonged to Iran's state oil company, which the government says funds a designated terrorist organization. Aspan Petrokimya, a Turkish trading company, claimed ownership and fought the forfeiture, arguing the government hadn't shown the activity affected foreign commerce as the law requires. The district court disagreed, entered judgment for the government, and the D.C. Circuit affirmed, holding its precedent required treating foreign commerce the same as interstate commerce, reaching conduct that merely affects commerce broadly.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a law reaching conduct that "affects" foreign commerce can cover activity happening entirely abroad between foreign parties, or whether it instead requires a closer connection, such as commerce conducted directly with the United States. The petitioners argue the narrower reading is correct and say other appeals courts have disagreed with the approach the lower court used here.

## Where it stands

The government filed a brief opposing review, and the petition was distributed for the Court's December 4, 2026 conference. The docket does not yet show any decision on whether the Court will hear the case.

## What each outcome would mean

Aspan and the cargo owners, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up and reverse the D.C. Circuit's ruling, which upheld forfeiture in the government's favor. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full briefing and argument and decide the legal question itself, without yet deciding who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the D.C. Circuit's decision stands, leaving the oil forfeited to the United States and Aspan's challenge unsuccessful.
