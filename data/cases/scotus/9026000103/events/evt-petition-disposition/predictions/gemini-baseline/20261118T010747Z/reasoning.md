## Case-Specific Adjustments to `P(grant) = 0.015`
Starting from the `0.0268` zero-amicus `baseline` arm (rather than the `0.0517` band-wide risk set, which includes petitions that later attract amici or CVSGs), I consider one modest upward factor and three decisive downward factors:

### Upward consideration (+0.5% to +0.8%)
- **Doctrinal hook for originalist Justices and published D.C. Circuit decision**: Petitioners (represented by experienced D.C. white-collar/sanctions counsel at Miller & Chevalier) frame their single question presented directly around Justice Thomas's dissent from denial of certiorari in *Baston v. United States*, 580 U.S. 1182, 1186 (2017), and Justice Gorsuch's concurrence in *Haaland v. Brackeen*, 599 U.S. 255, 320–22 (2023), challenging the D.C. Circuit's application (*United States v. Park*, 938 F.3d 354 (D.C. Cir. 2019)) of *Lopez*'s "substantial effects" test to the Foreign Commerce Clause ("Commerce *with* foreign Nations").

### Downward adjustments (bringing `P(grant)` down to `0.015`)
1. **No genuine circuit conflict on commercial commodity transactions**: As the Solicitor General's Brief in Opposition demonstrates (BIO 9–11), none of the circuit decisions cited in the petition held that large-scale international commercial trade affecting U.S. commodity prices falls outside Congress's Foreign Commerce Clause power:
   - *United States v. Rife*, 33 F.4th 838, 840, 844–45 (6th Cir. 2022), *affirmed* a § 2423(c) conviction under the Treaty Power while emphasizing that the defendant's overseas child sexual abuse "was undisputedly noncommercial" and "not itself trade or commerce of any kind." (Similarly, the Third Circuit's subsequent discussion in *United States v. Corrigan Clay*, 128 F.4th 163 (3d Cir. 2025), involved non-commercial § 2423(c) conduct.)
   - *United States v. Davila-Mendoza*, 972 F.3d 1264, 1271, 1274–76 (11th Cir. 2020), *assumed without deciding* that *Lopez*'s substantial-effects test applies to the Foreign Commerce Clause and held only that the government failed to prove a substantial effect on U.S. commerce for local foreign drug smuggling not bound for the United States. Here, by contrast, Claimant Aspan *admitted* all allegations in the Amended Complaint, including that NIOC's multi-million-dollar sanctioned oil shipments for the IRGC play a significant role in a black market whose fluctuations predictably influence domestic U.S. oil prices (Pet. App. 11a).
   - *United States v. Weingarten*, 632 F.3d 60, 70–71 (2d Cir. 2011), construed "travel in foreign commerce" in 18 U.S.C. § 2423(b) on statutory grounds and expressly declined to reach the Foreign Commerce Clause.
2. **Severe procedural and jurisdictional vehicle defects**:
   - ***Microsoft Corp. v. Baker* appellate-jurisdiction trap**: After Judge Moss denied Aspan's motion to dismiss the Amended Complaint, Aspan sought to manufacture an immediate appeal by admitting every factual allegation in the Amended Complaint, not opposing judgment on the pleadings, and entering a stipulation in which the government consented to Aspan *amending its Answer on remand to withdraw those admissions* should the D.C. Circuit reverse in whole or in part (Pet. App. 20a–22a). Judge Moss himself ordered supplemental briefing questioning whether that conditional arrangement squared with the final-judgment rule (Pet. App. 21a). Although the D.C. Circuit accepted jurisdiction under *LeFande v. District of Columbia*, 841 F.3d 485 (D.C. Cir. 2016), the Solicitor General squarely flags *Microsoft Corp. v. Baker*, 582 U.S. 23, 26–27, 41 (2017), and *Riley v. Bondi*, 606 U.S. 259, 273 (2025), as an antecedent jurisdictional obstacle that could prevent the Court from ever reaching the merits (BIO 11–12).
   - **Statutory framing and alternative constitutional/statutory grounds**: Aspan did not directly contest the constitutionality of 18 U.S.C. § 2339B(d)(1)(E), instead litigating pleading sufficiency after assuming § 2339B(d)(1)(E) tracks the Foreign Commerce Clause (BIO 12). Moreover, the SG notes unaddressed alternative Article I powers supporting high-seas forfeiture of terrorist assets—the Necessary and Proper Clause, the Piracies and Felonies on the High Seas / Offences against the Law of Nations Clause (Art. I, § 8, cl. 10), and foreign-sovereignty sanctions powers (BIO 12–13)—and the district court also relied on 18 U.S.C. § 2339B(d)(1)(F) and § 981(a)(1)(G)(i)'s alternative "source of influence" prong, which the D.C. Circuit found unnecessary to reach (Pet. 11; Pet. App. 16a).
3. **Panel composition, zero amicus interest, and unsympathetic facts**: Below, Judge Katsas—a prominent originalist and former clerk to Justice Thomas—joined Judge Garcia's opinion in full without writing a concurrence suggesting cert-worthiness. No amicus brief was filed by any constitutional-litigation group, trade association, or defense bar organization, and the petitioner appears to have waived filing a cert reply brief before distribution despite the SG's sharp vehicle objections (and footnote pointing out Pet. 4, 6, 18's misreading of "Irrespective of those arguments' force," BIO 9 n.*). Factually, an in rem forfeiture of $50M in dark-fleet Iranian crude oil funding the IRGC—claimed by a Turkish firm that allegedly submitted false Omani origin documents and then admitted U.S. oil-price effects—is an exceptionally unattractive vehicle for cabining federal extraterritorial power.
