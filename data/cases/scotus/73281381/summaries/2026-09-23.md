## What happened

Chicago's municipal code lets the city immobilize, impound, and eventually sell vehicles belonging to owners who accumulate unpaid parking and traffic fines. Ryan O'Donnell and Michael Goree had vehicles impounded and disposed of this way; O'Donnell's car was sold for scrap value, and Goree's was released to a lienholder. They sued, arguing the ordinance's entire enforcement system is unconstitutional on its face. A federal district court dismissed the suit, and the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, reasoning that the ordinance is a punitive exercise of the city's police power and therefore does not trigger the constitutional protection at issue. O'Donnell and Goree, on behalf of a proposed class, then asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a city can take and sell someone's vehicles to enforce unpaid traffic fines without running afoul of the rule against government taking private property without paying for it, and whether there is any limit on a local government's power to confiscate property this way when it is acting under its general authority to enforce laws rather than through the power of eminent domain. The petitioners argue the city must return any sale proceeds beyond what is owed and cannot seize vehicles unconnected to the violation. The city and its towing contractor respond that the ordinance is a punitive law-enforcement measure squarely allowed by existing precedent, that no lower courts disagree on the issue, and that petitioners' challenge has procedural problems that make this a poor case for deciding the broader question.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for the Court's conference on May 14, 2026, and the Court then asked the respondents to file a response, which followed after an extension. The petitioners filed a reply, and the case has since been distributed again, for the conference of September 28, 2026; the Court has not yet said whether it will hear the case.
