## In brief

Chicago drivers ask the Court to revive their claim that the city's practice of towing, impounding, and selling vehicles over unpaid parking tickets amounts to an unconstitutional taking.

## What happened

Ryan O'Donnell and Michael Goree had vehicles impounded and disposed of under a Chicago ordinance that lets the city immobilize, tow, and ultimately sell vehicles belonging to owners with multiple unpaid parking judgments. The city sold O'Donnell's car for scrap and gave Goree's to a lienholder, without compensating either man or crediting their debts. They sued, claiming the ordinance is unconstitutional on its face. A federal district court dismissed the case, and the Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, holding that the ordinance is an exercise of the city's power to enforce its laws, not a taking of property requiring compensation.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a city's law-enforcement power to punish unpaid traffic debts, by seizing and selling vehicles, has any limit under the constitutional rule that private property cannot be taken for public use without compensation. More specifically, petitioners argue the city must apply sale proceeds to the debt and return any leftover value; the city argues its enforcement power allows it to keep everything.

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for several conferences, most recently one on October 16, 2026, after full briefing including a brief in opposition and a reply. The docket does not yet show any order granting or denying the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

O'Donnell and Goree, as petitioners, are asking the Court to take up their case. If the Court grants the petition, it would hear full arguments and decide whether the ordinance violates the takings rule, deciding nothing about who is right simply by agreeing to hear it. If the Court denies the petition, the Seventh Circuit's ruling stands, leaving in place the dismissal of the claims and the city's practice as currently structured, without any Supreme Court ruling on the underlying constitutional question.
