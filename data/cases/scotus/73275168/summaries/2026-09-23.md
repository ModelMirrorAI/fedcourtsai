## What happened

Raymond Poore pleaded guilty to possessing a firearm as a felon. Because he had a prior state conviction for battery as a party to the crime, the district court used a sentencing guideline provision that counts prior "crimes of violence" toward a higher sentence, relying on an official commentary note interpreting that provision. Poore argued the commentary should not control because the guideline's own text does not cover his type of offense, and that recent Supreme Court rulings limiting deference to agencies' interpretations of their own rules should limit deference to this sentencing commission commentary too. The trial court disagreed and gave him 42 months. The Seventh Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed, saying it was bound by its own prior rulings and that the newer Supreme Court decisions did not clearly undo the older case allowing deference to the commentary. The government's brief also argues the case may be moot because Poore has since finished his prison term.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether the limits the Court has placed on deferring to agencies' interpretations of their own regulations also apply when a court is deciding how much weight to give the Sentencing Commission's explanatory notes about the sentencing guidelines. In practical terms, the question is whether judges must independently decide if a sentencing guideline is unclear before relying on the Commission's commentary to increase someone's sentence, or whether courts may continue treating that commentary as controlling more automatically.
