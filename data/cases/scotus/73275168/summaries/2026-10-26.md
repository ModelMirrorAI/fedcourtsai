## In brief

A man sentenced under a longer prison term asks the Court to decide whether judges may defer to Sentencing Commission commentary when interpreting sentencing rules.

## What happened

Raymond Poore pleaded guilty to illegally possessing a firearm. At sentencing, the district court classified his earlier state conviction for battery, committed as a party to the crime, as a "crime of violence," which raised his guideline range. That classification rested on commentary accompanying the relevant sentencing guideline, not the guideline's own text. Poore argued this commentary deserved no deference after two recent Supreme Court decisions limiting when courts defer to agencies' interpretations of their own rules. The Seventh Circuit disagreed, holding itself bound by earlier circuit precedent, and affirmed his 42-month sentence. Poore asked the Supreme Court to review that ruling.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether recent rulings limiting how much courts must defer to an agency's own interpretation of its rules also limit how much deference courts give to the Sentencing Commission's explanatory notes when those notes interpret the sentencing guidelines, including here where the notes classified Poore's offense as a "crime of violence."

## Where it stands

The petition has been distributed for multiple conferences since January 2026, most recently April 17, 2026, and the docket shows no decision yet on whether the Court will hear the case. The government opposes review, arguing in part that the case is moot because Poore has completed his prison term.

## What each outcome would mean

Poore, the petitioner, asks the Court to take up his case and reverse the Seventh Circuit's ruling, which the government, as respondent, defends. If the Court grants review, it would hear full arguments on the deference question and decide it; a grant would not itself decide who wins. If the Court denies review, the Seventh Circuit's decision upholding Poore's longer sentence stands, though this would not mean the Supreme Court agrees with that ruling.
