## Why this petition is below the anchor

The strongest consideration favoring review is a recurring procurement issue: how government-provided soil data and construction drawings allocate the risk of unexpected site conditions. The Federal Circuit opinion is published, and its subject can affect other contractors. The Federal Circuit's specialized jurisdiction also means that a lack of conventional regional-circuit conflict is not by itself dispositive.

Nevertheless, the petition principally disputes application of an accepted standard. Its three questions frame the decision as a categorical requirement for a boring at the exact failure location. The opinion instead expressly recognizes implicit indications and quotes the rule that reliance on distant borings turns on geology and topography, not a bright-line rule (decision below, pp. 6–9). It relies on different west-bank soil conditions, a slurry-filled void, steeper slopes, pre-bid photographs, and the contractor's own expert evidence (pp. 8–11). It finishes by applying the reasonable-contractor standard to the contract as a whole (p. 13). This is substantial tension between the proposed broad question and the actual holding.

I checked the historical authority independently through CourtListener: *H.B. Mac, Inc. v. United States*, 153 F.3d 1338, 1346–47 (Fed. Cir. 1998), rejects a bright-line distance rule and evaluates the particular geology. That supports reading the decision below as an application of established law, rather than an express new categorical doctrine. The petition identifies no developed conflict with a Supreme Court holding or a competing appellate rule; its principal cited authorities are Federal Circuit decisions.

Vehicle problems are especially important. The traffic-drawing argument was forfeited before the Board, and the drawings themselves were absent from the appellate appendix (decision below, pp. 11–12). A separate argument based on omission of borings was also forfeited (p. 8 n.6). The original prohibition on barges was removed before bidding and expressly disclaimed as the basis of the claim; the operative amendment warned that barges might be necessary (pp. 12–13). These limitations obstruct the petition's proposed integrated-documents theory independently of the broad risk-allocation concern. The Board also treated the defective-specification theory as abandoned (appendix, A-72–A-74), so I do not assume that theory provides an alternate clean route to review.

The waiver, lack of amici, absence of a request for a response, and absence of a relist provide no countervailing attention signal. A waiver is not an adjudication or an irrevocable obstacle, but a request for a response would ordinarily be an intermediate step before a grant. Together these factors justify reducing the reached-band 5.17% anchor substantially, below even the 2.68% no-amicus reference, to 0.6%.
