## In brief

A construction contractor asks the Court to revive its claim for extra payment after a canal project ran into unexpected unstable ground.

## What happened

Hamp's Construction contracted with the Army Corps of Engineers to improve Trapp Canal in Louisiana. While working on one section, the company found collapsed, unstable banks it says the contract documents never warned about, forcing costlier construction methods and delays. Hamp's Construction sought additional payment and time, arguing the government's own contract papers represented the site as more stable than it was. An armed services appeals board denied the claim, finding the contract made no representation about conditions in the specific area where the failures occurred. The Federal Circuit affirmed that decision.

## What the Court is being asked

The Court is asked whether a government contract can be read as making no representation at all about ground conditions in a work area, even when it includes nearby soil data, site drawings, and other project information. It is also asked whether a court may treat the absence of soil-testing data at the exact spot where problems arose as decisive, rather than weighing the contract's documents as a whole, and whether the government can avoid responsibility for conditions its own materials seemed to describe once problems turn up during construction.

## Where it stands

The petition was filed on September 28, 2026, and docketed on October 7, 2026. The government's response is due November 6, 2026; the Court has not yet acted on the petition.

## What each outcome would mean

Hamp's Construction, which lost in the lower courts, is asking the Court to take up the case. If the Court grants the petition, it will hear full arguments and decide the legal question, which would not by itself determine who wins. If the Court denies the petition, the Federal Circuit's ruling against Hamp's Construction stands, leaving the company without the additional payment and time it sought, and the Army's position prevails as a final matter.
