# Rationale

## Call and information set

I assign **44% to any grant**, including a grant-vacate-remand (GVR), and predict **denial**. An illustrative allocation is 55% denial, 27.3% plenary review, 16.7% disposition in the cert order, and 1% another nongrant termination. This is a close cert-selection judgment, not a forecast that Maryland would necessarily prevail on the merits.

The baseline is `record/snapshots/2026-11-09.json`, the relist event definition, and `record/context.json`: forward mode, date cutoff November 10, 2026, five distributions, no CVSG, private petitioners, band `high`, version `sal-v8`, and docket-number Term 2025. I use that supplied Term rather than treating the November 2026 conference as a different docket cohort.

The five distributions need interpretation. September 2, October 5, and October 13 distributions were followed by express reschedules before their respective conferences. The November 2 distribution was for November 6, and November 9 redistributed the petition for November 13. Thus this is the first visible post-conference relist, not evidence of four completed conferences followed by four substantive relists. The numerical count remains five for the increment claim and the harness's conditioning state.

I read the questions presented, the petition's principal cert arguments and vehicle discussion, the opposition's framing, procedural account and reasons against review, and the reply's response to those objections. Sources below refer to the provisioned `record/documents/` files and their printed page numbers. The QP asks whether Maryland's numerous public-place carry prohibitions violate the Second and Fourteenth Amendments. The forecast addresses that question's prospects for review, not the separately challenged private-property consent rule.

## Anchor and adjustment

The frozen `cert_anchor.rate` is **0.3455056179775281**. The committed `metrics/statpack.json` independently reproduces it: the `high` band's reached population in OT2017–OT2024 contains 246 grant-family outcomes among 712 weighted resolved petitions. I exclude OT2025 and OT2026 from that anchor. This is the proper starting population, not the whole-docket grant rate. The salience version matches the pack.

The paid-segment terminal-count cuts are cross-checks, not independent predictors. Pooling every relist bucket from 1 upward gives 726 grant-family outcomes among 3,510 resolved petitions, **20.7%**. The `3+` bucket is 180/483, **37.3%**, but this case's numerical placement there partly reflects reschedules. Neither bucket measures the forward hazard of another distribution. The CVSG population has a 34.8% grant-family rate, n=164, versus 6.4%, n=13,550, without a CVSG; absence of a CVSG is not a fresh negative signal in an ordinary constitutional case against state officials. The snapshot shows one joint amicus brief, filed June 29. The one-amicus bucket's grant-family rate is about 14.9%, n=1,159. That marginal, terminal count is a limited stakes/context check, not an extra likelihood ratio to multiply into the band. The broad Fourth Circuit cut is likewise not a substitute for the selected high-band population.

I move moderately above 34.6%, to 44%, for a final judgment, concrete disagreements over particular locations, a recent intervening Second Amendment decision, and genuine post-conference interest. I do not move to an overwhelming grant forecast merely because the docket contains five distribution entries.

## The competing cert arguments

**For review:** The petition, pp. 17–21, identifies potentially consequential differences concerning medical facilities, demonstrations, public transit, and government ownership. Its strongest point is not that the entire sensitive-place doctrine is unsettled, but that different courts can produce different results for recognizably similar restrictions. The petition, pp. 33–34, also presents a final merits judgment rather than a preliminary-injunction record. The January 20, 2026 lower-court opinion matches the petition's court, consolidated appeal numbers, date, and panel: Gregory and Diaz in the majority, Agee concurring in part and dissenting in part. I used its introductory disposition and discussion of the facial-challenge posture, with the parties' accounts of the full decision. That makes this a more developed vehicle than a bare emergency application.

**Against review:** Maryland's opposition, pp. 14–20 and 26–35, frames the laws as applications of historical analogies rather than defiance of Bruen. Several apparent differences involve different laws, exceptions or procedural postures. The petition's proposed prerequisite of comprehensive government security is considerably broader than the proposition that some of Maryland's bans are unconstitutional; the opposition, pp. 21–26, gives substantial reasons not to accept that categorical theory. Most challenges are facial, and the opinion below expressly invokes that burden. The broad package of places therefore creates both an opportunity to clarify doctrine and a reason to prefer a more focused vehicle. The opposition also points to continuing en banc consideration in Koons. The reply, pp. 2–5, fairly answers that percolation is already extensive, but does not eliminate those distinctions. I have not independently established a later Koons disposition and do not assume one.

**Wolford is intervening precedent, not a future decision to await.** The September 2 reply, pp. 1–7, relies on the Supreme Court's June 25, 2026 Wolford decision and reports the Ninth Circuit's August 10 response. I verified the Supreme Court decision through CourtListener, opinion 11347760, reading its opening syllabus and targeted passages. Its holding invalidates Hawaii's affirmative-consent default for carrying on private property open to the public; it does not directly decide this petition's menu of sensitive places. The opinion's treatment of weak historical analogues gives petitioners a real argument for reconsideration, but not an automatic entitlement to a GVR. I use the reply's description of the Ninth Circuit's response as advocacy, not as an independently retrieved holding.

The opposition, p. 6 n.4, explains that Maryland abandoned its own private-property petition after Wolford. A corpus lookup of the separate cross-petition, No. 25-1206, confirms dismissal on August 13, 2026. That is the government's different petition, not the disposition forecast here. It reinforces that the issue Wolford directly resolves is already outside this petition's live dispute; dismissal of that cross-petition does not extinguish these petitioners' challenge. The snapshot's empty Vide list supplied no related record, so this lookup rests on the petition and opposition identifying the cross-petition, not a claimed Court-entered Vide link.

## Other probabilities and limitations

- **Another distribution: 60%.** There are already five, and the next increase means six or more. Genuine conference attention and the range of available procedural routes make one or two additional distributions slightly more likely than immediate disposition. The three earlier reschedules limit how strongly I read the raw count.
- **A new CVSG: 2%.** None is shown. The question concerns state constitutional restrictions, not a distinctive federal regulatory interest requiring the Solicitor General's advice.
- **Cert-order disposition conditional on grant: 38%.** A GVR in light of Wolford is the main summary path. The petition expressly requests that alternative on pp. 33–34; the corpus's Antonyuk No. 23-910 GVR illustrates that reconsideration without plenary review is a real route in this field. But Wolford's narrow subject and the request for a comprehensive new framework make plenary review more likely within the grant branch. The retrieved generic GVR sample supplies examples, not a frequency estimate.
- **A dissent or statement conditional on denial: 32%.** The subject and real relist create a meaningful possibility of a recorded objection, but a silent denial remains the modal denial branch. The unconditional order-writer marginals are smaller than that conditional existence forecast: Thomas is highest, followed by Alito and Gorsuch. They are writing probabilities, not forecasts of disclosed votes or of joining another Justice's opinion. There is no parsed authorship baseline claimed for these numbers.

The stakes score, **0.86**, measures the potential nationwide significance of defining ordinary public destinations as firearm-free, not the probability of review.

`documents.json` marks the petition and decision below as truncated, but not empty or OCR-derived; the opposition and reply are not truncated. I did not infer silence from omitted appendix pages. The lower-court docket helper searched only 14 entries and found no rehearing petition or disposition; that is incomplete coverage, not proof that nobody sought rehearing. Web search/open attempts yielded no usable content; CourtListener retrieval worked. No current docket or disposition of this petition was sought, and no own-case outcome was encountered or known. The external Wolford decision and the other petition's dismissal both predate the baseline and are legitimate forward context. No blocker remains.
