## In brief

The Court declined to hear a challenge to Maryland's laws banning firearms in places like parks, transit, and healthcare facilities.

## What happened

Gun owners and gun-rights groups sued Maryland over laws barring firearms from numerous public locations, including mass transit, parks, healthcare facilities, entertainment venues, alcohol-serving establishments, and public demonstrations. A federal district court upheld most restrictions but struck down a rule requiring permission to carry on private property open to the public. A divided Fourth Circuit panel largely upheld Maryland's restrictions, with one judge dissenting as to several of them, while unanimously agreeing the private-property rule was unconstitutional. The gun owners then asked the Supreme Court to review the restrictions that were upheld.

## What the Court is being asked

The case asks whether Maryland's many bans on carrying firearms in places open to the public are consistent with the constitutional right to bear arms, and specifically whether such bans are valid only in places where the government itself provides security against armed threats.

## Where it stands

The petition was distributed for several conferences over roughly two months. On November 16, 2026, the Court denied the petition, with Justice Thomas issuing a statement respecting the denial.

## What each outcome would mean

Novotny and the other gun owners, as petitioners, asked the Court to reverse the Fourth Circuit's decision upholding most of Maryland's restrictions. The denial means the Court will not review the case, leaving the Fourth Circuit's ruling in place: Maryland's restrictions on carrying firearms in the challenged locations remain enforceable, while the earlier ruling striking down the private-property consent rule also stands, since Maryland did not pursue further review of that portion. The denial does not mean the Court agreed with the Fourth Circuit's reasoning.
